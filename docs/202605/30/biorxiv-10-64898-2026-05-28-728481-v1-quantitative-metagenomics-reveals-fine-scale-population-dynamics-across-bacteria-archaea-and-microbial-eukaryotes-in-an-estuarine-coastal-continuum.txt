ABSTRACT 24 

Microbial communities are foundational to marine ecosystem function, yet their diversity is often 25 

obscured by broad taxonomic groupings and relative-abundance surveys that mask the dynamics 26 

of individual populations. This limitation is especially important across estuarine–coastal 27 

gradients, where microbial standing stocks, environmental conditions, and community 28 

composition vary sharply over space and time. Here, we used quantitative, genome-resolved 29 

metagenomics to examine microbial population dynamics across a one-year estuary-to-ocean 30 

transect spanning the Neuse River Estuary, Pamlico Sound, and adjacent North Atlantic shelf 31 

waters. Internal standard normalization enabled absolute abundance estimates for single-copy 32 

genes and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), allowing individual populations to be 33 

tracked as genome equivalents per liter. Bacterial standing stocks were higher in estuarine 34 

waters, and communities varied primarily with salinity and season. We recovered 415 MAGs, 35 

including 386 bacterial genomes that represented, on average, 52% of bacterial genome 36 

equivalents, along with archaeal and eukaryotic representatives. Many abundant MAGs lacked 37 

close reference genomes, demonstrating that numerically important coastal populations remain 38 

poorly characterized. Genome-resolved abundances revealed pronounced niche partitioning 39 

among closely related taxa, including seasonal and spatial turnover of Synechococcus ,40 

Cyanobium , and Vulcanococcus populations associated with distinct pigment-defined cytometric 41 

groups. Rhodobacteraceae MAGs also showed population-specific correlations with 42 

picoeukaryotic MAGs, including a winter offshore Planktomarina population that reached 18% 43 

of total bacterial genome equivalents during a Micromonas -associated bloom. By providing 44 

absolute population abundances, this study transformed coastal microbiome surveys into 45 

numerical frameworks for resolving microbial population structure, ecological interactions, and 46 

biogeochemical relevance across dynamic environmental gradients. 47

IMPORTANCE 48 

Estuarine and coastal waters contain diverse microbial communities that help regulate food webs 49 

and the cycling of carbon and nutrients, but many of the individual microbial populations 50 

responsible for these processes remain poorly understood. In this study, we examined bacteria, 51 

archaea, and small algae across the Neuse River Estuary, Pamlico Sound, and nearby coastal 52 

ocean waters over one year. By measuring the absolute abundance of individual microbial 53 

genomes, rather than only their relative proportions, we showed that closely related populations 54 

can have very different seasonal and spatial patterns. This was especially clear for cyanobacteria 55 

related to Synechococcus and heterotrophic bacteria in the family Rhodobacteraceae, which 56 

showed distinct population dynamics and associations with small algae. These results 57 

demonstrate how quantitative genome-resolved measurements can reveal hidden microbial 58 

population structure and improve our understanding of how microorganisms shape coastal 59 

ecosystem function. 60

INTRODUCTION 61 

Microorganisms form the foundation of marine ecosystems, driving biogeochemical 62 

cycles and sustaining higher trophic levels through primary production and recycling of 63 

dissolved organic matter (Azam and Malfatti, 2007; Moran, 2015). Despite their ecological 64 

importance, many microbial communities remain “black boxes” of which individual populations 65 

are not well characterized, including their metabolic potential and spatial and temporal dynamics. 66 

Understanding microbial composition and functional potential is especially crucial in productive 67 

coastal regions susceptible to anthropogenic perturbations such as eutrophication and habitat 68 

alterations, and where large human populations reside (Vitousek et al., 1997; Paerl et al., 2010). 69 

Additionally, genomic characterizations of microbial communities usually provide only 70 

relative abundances, making cross-sample comparisons challenging because changes in relative 71 

abundance do not necessarily reflect changes in cell abundance. This limitation is especially 72 

important when comparing estuarine and coastal ecosystems, where microbial cell densities can 73 

vary by orders of magnitude and relative abundance alone can obscure whether individual 74 

populations are increasing, decreasing, or simply changing proportionally. The application of 75 

internal standard normalization techniques enables read counts to be converted into volumetric 76 

estimates of genome or gene concentrations in environmental samples, thereby moving beyond 77 

relative community composition to quantify the absolute standing stocks of individual microbial 78 

populations (Gifford et al., 2011, 2020; Moran et al., 2013; Satinsky et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2019; 79 

Sharpe et al., 2023, Bei et al., 2025). 80

The Albemarle-Pamlico Estuarine System (APES) is the largest lagoonal estuary in the 82 

United States and is characterized by shallow depths, low tidal exchange, and long water 83 

residence times, making it highly sensitive to freshwater inputs and seasonal hydrological 84 

variability (Paerl et al., 2009, 2018). These hydrological dynamics strongly influence nutrient 85 

availability, phytoplankton biomass, and phytoplankton community composition across the 86 

system (Pinckney et al., 1998; Gong et al., 2018, 2020; Sanchez-Gallego et al., 2025). In 87 

contrast, nearby North Atlantic continental shelf waters are generally less productive than APES 88 

waters and support distinct microbial communities (Wang et al., 2019). Long-term coastal 89 

monitoring has further shown that microbial communities in this region undergo pronounced 90 

seasonal shifts, including turnover among closely related taxa (Ward et al., 2017). Together, these 91 

features make the APES-to-coastal ocean continuum a useful natural gradient for examining how 92 

microbial populations vary across strong spatial and seasonal environmental gradients. 93 

The hydrology and phytoplankton ecology of APES and adjacent North Atlantic shelf 94 

waters have been extensively studied through long-term monitoring, pigment analyses, 95 

microscopy, flow cytometry, and amplicon sequencing (Paerl et al., 2009, 2010; Peierls and 96 

Paerl, 2010; Ward et al., 2017; Gong et al., 2018, 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2025). 97 

However, comparatively little is known about the genome-resolved diversity, abundance, and 98 

dynamics of bacterioplankton populations in this system. This gap is especially important for 99 

ecologically significant groups such as Synechococcus , which are key primary producers in 100 

estuarine and coastal waters and exhibit seasonal changes in abundance, composition, and 101 

pigment phenotype, yet remain poorly resolved at the population-genomic level in APES and 102 

nearby coastal waters (Ward et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019; Paerl et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 103 

2025; Sanchez-Gallego et al., 2025). Similarly, the population-level dynamics of abundant 104

To improve our understanding of marine microbial population dynamics and their 107 

influence on coastal ecosystems, we conducted a quantitative metagenomics survey across the 108 

estuary-to-ocean continuum of the APES. We specifically examined: (1) how microbial 109 

communities changed with time and space, (2) the environmental drivers of composition 110 

changes, and (3) the distributions of individual genome-delineated bacterial populations of 111 

ecological significance. Surface metagenome samples were collected from three sampling sites 112 

representing Neuse River Estuary, Pamlico Sound and North Atlantic continental shelf (Onslow 113 

Bay) waters over a one-year period. Internal standard normalization was applied to estimate the 114 

volumetric abundances of single-copy genes and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Our 115 

results indicate that both community composition and the distributions of individual genome-116 

delineated populations show strong spatial and seasonal patterns across the estuarine-to-coastal 117 

gradient. By coupling MAG reconstruction with internal standard normalization, we 118 

quantitatively resolved microbial population structure at genome-level resolution, revealing 119 

patterns that would be obscured by relative-abundance or higher-level taxonomic analyses alone. 120

Volumetric abundance estimation 156 

To estimate gene and genome volumetric abundances, internal standard (IS) recovery 157 

ratios were calculated for both genes and genomes using methods described previously (Gifford 158 

et al., 2020; Sharpe et al., 2023). The relationship between the number of IS genomes added to 159 

the sample and the number of IS reads recovered were used to estimate volumetric abundances 160 

of genes in the sequence library. In this study, we estimated volumetric genome equivalent 161 

abundances using the single copy gene recA . The relationship between the number of IS 162 

genomes added to the sample and the number of IS genomes recovered was used to estimate 163 

volumetric abundances of MAGs based on the number of MAGs sequenced. 164 

To estimate archaeal absolute abundance, we established a station-specific relationship 165 

between SingleM coverage and genome equivalents using bacterial data. For each station, we 166 

performed linear regression between bacterial SingleM coverage values and bacterial recA gene 167 

counts, with the y-intercept constrained to zero. The slope of each regression was used as a 168 

scaling factor to convert SingleM archaeal coverage values into estimates of archaeal genome 169 

equivalents. 170

Taxonomic annotations 172 

Reads were annotated with NCBI RefSeq (v.223; bacterial, eukaryotic, archaeal, and viral 173 

reference sequences) using DIAMOND blastx (Buchfink et al., 2015). A RecA protein database 174 

was assembled containing proteins from the RefSeq protein database annotated with the key 175 

words “recombinase RecA,” “protein RecA,” “recombinase A,” or “RecA protein.” Bacterial 176 

recA reads in the metagenomes were first identified by a DIAMOND homology search against 177 

this custom RecA protein database. Then the recA reads were re-annotated with RecA reference 178 

sequences from GTDB representative genomes (R202; TIGR2012) to match RecA taxonomy 179 

with MAG taxonomy (Parks et al., 2022). For archaeal annotations, the SingleM “pipe” 180 

workflow was used to annotate all samples and the archaeal annotations were extracted to 181 

visualize the archaeal compositions (Woodcroft et al., 2024). For eukaryotic annotations, reads 182 

were annotated with PhyloDB (https://github.com/allenlab/PhyloDB) and NCBI Refseq using 183 

DIAMOND blastx. 184

RESULTS and DISCUSSION 217 

Environmental variability across sampling sites 218 

From August 2021 to August 2022, we collected surface-water samples (~1 m depth) at 219 

three stations spanning the estuary-to-ocean continuum: NRE180 in the lower Neuse River 220 

Estuary (more riverine western, up-estuary), PS5 in Pamlico Sound (higher saline, eastern 221 

estuary), and OB1 in Onslow Bay (Fig. 1A and 1B). NRE180 and PS5 are part of a long-term 222 

monitoring effort (MODMON). These sites had similar temperatures and dissolved-oxygen 223 

concentrations (SI Fig. 1), yet differed markedly in hydrologic regime, as shown by a 224 

pronounced salinity gradient from the estuary to coastal waters (Fig. 1C). 225 

Mean chlorophyll-a concentrations indicated that NRE180 and PS5 were more 226 

productive than OB1, averaging 7.2 and 6.7 μg Chl a L⁻¹, respectively, compared with 0.9 μg 227 

Chl a L⁻¹ offshore (Fig. 1D). NRE180 had elevated POC, PN, DOC, pico- and nanoeukaryote 228 

abundances, and prokaryotic cell densities, whereas PS5 had greater numbers of >10 μm 229 

eukaryotes and Synechococcus-like cells (SI Fig. 1; SI Table 1). OB1 had the lowest eukaryotic 230 

and prokaryotic cell counts, consistent with its more oligotrophic character. 231 

Microbial community compositions 232 

Over the year-long survey we generated 17 quantitative metagenomes, six each from the 233 

Neuse River Estuary (NRE180) and Pamlico Sound (PS5) and five from the offshore station in 234 

Onslow Bay (OB1). 235 

Read-level taxonomic profiling against NCBI RefSeq (v223) showed that the 236 

metagenomes were overwhelmingly bacterial. Across the 17 libraries, on average >90% of 237 

quality-filtered reads mapped to Bacteria; the remainder affiliated with Eukaryota (~5%), viruses 238

Archaea metagenome-derived abundances. 284 

Archaeal reads classified with SingleM and normalized with internal standards showed 285 

that archaeal genome equivalents were one to two orders of magnitude lower than bacterial 286 

abundances but varied seasonally, declining to ~10⁷ L⁻¹ in cooler months and increasing to ~10⁸ 287 

L⁻¹ during summer (Fig. 2B). Warm-season archaeal communities were dominated by Marine 288 

Group II families Poseidoniaceae and Thalassarchaeaceae, consistent with their prevalence in 289 

euphotic coastal waters, whereas cooler months contained more diverse but lower-abundance 290 

archaeal assemblages, including Woesearchaeales at the estuarine and sound stations and 291 

Thermoproteota in the January offshore sample. Thaumarchaeota and other ammonia-oxidizing 292 

lineages were minor components, potentially reflecting the low ammonia concentrations 293 

observed across most samples. Overall, archaeal abundances and composition showed a strong 294 

seasonal signal but represented a small fraction of the total microbial community. 295

Community shifts and environmental drivers 308 

Redundancy analysis (RDA) of the 20 most abundant recA-defined bacterial families 309 

explained 48% of community variation across the first two axes, revealing a primary spatial 310 

separation between offshore OB1 and the estuarine NRE180 and PS5 sites along the salinity-311 

associated RDA1 axis, and a secondary seasonal gradient along RDA2 (Fig. 3). Offshore 312 

communities were associated with higher salinity and greater relative abundances of Halieaceae, 313 

Flavobacteriaceae, Ca. Puniceispirillaceae, UA16, and MB11C04, while estuarine and sound 314 

communities showed stronger seasonal structure, with summer-to-autumn samples associated 315 

with warmer temperatures, phytoplankton-related variables, and increased Ilumatobacteraceae, 316 

Actinomarinaceae, and SM1A02. Winter and spring samples were characterized by higher 317 

relative abundances of Pelagibacteraceae, Cyanobiaceae, and Microbacteriaceae. Overall, the 318 

ordination indicates that salinity structured the major estuarine-to-coastal biogeographic gradient, 319 

while temperature and correlated phytoplankton/cyanobacterial variables contributed to seasonal 320 

community succession within APES, consistent with previous studies showing strong effects of 321 

salinity and temperature on coastal microbial communities (Fortunato et al., 2012; Chow et al., 322 

2013; Ward et al., 2017; Sanchez-Gallego et al., 2025). 323

MAG recovery and quantitative coverage of microbial populations. 325 

To resolve community dynamics at the population level, we reconstructed MAGs from each of 326 

the 17 libraries, generating 415 MAGs: 386 bacterial, 18 archaeal, and 11 eukaryotic. 327

Eukaryotic MAGs 374 

Eukaryotic genome reconstruction is inherently more challenging than prokaryotic 375

Cyanobiaceae Genomic Insights and Pigment‐Based Signatures 400 

The family Cyanobiaceae, which encompasses the globally abundant genera 401 

Synechococcus , Prochlorococcus , and Cyanobium (Parks et al., 2022), was the most prominent 402 

cyanobacterial lineage in our data set (Fig. 2A). We reconstructed 21 Cyanobiaceae MAGs that 403 

collectively span the dominant Synechococcus clades in the region (Fig. 4; SI Table 6). Seven 404 

have no ≥95% ANI match in public databases, and only two of the remaining 14 have ANI 405 

matches exceeding 99%, indicating substantial genomic novelty among the MAGs. Closest 406 

references were mostly from the northwestern Atlantic margin, Chesapeake Bay, Woods Hole, 407 

and the Gulf of Mexico, indicating broad regional representation among related 408 

picocyanobacterial populations (SI Table 6). 409 

The ten most abundant Cyanobiaceae MAGs accounted for ~55% of Cyanobiaceae 410 

genome equivalents in each sample (Fig. 6B) and displayed pronounced, site-specific seasonal 411 

patterns. In estuarine NRE180 and sound PS5 samples, winter and early-spring communities 412 

were dominated by M157 (Synechococcus_C), whereas summer–autumn assemblages shifted 413 

toward M166 ( Vulcanococcus ) and M150 ( Cyanobium _A). 414

measurements alone. Although flow cytometry distinguishes broad PE- and PC-containing 439 

groups, MAG-based abundances resolved seasonal turnover and shifts in dominance among 440

pigment ecotypes (Stomp et al., 2007; Haverkamp et al., 2009), and with prior work connecting 443 

pigment diversity to genomic variation and flow cytometry-defined pigment classes (Six et al., 444 

2007; Scanlan et al., 2009; Li et al., 2024). While direct links between MAG features and in situ 445

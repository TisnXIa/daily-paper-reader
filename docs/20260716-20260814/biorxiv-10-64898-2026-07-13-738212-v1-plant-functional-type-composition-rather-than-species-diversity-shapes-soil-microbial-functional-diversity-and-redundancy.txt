Abstract 31 

Cover crops (CCs) are widely used to improve soil health, but their species-specific 32 

effects on microbial functional diversity and redundancy remain poorly understood. We 33 

evaluated how monocultures of field pea ( Pisum sativum ), forage radish ( Raphanus sativus ), and 34 

cereal rye ( Secale cereale ), as well as a three-species mixture (3spp) and a five-species mixture 35 

(5spp), influence rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities in a field experiment. Amplicon 36 

sequencing and predictive functional profiling were used to assess microbial diversity, 37 

composition, functional potential, and functional redundancy (FR). Microbial alpha diversity 38 

showed little change across treatments, but community composition and predicted functional 39 

profiles were strongly influenced by CC identity. The 3spp rhizobiome showed the highest 40 

bacterial FR, broad metabolic capacity, and greater network connectivity, suggesting increased 41 

functional stability of all treatments. In contrast, the 5spp rhizobiome showed reduced bacterial 42 

FR and more widespread functional depletion, likely due to imbalances in plant functional types. 43 

Monocultures showed species-specific patterns, with rye supporting functionally efficient 44 

communities and radish promoting more competitive interactions and reduced functional 45 

diversity. Fungal communities responded differently from bacterial communities. While fungal 46 

taxonomic shifts were limited, they showed stronger compositional differences among treatments 47 

and more stable functional profiles. Notably, fungal FR was highest in the 5spp rhizobiome. 48 

Overall, these findings highlight that balanced plant functional composition, rather than greater 49 

species richness alone, is important for shaping rhizosphere microbial function and redundancy. 50 

Balanced mixtures promoted higher bacterial redundancy, while more diverse but functionally 51 

imbalanced mixtures did not consistently enhance microbial function. 52

Importance 54 

The benefits of using CCs in agricultural settings are mostly influenced by microbial 55 

communities, including nutrient cycling, organic matter turnover, and resilience to disturbance. 56 

However, CC mixtures are often promoted on the assumption that more species provide greater 57 

soil benefits. This work challenges that assumption by showing that the functional identity and 58 

balance of CC species may matter more than the number of species alone. This distinction is 59 

important for farmers, land managers, and researchers because CC seed mixes can be costly, and 60 

poorly balanced mixtures may fail to deliver intended microbial benefits. Understanding which 61 

plant combinations support functionally stable microbial communities can improve CC 62 

recommendations and help design agricultural systems that maintain soil processes under 63 

changing environmental conditions. 64 

Introduction 65 

Soil microorganisms play a crucial role in maintaining soil health and crop productivity, 66 

as they facilitate nutrient cycling, break down organic matter, promote plant growth, and help the 67 

ecosystem recover from environmental stress (1). Microbial communities carry out a wide range 68 

of biochemical functions that drive carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur cycling, among 69 

others (2). Not only is the presence of a diverse microbial community important, but the degree 70 

of functional redundancy (FR), the number of organisms that can perform the same function, is 71 

also key to maintaining ecosystem stability (3, 4). FR has been proposed as a mechanism to 72 

buffer against microbial community collapse, because if one taxon is lost due to disturbance, 73 

another may still perform the same role, thereby maintaining overall ecosystem stability (5, 6). 74 

In agriculture, the use of cover cropping to enhance microbial diversity and FR is gaining 75 

interest, particularly for long-term soil fertility and resilience to stress. Cover crops (CC) are 76

Similarly, studies examining the decomposition of CC residues showed that mixtures with 88

14), fewer studies have examined how individual CC species affect FR. Most studies on soil 92 

microorganisms under different CCs focus on taxonomic diversity or community composition 93 

(11, 15, 16), with less emphasis on the stability and resilience conferred by overlapping 94 

microbial functions. For example, microbial alpha diversity had only subtle impacts on enzyme 95 

activity in cover-cropped soils, with total community size more closely associated with functions 96

buffer against biodiversity loss in microbial communities (18), few studies have explicitly 98

Cover crop species differ in their physiology, nutrient use strategies, and root exudate 103 

profiles, all of which can influence the microbial communities in the surrounding soil. Legumes 104 

such as Austrian winter pea ( Pisum sativum ) host nitrogen-fixing bacteria and generally support 105 

communities enriched in taxa, including Rhizobium and Bradyrhizobium (21, 22). Grasses (e.g., 106 

oats; Avena sativa L .) and brassicas (forage radish; Raphanus sativus L .) may support different 107 

groups of microbes, including those involved in phosphorus solubilization, sulfur oxidation, or 108 

other nutrient pathways (9, 23, 24). Some of these interactions may be species-specific, because 109 

particular plant species select for microbial communities through root exudates that can act as 110 

carbon sources or chemical signals (25, 26). These plant-microbe interactions are dynamic and 111 

can influence both the taxonomic composition and functional capabilities of the soil microbiome. 112 

In this study, we focused on determining how different CCs affect microbial taxonomic 113 

and predicted functional diversity and redundancy in field soils. Specifically, we hypothesize that 114 

microbial taxonomic diversity, functional diversity, and FR will differ among CC species, with 115 

legumes (e.g., pea) supporting higher redundancy due to their enrichment of beneficial microbes 116 

involved in nitrogen cycling and plant growth. We further hypothesize that CC mixtures will 117 

support greater microbial taxonomic and functional diversity, as well as increased FR, compared 118 

to monocultures, due to the greater diversity of plant inputs and ecological niches provided by 119 

CC mixtures. To test these hypotheses, we used 16S rRNA gene and ITS amplicon sequencing to 120 

assess microbial community composition and diversity and employed functional prediction tools 121 

to estimate functional potential and redundancy from these data. This approach will provide 122

Materials and Methods 130 

Crop Information and DNA Extraction 131 

We established a CC cash crop rotation experiment in Fall 2023 at Simpson’s Farm in 132 

Pendleton, SC (34°37′30.1′′ N, −82°44′13.9′′ W). Cover crops were planted on November 8, 133 

2023, using a no-till drill-cone plot planter. The six CC treatments were planted in the field in a 134 

randomized block design with four blocks and four replicates per treatment. There was a total of 135 

24 plots, each 3 m x 15 m in size, with a 2m buffer zone between the plots and blocks. The soil 136 

at the site is Cecil sandy loam (clayey, kaolinitic, thermic typic Kanhapludults). The CCs were 137 

grown without any external irrigation or fertilizer inputs. Cover crops treatments were field pea 138

Soil Analyses 150 

Standard soil nutrient analysis was performed by the Clemson University Agricultural 151 

Service Lab. Three extracellular enzymes, namely, N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG, EC 152

organic carbon and total nitrogen using a LECO CN analyzer (model: CN828). The low-carbon 161

Differential Abundance and Functional Predictions 217 

To identify differentially abundant taxa, ANCOM-BC2 (Lin & Peddada, 2020) was 218 

applied at multiple taxonomic ranks (Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, and ASV) 219 

considering global and pairwise comparisons with FDR correction. For both 16S rRNA gene and 220 

ITS datasets, taxa were filtered prior to genus- and ASV-level ANCOM-BC2 analyses by 221 

retaining taxa present in at least 20% (16S) or 10% (ITS) of samples and with ≥10 total reads. 222 

Functional predictions were generated from 16S rRNA gene ASV data using the 223 

PICRUSt2 database (52). Functional predictions were generated from ITS1 region sequences 224 

using the FungalTraits database (53). Microeco was used to prepare input microtable objects and 225 

calculate KEGG-based functional profiles (41). The FR Index (FRI) was computed to estimate 226 

redundancy within predicted pathways for bacterial communities (54). FR for fungal 227 

communities was calculated using the “SYNCSA” package in R based on the FungalTrait 228 

database (55–58). Global and pairwise differences in functional profiles and redundancy indices 229 

among treatments were tested using ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests with appropriate post-hoc 230 

corrections (44, 45). To assess the reliability of functional predictions, weighted Nearest 231 

Sequenced Taxon Index (NSTI) scores were calculated for all samples using PICRUSt2 (59). 232 

NSTI represents the average phylogenetic distance between each ASV and its nearest reference 233 

genome, weighted by abundance. Lower NSTI values indicate higher prediction accuracy (59). 234

Discussion 397 

Although CCs are known to influence soil microbial communities through root 398 

exudation, residue inputs, and alterations in soil physicochemical properties (60, 61), less is 399 

understood about how individual CC species and multiple species in mixtures (CC diversity) 400 

shape microbial functional potential and FR across both bacteria and fungi. In this study, CC 401 

identity and species composition had stronger effects on rhizosphere microbial community 402 

composition, predicted functional potential, and functional redundancy than CC species richness 403 

alone, as evidenced by the more consistent and pronounced differences among individual CC 404 

treatments across taxonomic, functional, and network analyses. While there were limited changes 405 

in microbial alpha diversity between treatments, both community composition and predicted 406 

functional profiles were strongly structured by specific CC species or CC combinations, 407 

indicating that plant functional traits are primary drivers of microbial organization. 408 

Our results partially support Hypothesis 1, as microbial taxonomic composition, 409 

functional potential, and FR differed among CC species, particularly between monocultures and 410 

mixtures. However, Hypothesis 2 was not supported based on species richness alone, as CC 411 

mixtures did not consistently increase microbial diversity or FR relative to monocultures. 412 

Instead, results suggest that plant functional composition played a stronger role, with the 413 

functionally balanced 3spp mixture showing greater functional potential and FR than the legume-414 

dominated 5spp mixture. Similarly, Hypothesis 3 was not supported when considering diversity 415 

alone, as mixtures did not consistently buffer microbial communities from functional or 416 

compositional shifts. Contrary to expectations from the diversity–stability hypothesis (62, 63), 417

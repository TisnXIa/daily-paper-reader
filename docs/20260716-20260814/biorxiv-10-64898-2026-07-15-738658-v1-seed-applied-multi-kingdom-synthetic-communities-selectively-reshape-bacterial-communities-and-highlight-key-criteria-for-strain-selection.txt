Pioneer studies on SynCom inoculation suggest that 69 both the number of organisms (i.e. propagule size [13]) and the timing of arrival (i.e. priority effects 70 [14]) are key drivers of the successful establishment within communities [15]. Thus, a better 71 understanding and consideration of the ecological processes at play during SynCom inoculation is key 72 to improve inoculation success [16]. 73 One way to harness these ecological processes is also to target specific “critical windows” during 74 plant development that could be more favorable for inoculant establishment. For instance, at the 75 seed stage native plant microbiota is sparse, favoring inoculant establishment through priority effect 76 and propagule pressure [14]. Improvement in seed technologies also allows the coating of seed for a 77 more  efficient  delivery  of  microorganisms  [17].  The  inoculation  and  monitoring  of  bacterial 78 communities in seed to seedling stages has shown promising results with high colonization [15, 18]. 79 However, like most studies using SynComs inoculation experiments, the focus has primarily been on 80 bacterial communities, while only a few studies consider both bacterial and fungal inoculation (4 out 81 of 86 surveyed in Xu et al., 2025 [19]). Yet, interkingdom interactions between bacteria and fungi 82 naturally occur within plant microbiota and modulate plant phenotype [20]. More recently, an 83 increasing interest has grown in multi-kingdom inoculum as they enhance disease suppressiveness 84 [21, 22] .Still, while we know that complex interactions occur between these kingdoms [23], limited 85 knowledge on how to design and inoculate efficiently these multi-kingdom inoculants is available. An 86 upgraded guideline for the use of multi-kingdom inoculant is then required to fully harness the 87 beneficial effects mediated by these multi-kingdom interactions. 88 In this study, we aim to gain a deeper understanding of multi-kingdom SynCom inoculation to 89 characterize their colonization success of seeds and seedling, and their impact on the native 90 microbiome. To do so, 20 different SynComs were designed based on distinct criteria and inoculated 91 onto Brassica napus L. seeds and grown for 15 days in non-sterile soil. That is why we hypothesized that SynCom 95 designs based on specific assembly criteria would achieve higher colonization than those assembled 96 randomly (H2). The objectives were to increase microbial biomass on seeds, while maintaining a balanced 198 proportion between bacteria, yeasts and filamentous fungi. In addition, we aimed to avoid negative 199 effects on seed germination and to ensure reliable recovery of inoculated microorganisms for 200 subsequent assessment of seed colonization. Each 202 condition was applied to 120 seeds. 203 Each method was used to inoculate a bacterial strain (Pantoea agglomerans CFBP 9288) a yeast 204 (Filobasium sp. CLIB 4440), the fungal strain Cladosporium sp. pCoAvM041, and a control condition 205 using  sterile water instead of microbial suspension resulting in a total of  four independent 206 inoculations. Seeds were inoculated at a 10 6microorganisms.mL -1 concentration and dried for 10 min 207 under a laminar flow hood. To assess seed colonization, eight seeds per each condition and per 208 method were individually macerated in sterile water, or in 1% sodium citrate for alginate-coated 209 seeds. Germination was evaluated by placing 100 seeds per condition on pleated paper, followed by 210 incubation for 2 days in the dark at 20°C and 12 days under a 16h light and 8h dark photoperiod. 211 - SynCom inoculation on Brassica napus seed 212 The 20 SynComs were inoculated on Brassica napus seeds. Each strain was first prepared individually 213 at a target concentration of 10 6microorganisms.mL -1 , then mixed in equal proportions to assemble 214 the SynComs.

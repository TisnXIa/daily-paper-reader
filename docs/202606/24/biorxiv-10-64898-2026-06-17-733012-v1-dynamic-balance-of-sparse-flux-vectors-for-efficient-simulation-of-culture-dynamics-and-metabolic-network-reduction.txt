Abstract 15 

Dynamic Flux Balance Analysis (DFBA) enables simulation of microbial culture dynamics 16 

under changing environmental conditions, but remains computationally expensive for tasks 17 

such as parameter calibration and fermentation optimization when applied using genome-18 

scale metabolic models (GEMs). To address this challenge, we introduce Dynamic Flux Vec-19 

tor Balancing (DFVB), a reformulation of DFBA that solves an equivalent problem using a 20 

pre-computed, sparse basis of flux solutions that reduces the dimensionality of the internal 21 

optimization problem without information loss. Notably, DFVB provides a compact, inter-22 

pretable representation of flux states that can readily identify dynamically inactive pathways 23

# Introduction 38 

Genome-scale metabolic models enable the quantitative simulation of cellular physiology by integrating thousands 39 

of biochemical reactions and metabolites within a comprehensive optimization framework (Orth, Thiele, et al. 2010). 40 

These models have become indispensable tools for guiding metabolic engineering, strain design, and optimization of 41 

biotechnological processes ranging from commodity production to pharmaceuticals and enzyme manufacturing, sup-42 

ported by constraint-based modeling methods such as Flux Balance Analysis (FBA) (Gotsmy et al. 2024; Gu et al. 43 

2019; Sánchez et al. 2014). The dynamic extension of this approach, Dynamic Flux Balance Analysis (DFBA), en-44 

ables simulation of the temporal evolution of the cell culture under changing environmental conditions (Mahadevan, 45 

Edwards, et al. 2002). However, DFBA requires solving a large number of coupled ordinary differential equations 46 

and linear programming problems, resulting in a substantial computational burden that limits its scalability and 47 

practical integration into complex cell culture models. 48 

Recent studies have focused on overcoming computational limitations by enhancing solver performance, intro-49 

ducing nonlinear reformulations through Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions (de Oliveira et al. 2023), interior-50 

point optimization methods (Scott et al. 2018), Runge–Kutta integration schemes (Schroeder and Saha 2020), and 51 

hybrid approaches incorporating metabolomic constraints (Dromms et al. 2020). Despite these advances, the com-52 

putational efficiency of DFBA simulations remains limited to specific applications, as most approaches focus on 53 

improving numerical optimization rather than addressing the structural complexity of the underlying metabolic 54 

model (Gotsmy et al. 2024; Höffner et al. 2013). 55 

To tackle the above difficulties, various strategies have been proposed for reducing the complexity of metabolic 56

Static analysis methods like Flux Variability Analysis (FVA) are also often utilized to identify and prune blocked 62 

reactions (Mahadevan and Schilling 2003), while minimal mass-balance representations offer an alternative path for 63 

network reduction (Singh and Lercher 2020). Specifically, formulations like Alpha Spectrum (Wiback et al. 2003), 64

aim to represent the feasible flux space through extreme or sparse bases, substantially lowering the dimensionality of 66 

subsequent LP-based flux analyses while theoretically preserving biochemical accuracy. However, a common draw-67 

back of various approaches is that they frequently rely on a priori definitions of subsystems or make assumptions 68 

about the metabolic state, which may fail to reliably capture the transient (de)activation of pathways inherent to 69 

dynamic fermentation processes. Moreover, the robust and efficient application of these model reformulation and 70 

reduction techniques to dynamic DFBA simulations, particularly those based on minimal mass-balance descriptions, 71 

has received limited attention and remains to be evaluated. 72 

This study addresses these limitations by introducing Dynamic Flux Vector Balancing (DFVB), a computational 73 

framework designed to structurally reduce model complexity and improve the efficiency of dynamic simulations in 74 

genome-scale metabolic models. By computing a sparse null-space basis of the flux solution space, the proposed 75 

method isolates the dynamically active metabolic subspace under a given environmental context. This reformulation 76 

not only preserves the prediction fidelity of conventional DFBA, but also enables a systematic, simulation-based 77 

reduction of the network, identifying and removing reactions that remain inactive throughout the simulated process. 78 

Furthermore, this approach enhances the identification of kinetic parameters from experimental fermentation data, 79 

leading to lower prediction uncertainty. Finally, driven by model reduction, this method is scalable to high-80 

dimensional GEMs. The resulting framework substantially reduces computational complexity, enabling the robust 81 

integration of dynamic metabolic modeling into standard bioprocess simulation environments. 82

## The Dynamic Flux Vector Balancing (DFVB) Framework 84 

Dynamic Flux Vector Balancing (DFVB) provides a rigorous mathematical reformulation of standard Dynamic Flux 85 

Balance Analysis (DFBA) designed to accelerate simulations of culture dynamics without losing precision (Fig. 1). 86 

Instead of computing the full vector of reaction fluxes ( v) at each integration step, DFVB maps the feasible flux 87 

space onto the right null-space of the stoichiometric matrix ( N). This basis is computed using a Rank-Revealing LU 88 

(RRLU) decomposition to preserve matrix sparsity (refer to Methods), thereby easing its biological interpretation 89

By parameterizing the flux distribution as a linear combination of null-90 

space basis vectors, weighted by a vector of coefficients ( α), this algebraic transformation implicitly satisfies the 91 

intracellular quasi-steady-state mass balances (Eq. 1). Effectively, this reduces the problem dimensionality from 92 

the total number of reactions ( m) in standard FBA to (m − k) + z variables, where k represents the rank of the 93 

stoichiometric matrix, z denotes the number of exchange reactions, and typically k ≫ z. Consequently, the total 94 

number of constraints decreases from n + 2 m to z + 2 m, where n is the total number of metabolites. Crucially, this 95 

reformulation is not an approximation, but an exact transformation that yields an identical optimal solution to the 96 

original FBA problem while significantly reducing the complexity of the constraint set. 97 

The parameterization of fluxes through latent variables provides in addition a direct mechanism for systematic, 98 

simulation-based model reduction (see Alg.1). Because each coefficient αj (t) reflects the activation of its corre-99 

sponding flux vector basis, DFVB simulations can identify dynamically inactive pathways. Basis vectors for which 100 

αj (t) = 0 across the entire simulation window ( t ∈ [0 , T ]) are classified as inactive. The sequential elimination 101 

of these inactive basis vectors from N, followed by the removal of the resulting zero-flux reactions and orphan 102 

metabolites from S, produces a highly compact, context-specific stoichiometric network. This algorithm retains 103 

all dynamically accessible biological pathways while eliminating irrelevant degrees of freedom. This significantly 104 

reduces the computational demand required for large-scale dynamic modeling, transcriptomic analysis, and kinetic 105 

parameter estimation. 106

In order to evaluate the accuracy of DFVB relative to DFBA, simulations were performed using four metabolic 108 

models under multiple fermentation conditions. The first evaluation employed the simple illustrative model proposed 109 

by Zhao et al. (2017), shown in Fig. 2A with its corresponding null-space basis. As presented in Fig. 2B, DFVB and 110 

DFBA produced identical dynamic profiles for all simulated variables, exhibiting numerical agreement up to machine 111 

precision. The associated α-dynamics revealed that only a single basis vector remained active throughout the 112 

simulation, corresponding to the flux mode responsible for biomass formation. The second basis vector, associated 113 

with product formation, remained inactive. Despite eliminating four reactions and three metabolites after removal 114 

of inactive columns from the null-space matrix, the reduced model reproduced the exact full-system trajectory. 115 

This suggests that DFVB preserves the essential dynamic behavior of systems when the optimal flux distribution 116 

is unique, enabling exact simulation without loss of information due to model reduction. 117 

A similar analysis was performed using the E. coli core metabolic model under aerobic, anaerobic, and oxygen-118 

limited regimes (Fig. 2C). In all three scenarios, DFVB and DFBA displayed perfectly overlapping trajectories for 119 

biomass, glucose, oxygen, and acetate. Under oxygen-limited conditions, both frameworks identically predicted 120 

acetate accumulation during exponential growth, followed by a metabolic shift toward acetate consumption once 121 

glucose was depleted. This diauxic behavior is characteristic of E. coli under glucose overflow (Pinhal et al. 2019). 122 

Table S1 in the Supplementary File summarizes the accuracy of DFVB relative to DFBA across the illustrative 123 

model, the E. coli core model, iML1515, and Yeast9. For all models examined, DFVB reproduced the DFBA 124 

trajectories with RMSE values close to numerical precision. The variables directly constrained by the objective 125 

function, namely biomass and glucose, exhibited the smallest discrepancies across all models and conditions. In 126 

the aerobic simulations of the genome-scale models ( iML1515 and Yeast9), slightly larger RMSE values were 127 

observed in the production profiles of by-products (see Supplementary File Text S7), though these deviations did 128 

not substantially affect the overall simulated optimal trajectories. 129 

## Computational Performance Evaluation 130 

The computational efficiency of DFVB was evaluated across the E. coli core model, iML1515, and Yeast9. Following 131 

a single DFVB simulation, each metabolic model was reduced according to the subset of active reactions and 132 

metabolites (Algorithm 1). The extent of this structural reduction is summarized in Table 1. While the reduction 133 

was modest for the E. coli core model, the genome-scale models showed substantial compression: approximately 134 

14% and 28% of the basis vectors remained active for iML1515 and Yeast9, respectively. Consequently, nearly 74% 135 

of reactions and 68% of metabolites were eliminated in iML1515, and 68% of reactions and 64% of metabolites 136 

were removed in Yeast9. 137

# Discussion 182 

This study introduced and evaluated DFVB, a computational framework for dynamic metabolic flux simulations 183 

designed to overcome the structural limitations of conventional approaches like DFBA. By reformulating the high-184 

dimensional differential-algebraic system into a reduced-order model based on a sparse null-space basis, we demon-185 

strated that DFVB retains the full simulation capability of standard DFBA while offering critical advantages in 186 

model reduction and parameter identifiability. 187 

Our accuracy benchmarks confirmed that DFVB reproduces DFBA trajectories to machine precision when the 188 

optimal flux distribution is unique, thus preserving the exact essential dynamic behavior of the system even after 189 

model reduction. The minor discrepancies observed in the by-product profiles of genome-scale models ( iML1515 190 

and Yeast9) can be attributed to the larger number of degrees of freedom inherent to these reconstructions. As 191 

noted by Smallbone and Simeonidis (2009), highly interconnected networks often possess a degenerate solution 192 

space where multiple distinct flux distributions satisfy the same optimal objective value. Under these conditions, 193 

DFBA and DFVB may naturally follow different—but equally valid—optimal internal trajectories. 194 

From a computational perspective, the initial burden of computing the null-space projection was effectively 195 

mitigated by coupling DFVB with linear solver warm-starts and structural reduction. While warm-starts ensured 196

merely as a dynamic simulator, but as an automated, context-specific pre-processing tool for identifying reduced 202 

model topologies. 203 

Beyond numerical efficiency, the simulation-based reduction applied by DFVB proved to be biologically relevant. 204 

When compared against E. coli transcriptomic profiles, the active reactions identified by DFVB correlated more 205 

closely with gene expression than those identified by FVA. The superior F1-score and higher recall suggest that 206 

DFVB is highly sensitive to capturing the true representative phenotype under specific environmental conditions. 207 

While its aggressive reduction criteria can overestimate the number of inactive reactions, resulting in more false 208 

positives than the FVA, the reduced network topology strikes a superior balance for dynamic modeling, retaining 209 

essential active pathways while removing irrelevant metabolic functions in the simulation context. 210 

A key virtue of DFVB lies in its capacity for structural regularization during kinetic parameter calibration. 211 

Estimating parameters from nonlinear kinetic models in DFBA is notoriously challenging due to solution degeneracy 212 

(Sánchez et al. 2014). In the aerobic batch dataset, this was evidenced by the high uncertainty in Kg and Kie 213 

estimation, reflecting a lack of experimental information in the high-inhibition region. Nonetheless, DFVB effectively 214 

constrained the kinetic search space and narrowed the confidence intervals. Furthermore, since the experiment 215 

was conducted under non-limiting dissolved oxygen conditions, the strict oxygen uptake constrains the saturation 216 

of the cellular respiratory capacity (J Nielsen and Villadsen 1994). DFVB captured this bottleneck effectively, 217 

forcing the excess carbon flux toward fermentative pathways and resulting in an accurate ethanol production 218 

prediction. The virtue of this regularization was even more pronounced in the anaerobic fermentation scenario. 219 

DFBA failed to accurately predict ethanol production because its unconstrained LP optimization maximized the 220 

growth rate through flux distributions that did not correspond to the actual metabolic phenotype of the yeast. 221 

DFVB bypassed this degeneracy by restricting the feasible space to a linear combination of active basis vectors, 222 

effectively enforcing a strict physiological coupling between growth and ethanol synthesis. This structural rigidity 223 

also enabled a more biologically accurate estimation of the nitrogen yield ( Yxn ). It has been demonstrated that 224 

yeast biomass yield on nitrogen increases significantly under deficiency due to a drastic reduction in cellular protein 225 

content, rising from approximately 19 gDW g −1 to 30 gDW g −1 (Boer et al. 2003; Varela et al. 2004). The higher 226 

Yxn estimate obtained by DFVB (36.14 gDW g −1) successfully captured this adaptive metabolic state. In contrast, 227 

DFBA remained trapped in parameter regions associated with nitrogen-rich conditions (14.58 gDW g −1), failing to 228 

identify the varying yield that governed the culture dynamics. It is important to note, however, that this robust 229 

parameter identification relies heavily on the suitability of the initial parameter guess; an incorrect initialization 230 

could potentially lock the reduced DFVB model into a biased structural representation. 231

# Methods 241 

## Flux Balance Analysis 242 

Constraint-based methods are employed for the computation of metabolic fluxes using GEMs (Orth, Thiele, et 243 

al. 2010). The vector of fluxes ( v) is calculated under the steady-state assumption, i.e., there is no significant 244 

accumulation of intracellular metabolites. Additionally, reaction fluxes are phenomenologically constrained by 245

## Null-space Representation of Mass-balanced Fluxes 268 

The homogeneous linear system S · v = 0 can be represented using the right null-space of the stoichiometric matrix 269 

S (Lay et al. 2016). Let N be the null-space of S and α be a real-valued vector. Then, a mass-balanced vector v270 

lies in the column space of N and is given by 271 

v = N · α (5) Although the null space of a matrix is a unique linear subspace, it can be spanned by an infinite number of 272 

possible bases. To minimize computational cost, a specific basis can be selected based on a sparsity criterion (Saa 273 

and LK Nielsen 2016; Bordbar et al. 2014; Gomes de Oliveira Dal’Molin et al. 2018). In this work, the null-space 274 

basis of S is computed using an algorithm based on Rank-Revealing LU (RRLU) decomposition (Pan 2000). Briefly, 275 

the stoichiometric matrix is decomposed as S = LUQ , where L and Q are invertible matrices and U is an upper 276 

triangular matrix that reveals the rank structure. The null-space basis N is then constructed from the columns 277 

of Q−1 corresponding to the zero columns of U. This approach is selected because RRLU preserves the original 278 

matrix entries and structure, thereby facilitating the maintenance of sparsity in the resulting basis and avoiding 279 

the computational burden of SVD approaches (Pan 2000). 280 

The null-space basis matrix can be partitioned into rows corresponding to internal reactions ( Nint ) and external 281 

reactions ( Nex ). By focusing on the partition of the matrix and the associated fluxes, solutions can be systematically 282 

explored using an objective function analogous to that employed in FBA. This approach leads to the formulation 283

It is crucial to establish that the FVB formulation is not an approximation but a reformulation of the original 292

mathematical proof of this proposal in Supplementary File Text S1. Another key insight is the dimensionality 294

requiring only z equality constraints to couple the exchange fluxes ( Nex · α = vex ) alongside the preserved 2m300 

bounds. Since the number of exchange reactions is strictly smaller than the number of metabolites ( z < n ), the 301 

total constraint count decreases from n + 2 m to z + 2 m. Therefore, this reformulation can effectively leverage the 302 

reduced degrees of freedom to improve solver convergence. 303

## Simulation-based Model Reduction 310 

DFVB simulations provide time-resolved trajectories for both metabolite concentrations and the flux-basis coef-311 

ficients α(t), under a given scenario captured by the metabolic objective, bounds and kinetic expressions. Since 312 

each coefficient αj (t) corresponds to the contribution of the specific null-space vector j, its temporal profile directly 313 

reflects the activation of the associated flux mode (Gomes de Oliveira Dal’Molin et al. 2018; Bordbar et al. 2014). 314 

Basis vectors for which αj (t) = 0 for all t ∈ [0 , T ] can therefore be considered inactive throughout the entire 315 

simulation. 316 

These inactive basis vectors can be systematically removed from the null-space matrix N, yielding a reduced 317 

basis that spans only dynamically active metabolic functions. Notably, eliminating columns from N may produce 318 

rows consisting entirely of zeros; the reactions associated with such rows cannot carry flux under the simulated 319 

conditions and are thus also classified as inactive. Once inactive reactions are identified, they can be removed from 320 

the original stoichiometric matrix S, as well as any metabolite who becomes orphan. This sequential elimination of 321 

inactive basis vectors, reactions, and metabolites produces a reduced metabolic model that preserves all dynamically 322 

accessible pathways while discarding components that are irrelevant over the entire DFVB trajectory. A schematic 323 

overview of the entire DFVB method and model reduction framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. Algorithm 1 formalizes 324 

this procedure. 325

## Evaluation of Dynamic Flux Simulations in Metabolic Models 327 

The computational performance of DFVB was compared against DFBA using metabolic models spanning a wide 328 

range of scales. The considered models include the illustrative model from Zhao et al. (2017) (5 metabolites, 7 329 

reactions), which features a single substrate, a product species P, biomass formation, and an internal cycle to 330 

explore alternative metabolic routes. To evaluate biologically realistic networks, we employed the Escherichia coli 331 

core model (73 metabolites, 96 reactions) (Orth, Fleming, et al. 2010), and two genome-scale metabolic models 332 

(GEMs): iML1515 for E. coli (1878 metabolites, 2713 reactions) (Monk et al. 2017) and Yeast9 for Saccharomyces 333

an explicit biomass metabolite and an associated exchange reaction (see Supplementary File Text S2). In all 335 

simulations, growth rate maximization was used as the objective function, assuming growth in a minimal medium. 336 

Three growth regimes were evaluated: aerobic, anaerobic, and oxygen-limited. Glucose and ammonium were 337 

provided as the sole carbon and nitrogen sources, respectively. The bounds for reversible reactions were set to 338 

[−1000 , 1000] mM gDW −1 h−1, while irreversible reactions had their lower bound constrained to zero. Oxygen 339 

availability was controlled by adjusting the lower bound of the uptake flux. For the aerobic and anaerobic scenarios, 340 

this bound was set to −1000 and 0 mM gDW −1 h−1, respectively. For the oxygen-limited condition, uptake was 341 

defined by a Michaelis-Menten expression with a maximum oxygen consumption rate of 20 mM gDW −1 h−1 and 342 

a half-saturation constant of 0.003 mM. In simulations using GEMs, initial conditions were set to 0.5 g L −1 for 343 

biomass, 1.8 g L −1 for glucose, 0.5 g L −1 for ammonium, and zero for all metabolic products. Initial dissolved 344

## Biological Analysis of Model Reduction 364

## Kinetic Parameter Estimation for Modeling Culture Dynamics 395 

To demonstrate the practical advantages of the DFVB framework over conventional DFBA, kinetic parameter cal-396 

ibration was performed in two distinct fermentation scenarios. In standard DFBA, estimating kinetic parameters 397 

from experimental data is notoriously challenging and computationally expensive (Sánchez et al. 2014). By struc-398 

turally reducing the metabolic network and isolating the dynamically active subspace, DFVB significantly decreases 399 

this computational burden, enabling a robust and more efficient parameter estimation process. 400 

The kinetic models calibrated for the aerobic batch culture of S. cerevisiae and Sauvignon Blanc fermentation 401 

are based on Sánchez et al. (2014) and Zenteno et al. (2010), respectively, and they are described in detail in 402 

Supplementary File Text S4. 403 

For the aerobic batch fermentation of S. cerevisiae , the calibrated parameters included the maximum glucose 404 

uptake rate ( vS, max ), the half-saturation constant for glucose ( KS ), the ethanol inhibition constant ( Kie ), the lower 405 

bound on oxygen uptake in the metabolic model ( lb O2 ), and the biomass yield coefficients for glycerol, citrate, and 406 

lactate ( Yx,gly , Yx,cit , Yx,lac ). 407 

For the Sauvignon Blanc fermentation model, the calibrated parameters were the maximum specific growth 408 

rate ( μ0); the half-saturation constants for nitrogen, glucose, and fructose uptake ( Kn, 0, Kg, 0, Kf, 0); the ethanol 409 

inhibition constant ( Kie, 0); the biomass yields on nitrogen, glucose, and fructose ( Yxn , Yxg , Yxf ); the ethanol yield 410 

on fructose ( Yef ); and the baseline production rates of ethanol from glucose and fructose ( βg, 0, βf, 0). 411

The parameter calibration was formulated as an iterative optimization problem. Here, the decision variable 412

detailed mathematical description of this optimization problem is provided in Supplementary File Text S5. For both 416 

kinetic models, the optimization was initialized using parameter estimates from the literature (see Supplementary 417 

File Text S4). The initial conditions were also included as fitted parameters, as experimental error in these mea-418 

surements can severely affect parameter calibration (Cao et al. 2011). Due to the lack of replicates in the Sánchez 419 

et al. (2014) dataset, a variation of 30% relative to the reported values was allowed to constrain the estimation. In 420

Finally, a bootstrap analysis with 1000 resampling iterations was performed for both calibration procedures 423

recalibrated using the resampled data. The resulting parameter distributions were then used to compute the mean 425

and overall calibration performance of DFBA and DFVB. 427 

## Experimental Methods 428 

Two independent experimental datasets were used to evaluate parameter fitting of dynamic models. The first 429 

dataset consisted of laboratory-scale fermentations of Sauvignon Blanc must (blend-quality lot sourced from Fundo 430 

San Ignacio, Lontué, Chile), carried out in 2-L jacketed bioreactors in triplicate. The system followed a stepwise 431 

temperature profile of 19, 16, and 21 ◦C, regulated by a circulating water bath connected to the vessel jackets, 432 

with temperature logging but no closed-loop control. Prior to filling, the must was homogenized, supplemented 433 

with 50 mg L −1 SO 2, as potassium metabisulfite, and adjusted to a pH of 3.45 using tartaric acid. Fermentations 434 

were inoculated with Saccharomyces cerevisiae VL3 (Laffort, France). Nitrogen supplementation consisted of two 435 

aqueous bolus additions of diammonium phosphate (10% w/w stock), applied at inoculation and mid-fermentation. 436 

The reactors were magnetically stirred at 400 rpm for 2 min and samples were collected one to two times per day 437 

via the top port. Biomass concentration and viability were quantified from unprocessed aliquots using the Oculyze 438 

system with 0.01% alkaline methylene violet, while dry cell weight was determined gravimetrically after drying 439 

cell pellets at 60 ◦C for 48 h. All other analytes were measured from the supernatant obtained by centrifugation 440 

(5 min, 4200 rpm). These measurements included density (Anton Paar DM35), ethanol (Anton Paar Alcolyzer), 441 

and enzymatic assays (Biosystems Y15) for glucose, fructose, yeast-assimilable nitrogen, glycerol, and organic acids. 442 

The progression of these fermentations to complete dryness ( <2 g L −1 residual sugars) provided a high-resolution 443 

experimental dataset. By capturing the complex transient dynamics of anaerobic growth under shifting temperature 444 

regimes, this experimental setup provides sufficient data to benchmark the parameter identification capabilities of 445 

both DFBA and DFVB frameworks in highly non-linear scenarios. 446

## Computational Implementation 455 

The algorithms were implemented and executed in MATLAB 2025a (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Linear 456 

programming problems were solved using Gurobi Optimizer 12.0.0 (Gurobi Optimization, Inc., Houston, TX, USA) 457 

with both relative and absolute tolerances set to 10 −8. To enable the warm-start configuration in Gurobi, the 458 

optimal basis from the previous LP ( vbasis and cbasis ) is loaded, and the LPWarmStart parameter is set to 1. 459 

Ordinary differential equations were integrated using MATLAB’s built-in ode113 solver, a variable-step, variable-460 

order Adams–Bashforth–Moulton method (Shampine and Reichelt 1997). Parameter estimation was carried out 461 

using the local optimizer fminsearchbnd (D’Errico 2026), a bound-constrained extension of the standard Nelder-462 

Mead simplex algorithm implemented in MATLAB. This derivative-free method circumvents the limitations of 463 

unconstrained optimization by applying internal coordinate transformations to the decision variables. This approach 464 

enables rigorous enforcement of lower and upper bounds on the kinetic parameters without computing gradients, 465 

ensuring that the calibrated values remain within biologically realistic ranges throughout the optimization process. 466 

Simulations were carried out on a laptop equipped with an Intel Core Ultra i7 155H processor, 16 GB RAM, 64-bit 467 

architecture, running Windows 11. Bootstrapping calibrations were run on a node of the Unix-based Cluster of the 468 

School of Engineering at the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile (more details for the computational resources 469 

employed can be found in Supplementary File Text S6). The code and model reconstructions used in this study 470 

are available in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/SysBioengLab/DFVB). 471

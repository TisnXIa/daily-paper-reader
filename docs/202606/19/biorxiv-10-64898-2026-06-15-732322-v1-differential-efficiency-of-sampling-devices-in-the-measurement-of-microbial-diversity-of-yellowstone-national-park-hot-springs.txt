ABSTRACT 42 

Metagenomic characterization of low-biomass Yellowstone National Park (YNP) hot spring waters 43 

remains challenging because microbial recovery is influenced by filtration methodology, sample 44 

preservation, DNA extraction, and sequencing strategy. We characterized thermophilic microbial 45 

communities in alkaline YNP hot spring waters (62–90.5°C) using three high-temperature-46 

compatible filtration systems (Sterivex, Supor, and polycarbonate membranes), automated onsite 47 

DNA extraction (μTitan), and shotgun metagenomic sequencing with Illumina short-read and 48 

Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long-read platforms. Across all filtration systems and 49 

sequencing workflows, microbial communities were consistently dominated by Bacteria (~90% of 50 

reads), whereas Archaea represented <10% of recovered sequences. Dominant microbial 51 

populations were reproducibly recovered across all approaches; however, recovery of lower-52 

abundance taxa varied among methods. This variability was most evident in polycarbonate-filtered 53 

samples, which exhibited greater replicate-to-replicate variation and less consistent detection of 54 

microbial species. Thermocrinis ruber and related Aquificae -associated thermophiles dominated the 55 

hottest waters (78.5–90.5°C), whereas warmer effluent-channel waters (63.5–66.5°C) contained T. 56 

ruber together with photosynthetic taxa, including Synechococcus spp. and Candidatus Thermochlorobacter 57 

aerophilum . Archaeal communities were primarily represented by Pyrobaculum - and Thermoproteus -58 

related taxa. Non-metric multidimensional scaling analyses indicated that overall community 59 

structure was largely unaffected by filtration or sequencing methodology, whereas alpha-diversity 60 

metrics showed that filter selection influenced richness and diversity estimates. These findings 61 

identify field-deployable workflows for metagenomic characterization of low-biomass thermophilic 62 

aquatic systems and demonstrate the importance of integrating filtration and sequencing strategies 63 

for studying extremophile microbiomes under remote sampling conditions. 64 

IMPORTANCE 65 

Accurate characterization of low-biomass geothermal water microbiomes remains challenging 66 

because microbial recovery is strongly influenced by sample handling, filtration efficiency, DNA 67 

extraction chemistry, and sequencing methodology. This study demonstrated that Yellowstone 68 

National Park alkaline hot spring water microbiomes were consistently dominated by Bacteria 69 

(>90% of recovered reads), whereas Archaea represented <10% of community abundance across all 70 

filtration systems. Although dominant microbial populations were reproducibly recovered, filtration-71 

device selection influenced the recovery of microbial diversity and low-abundance taxa. By 72 

integrating field-deployable onsite DNA extraction with ONT shotgun metagenomic sequencing, 73 

this work evaluates practical workflows for studying thermophilic planktonic microbial communities 74 

under remote field conditions. These findings are relevant, not only to geothermal microbiology, but 75 

also to low-biomass environments in medical, pharmaceutical, and aerospace industries, where rapid 76 

onsite processing and contamination-aware workflows are essential for preserving authentic 77 

microbial signatures in extreme environments. 78

INTRODUCTION 81 

Studies of Yellowstone National Park (YNP) hot spring ecosystems have provided foundational 82 

insights into the ecology, evolution, and functional adaptation of extremophilic microorganisms 83 

inhabiting high-temperature geothermal environments (1, 2). Metagenomic investigations across 84 

YNP thermal features have revealed extensive microbial diversity associated with variations in 85 

temperature, pH, sulfur chemistry, dissolved minerals, and hydrological conditions (3, 4). Accurate 86 

characterization of geothermal water microbiomes remains dependent on sample collection, 87 

filtration, preservation, and DNA extraction procedures (5, 6). In low-biomass environments, even 88 

minor perturbations introduced during sampling and processing can substantially influence 89 

downstream metagenomic interpretation (5). 90 

Most previous YNP metagenomic studies focused on microbial mats and sediment-associated 91 

communities because these matrices contain substantially higher microbial biomass, which yields 92 

sufficient nucleic acids for sequencing with conventional laboratory workflows (2, 6). Microbial mats 93 

and sediments frequently contain approximately 10 8–10 10 cells/g and support dense microbial 94 

consortia structured by steep geochemical gradients. In contrast, planktonic hot spring waters 95 

typically contain ~10 6 cells/mL, requiring filtration of large water volumes to obtain sufficient 96 

material for shotgun metagenomic sequencing (1, 7). 97 

Hot spring water samples also present significant technical challenges for molecular analysis. Most 98 

previous YNP water studies relied on workflows in which samples were collected in the field and 99 

transported to the laboratory for downstream filtration and DNA extraction (1, 2, 7). Such 100 

workflows may introduce physicochemical and biological alterations during transport, cooling, and 101 

storage (5). Thermophilic microorganisms adapted to extreme in situ conditions may undergo rapid 102 

physiological stress following removal from their native thermal environment, potentially resulting in 103 

selective cell lysis, nucleic acid degradation, altered community composition, and reduced recovery 104 

of fragile or low-abundance taxa (8-10). 105 

Therefore, immediate onsite processing may improve recovery and preservation of geothermal 106 

microbial communities by minimizing transport-associated artifacts and preserving authentic 107 

microbial signatures (5, 6, 11). To date, systematic evaluation of field-deployable extraction 108 

workflows for YNP geothermal waters has remained limited (6). In addition, elevated temperatures 109 

exceeding 80°C in many geothermal systems pose practical challenges for conventional filtration 110 

devices and field-based sample handling protocols (1, 8). To address these limitations, a portable 111 

onsite processing workflow was evaluated using the validated Microgravity Tested InsTrument for 112 

Automated Nucleic Acid Extraction (μTitan) system (5, 6, 11). Following biomass concentration, the 113 

μTitan system enabled immediate DNA extraction under remote field conditions, thereby 114 

minimizing delays between sample collection and molecular processing (5, 6). 115 

This study evaluated field-deployable workflows for metagenomic characterization of low-biomass 116 

thermophilic microbial communities in Yellowstone National Park hot spring waters. Three high-117 

temperature-compatible filtration systems were assessed across a temperature range of 63.5°C to 118 

90.5°C, followed by immediate onsite DNA extraction using the μTitan platform and shotgun 119 

metagenomic sequencing on Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platforms. We 120 

hypothesized that filtration methodology and sequencing platform would influence recovery of low-121 

abundance taxa but not alter characterization of dominant microbial populations. Because the 2019 122 

dataset consistently yielded >90% bacterial sequences, we evaluated whether onsite filtration and 123 

μTitan-based DNA extraction introduced bias against archaeal recovery. To address this question, 124 

additional samples collected in 2022 were processed using conventional laboratory-based DNA 125

MATERIALS AND METHODS 128 

Site description 129 

All field experiments were conducted in YNP at alkaline geothermal features in the White Creek 130 

thermal area, located in the Lower Geyser Basin, on June 18, 2019 (Fig. 1). Spring water samples 131 

were collected from the Five Sisters Springs (44.5325 °N, 110.7971 °W), Octopus Spring (44.5340 132 

°N, 110.7978 °W), and effluent channels from these features (see Table 1 for a list of samples). The 133 

temperature and pH of each site was measured in situ using a HQ30d combined pH-temperature 134 

probe (Hach, Loveland, CO, USA). 135 

Physical and chemical parameters of the sampled springs 136 

Vent fluid temperatures ranged between 68˚C to 85 oC, with the effluent channels of Octopus Spring 137 

and Five Sisters Springs measuring between 63.5˚C to 90.5 oC. Aqueous chemistry was measured 138 

from water samples taken either directly from the vent fluid or above the photosynthetic mats. The 139 

White Creek thermal area is generally categorized as a water-dominated alkaline-chloride system (12), 140 

which is fed from subsurface boiling in deep-heated reservoirs. Collectively, the sampling results all 141 

feature high chloride (309 ± 10 mg/L), high arsenic (716 ± 29 μg/L), and low sulphate (18 ± 1 142 

mg/L) concentrations. In addition, the sampled springs are alkaline (pH 7.99–8.85) and contain high 143 

concentrations of sodium (140 ± 6 mg/L Na +), which is typical of the sodium and chloride-rich 144 

waters that form alkaline-chloride hot spring fluids (1). 145 

On-site DNA extraction using μTitan system workflow: 146 

(A) Hot spring water sample collection. Prior to sampling, 7.6 m lengths of Masterflex L/S 15 platinum-147 

cured silicone tubing (Cole-Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) were cut into varying lengths (1.5 m, 148 

3 m, and 3.8 m sections) and sterilized by submerging the tubing into 10% bleach solution for 20 149 

mins. Each tubing section was then rinsed three times with sterile distilled water, coiled, and 150 

then wrapped in pre-autoclaved aluminum foil for transport to the field site. In-line 151 

polycarbonate 47 mm diameter filter holders (Pall Corporation, Port Washington, NY, USA) 152 

were wrapped in aluminum foil and sterilized by autoclave. 153 

To collect water samples, the pump head of a Geopump Peristaltic Pump (GeoTech, Denver, 154 

CO, USA) that was equipped with a long section of the sterile tubing was then unwrapped, while 155 

keeping both ends remained covered in foil to minimize contamination. Tubing was secured in 156 

place, leaving approximately 30 cm on the outflow side of the pump. An inline filter holder was 157 

attached to the outflow tubing using a second shorter piece of tubing to the outflow side of the 158 

filter holder, with the distal end placed into a graduated cylinder for measurement of filtered 159 

volume. The filter holder was subsequently unscrewed and opened. Flame-sterilized tweezers 160 

were used to position 47 mm filter membranes into the holder, reassembled, and visually 161 

inspected to ensure that no cross-threading, twisting, tearing of the membrane, or gasket 162 

displacement had occurred. 163

pole to a location just below the water surface. The pump was initiated and filtration was 165 

performed until 10 L was processed or filter clogging occurred. Following the filtration 166 

completion, the filter assembly was aseptically disassembled, and the filter was aseptically 167 

transferred directly into a screwcap 5 mL tube (VWR, Randor, PA, USA). This procedure was 168 

repeated at each site using both 47 mm diameter 0.22 μm Supor filters and 47 mm diameter 0.45 169

Sequencing workflow 191 

(A) Samples processed in 2019 : Laboratory-based Oxford Nanopore sequencing was performed using 192

synthesized using the rapid PCR Barcoding kit (SQK-RPB004) with 3 μL of adjusted input 194 

volume. PCR cycles were adjusted for total input amounts as follows; 16 cycles for 1 to 3 ng, 18 195 

cycles for >0.25 to 1 ng, and 22 cycles for <0.25 ng. Read depth varied for each sample, and low 196 

reads were obtained for samples that had DNA at or below detection limits according to the 197 

results of the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham MA). While low 198 

read depth was noted for some samples that had high inputs, likely reflecting low microbial 199 

biomass, biofilm-associated interference, or the presence of inhibitory compounds. Whole 200 

genome shotgun library synthesis was performed on 1ng input or maximum input volume using 201 

the Nextera XT whole genome kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), cleaned, quantified 202 

using the Qubit spectrofluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 203 

analyzed for quality using the Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). 204 

Libraries were equimolar pooled and sequenced using single-end 150 bp chemistry on a HiSeq 205 

1500/2500 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Samples were demultiplexed, and fastq files 206 

were used for analysis. 207

Sequence quality control and annotation 222 

Quality control of short-read Illumina sequence data was performed using Trimmomatic (v0.32) to 223 

remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads, using a quality cutoff value set at minimum Phred 224 

score of 20 along the length of the read. Quality control of Nanopore sequence data was performed 225 

using Nanofilt (v2.6.0) and default parameters. Reads shorter than 80 bp were removed from the 226 

data. The DIAMOND aligner (v2.0.4) was used to query the NCBI-NR protein database, and the 227 

MEGAN 6 lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm was used to bin high quality reads to their 228 

respective taxonomy. Additionally, the eggnog, KEGG, and SEED databases were queried for 229 

functional analysis. For long-read Nanopore sequence annotation, the DIAMOND frameshift and 230 

range curling mode was used. The frameshift option performs frameshift-alignment of DNA 231 

sequences against a protein reference database. The parameter used with the frameshift mode was -F 232 

15, leading to a dynamic programming penalty of 15. Range-culling estimates an alignment locally; 233 

this option was used with the parameter as follows --range-culling and --top 10. 234 

Taxonomic classification, relative abundance determination, and sequence quality analyses of native 235 

nanopore sequence datasets generated in 2022 samples were conducted using the One Codex 236 

platform (One Codex, Wilmington, DE, USA) with default parameters. Sequence reads were 237 

assigned taxonomic identities using the One Codex k-mer–based classification algorithm against a 238 

curated microbial reference database (13, 14). 239 

Statistical Analyses 240 

To determine whether long-read ONT sequencing could supplement or replace short-read Illumina 241 

sequencing for more rapid analyses in remote locations, multiple statistical analyses were performed. 242 

Alpha diversity among similar samples was measured with Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices as 243 

calculated by the R vegan package (15), with rarefication just under the size of the smallest library 244 

(N=5000). The R vegan package (15) was also used to visualize beta diversity using nonmetric 245 

multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of Bray-Curtis dissimilarity of rarefied samples. 246

The consistent dominance of Thermocrinis/Aquificae -associated bacterial lineages across filtration 314 

systems and sequencing workflows indicates that the major community members were robustly 315 

recovered regardless of analytical approach. Previous studies of the YNP geothermal ecosystems 316 

frequently emphasized archaeal dominance, particularly within acidic sediments and microbial mat-317 

associated communities where anaerobic microenvironments, sulfur cycling, and steep thermal 318 

gradients promote archaeal proliferation (1, 2). The present study, however, showed that planktonic 319 

alkaline hot spring waters may support relatively similar bacterial-dominated communities despite 320 

the presence of thermophilic archaea. Although these findings differ from previous 16S-based 321 

observations (7), the methods and sample types used are different and differences in microbial 322 

diversity are not unexpected. Thermocrinis species are well known hydrogen- and sulfur-oxidizing 323 

thermophiles that thrive in oxygenated alkaline geothermal waters (16) and are commonly associated 324 

with Aquificae -dominated spring outflows and mixing zones (17, 18). 325 

Alternate sequencing approach to support microbial diversity profile 326 

Selected hot spring water samples collected in 2022 were analyzed using amplification-free native 327 

nanopore sequencing following conventional laboratory-based DNA extraction to independently 328 

characterize microbial community composition. This approach provided a ground-truth assessment 329 

of community structure by eliminating biases associated with PCR amplification and library 330 

preparation. Sequencing quality metrics demonstrated successful recovery of high-molecular-weight 331 

environmental DNA. For sample FSS1, read lengths ranged from approximately 0.5 to 42 kb with 332 

an N50 of 7,532 bp, whereas sample FSS3 yielded reads ranging from 0.5 to 41 kb with an N50 of 333 

5,826 bp. These results indicate that the extracted DNA was of sufficient quality to support long-334 

read sequencing and taxonomic characterization. 335 

The taxonomic profiles obtained from the 2022 samples were consistent with those recovered in the 336 

2019 dataset. Native nanopore sequencing recovered microbial communities composed of 337 

approximately 90% Bacteria and >10% Archaea (Fig. 3), whereas the corresponding Rapid PCR 338 

Barcoding (SQK-RPB004) workflow recovered approximately 95% Bacteria and ~5% Archaea (data 339 

not shown), suggesting improved recovery of archaeal sequences with the amplification-free 340 

approach. Despite differences in relative abundance estimates, both amplification-based and 341 

amplification-free workflows identified the same dominant bacterial and archaeal populations, 342 

supporting the conclusion that thermophilic alkaline hot spring waters were predominantly inhabited 343 

by bacterial communities. To our knowledge, this study represents the first application of native 344 

nanopore sequencing for characterization of Yellowstone hot spring water microbiomes and 345 

highlights the value of amplification-free long-read sequencing for generating robust and minimally 346 

biased assessments of microbial diversity in extreme environments. 347

Influence of filtration material on microbial recovery 349 

Many previous YNP studies have used polycarbonate filters (19) to concentrate microorganisms 350 

from liquid samples, since they are rated to withstand higher temperatures during filtration 351 

(~140˚C). In order to confirm the differential filtration of microbes from hot water, we tested 352 

multiple filtration systems and found that Sterivex filtration system detected more taxa using short 353 

read sequencing (Illumina) of archaea in all Five Sister Spring water samples, especially from hot 354 

samples of both Five Sisters Spring (81.3˚C; FSS3) and one of the Octopus Spring (90.5˚C; OS) 355 

samples. One explanation is that short-read sequencing provides greater sequencing depth and 356 

enables detection of shorter DNA possibly including fragmented eDNA. Nanopore sequencing 357 

revealed that the Supor filtration recovered greater archaeal taxa when compared to Sterivex system 358 

for samples from Five Sisters Springs, possibly because nanopore is less likely to sequence shorter 359 

fragmented DNA from poorly intact organisms. Sterivex filtration also outperformed the 360 

polycarbonate system on recovering archaeal taxa for all hot springs samples tested during this 361 

study. Likewise, the Sterivex workflow detected a greater diversity of bacteria (60 taxa) while only 25 362 

taxa were observed from the polycarbonate method for Octopus Spring (66.5˚C) water samples. Of 363 

note is that at higher temperatures, the outer plastic of the Sterivex flow through filter could be seen 364 

to warp, although this did not affect recovery of Archaea or Bacterial taxa. 365 

Sequencing platforms 366 

Differences between short-read sequencing (Illumina-based) and nanopore sequencing (Oxford 367 

Nanopore) workflows were evident throughout the study. Illumina sequencing generated 368 

substantially higher read depth and more consistent recovery of low-abundance taxa, resulting in 369 

tight clustering of replicate samples in NMDS analyses (Fig 4) and greater reproducibility in alpha 370 

diversity (Fig 5) measurements. ONT sequencing produced longer reads (3 to 8 kb) for rapid PCR 371 

barcoding (RPB004) and 0.5 to 41 kb for the native DNA sequencing (NDB114) approach which 372 

improved taxonomic continuity and enabled long-read mapping across thermophilic genomes, 373 

although total read depth remained lower and variability among replicate samples was greater. This 374 

variability likely reflects stochastic sampling effects and library-preparation biases associated with 375 

low-biomass samples. Similar sequencing-depth limitations associated with ONT workflows have 376 

been reported in other low-biomass environmental metagenomic studies (9, 10). Despite these 377 

differences, dominant taxa were consistently recovered by both platforms. 378 

NMDS and community structure analyses 379 

The NMDS analyses further supported the conclusion that characterization of dominant microbial 380 

communities remained stable across filtration systems and sequencing approaches (Fig. 4). Biological 381 

samples clustered distinctly from field controls, indicating minimal influence of contamination or 382 

kit-associated background DNA on overall microbial community structure. Control samples formed 383 

separate outlier clusters, particularly in ONT datasets, suggesting that background contaminants 384 

contributed minimally to dominant thermophilic microbial signatures. Although subtle separation 385 

among filtration systems was observed, particularly within Illumina datasets, the overall clustering 386 

patterns demonstrated that filtration methodology exerted secondary effects relative to the strong 387 

ecological dominance of shared thermophilic bacterial populations. No strong segregation of 388 

samples by hot spring location was observed within the ordination space, indicating that differences 389 

among planktonic microbial communities were less pronounced than expected across these closely 390 

related alkaline geothermal environments. 391

Alpha diversity analyses indicated that filtration-device selection influenced microbial richness and 393 

community evenness (Fig. 5). Polycarbonate filters often yielded higher Chao1 richness and 394 

Shannon diversity estimates, particularly in ONT datasets, suggesting improved recovery of rare or 395 

low-abundance taxa. These filters have been widely used in geothermal studies because of their high 396 

thermal tolerance and maximum operating temperature of 140°C (19). They also exhibited greater 397 

variability among replicates, indicating that inconsistencies may arise during downstream processing. 398 

In several high-temperature samples, polycarbonate filters recovered lower microbial diversity and 399 

reduced abundance of dominant taxa compared with Sterivex and Supor filters. Because repeated 400 

microscopic examination of autoclaved polycarbonate filters revealed no evidence of structural 401 

damage, the observed variability is unlikely to result from filter degradation. Instead, it may reflect 402 

inefficiencies associated with biomass recovery during the extraction and grinding procedures. 403 

Sterivex filters generally provided the most consistent performance across samples, although their 404 

use for hot water samples (>65˚C) should be approached cautiously because prolonged exposure to 405 

elevated temperatures may compromise filter integrity. These findings suggest that filtration 406 

performance in extreme geothermal environments depends not only on thermal compatibility but 407 

also on the efficiency and reproducibility of downstream sample-processing workflows. 408 

Despite differences in richness estimates, Shannon and Simpson diversity values remained relatively 409 

low across all filtration systems and sequencing workflows. These low diversity values reflect the 410 

strong predominance of a limited number of thermophilic taxa, particularly Thermocrinis/Aquificae -411 

associated bacterial lineages, with only minor contributions from lower-abundance bacterial and 412 

archaeal populations. Similar low-evenness communities have been reported in other geothermal 413 

systems where environmental extremes impose strong selective pressures favoring specialized 414 

thermophilic populations (2, 3). Although filtration methodology influenced recovery of rare taxa 415 

and alpha-diversity estimates, the dominant thermophilic community structure remained highly 416 

consistent across workflows. 417 

The successful implementation of field-deployable molecular workflow(s) for hot spring waters 418 

(~90°C) represents an important step in the metagenomic characterization of low-biomass 419 

geothermal systems under remote sampling conditions. Immediate onsite filtration and portable 420 

nucleic acid extraction with a chemistry-adjustable (tunable) platform such as μTitan minimizes the 421 

delay between sample collection, filter processing, and molecular processing, thereby reducing 422 

opportunities for transport-associated perturbations, selective cell lysis, and nucleic acid degradation 423 

(5, 6). Because thermophilic microorganisms adapted to extreme geothermal environments may 424 

undergo rapid physiological stress following removal from their native habitat, rapid onsite 425 

processing may improve preservation of native microbial signatures and enhance recovery of low-426 

abundance extremophiles (8). 427 

In this study, we evaluated several workflows that were integrated with a field-deployable nucleic 428 

acid extraction device. These methods compared large-volume onsite sample concentration (>5 L) 429 

followed by real-time DNA extraction with preselected chemistry and platform-matched sequencing. 430 

Supor filters are recommended as one of the primary methods when high-molecular-weight DNA 431 

and ONT sequencing are priorities, while Sterivex filters provide a complementary option for 432 

consistent recovery of dominant bacterial and archaeal taxa but must be used with caution for high 433 

temperature samples. Historically, polycarbonate filters have been used for many years (20) and have 434 

shown higher capture richness and rare taxa, but have greater replication variability due to potential 435 

processing and handling issues. 436 

The workflows evaluated here may also be applicable to other low-biomass extremophile systems 437 

relevant to astrobiology (21), planetary protection (22), and remote environmental monitoring 438

ABSTRACT 21 Necrotic enteritis (NE), caused by Clostridium perfringens, is a major enteric disease in poultry 22 with substantial economic impact. NE is frequently triggered by co-infection with Eimeria spp ., 23 yet the relative contributions of Eimeria and C. perfringens to NE-induced dysbiosis and disease 24 progression remain poorly defined. To address this, Cobb broiler chickens were challenged with 25 

Eimeria maxima , C. perfringens , or both, and ileal and cecal microbiota were analyzed using 16S 26 rRNA gene sequencing and shotgun metagenomics. Temporal dynamics of intestinal microbiota 27 shifts were further assessed at multiple time points post-infection. Our findings demonstrate that 28 NE-associated dysbiosis is characterized by enrichment of pathobionts including C. perfringens ,29 

Escherichia , and Enterococcus cecorum , along with differential abundance of certain lactic acid-30 and short chain fatty acid-producing bacteria. Dysbiosis and disease progression were driven by 31 synergistic interactions between E. maxima and C. perfringens , with E. maxima exerting a 32 dominant influence. Notably, E. maxima alone promoted expansion of commensal C. perfringens 33 or closely-related bacteria, even without prior exposure. Metagenomic analysis of the cecal 34 microbiota further revealed a functional shift favoring utilization of host-derived glycans and 35 simple carbohydrates over dietary fibers, in response to E. maxima and NE challenges. This 36 transition coincided with E. maxima -induced epithelial damage, increased mucogenesis, and 37 nutrient malabsorption. NE-associated dysbiosis emerged four days post-E. maxima infection 38 and peaked 2–3 days following C. perfringens challenge. These findings suggest that Eimeria 39 infection creates a permissive intestinal environment for C. perfringens colonization and 40 proliferation, underscoring its pivotal role in NE pathogenesis. 41

IMPORTANCE 43 NE is a major health and economic burden in poultry production, primarily driven by C. 44 

perfringens and potentiated by Eimeria infection. This study provides critical mechanistic 45 insights into the distinct and synergistic roles of E. maxima and C. perfringens in NE disease 46 progression. We show that Eimeria plays a dominant role in driving NE-associated dysbiosis and 47 disease progression by inducing epithelial damage, inflammation, and nutrient malabsorption, 48 which facilitate C. perfringens colonization, proliferation, and toxin production. Comprehensive 49 characterizations of both structural and functional microbiome shifts revealed that NE-associated 50 dysbiosis is marked by enrichment of facultative pathobionts that favors utilization of host-51 derived mucins and simple dietary carbohydrates, alongside depletion of strictly anaerobic, fiber-52 fermenting, SCFA-producing bacteria. These microbial shifts reflect disease progression and 53 offer potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention. Our findings lay a 54 foundation for microbiota-based diagnostics and interventions to mitigate NE and other enteric 55 diseases. 56

INTRODUCTION 59 Necrotic enteritis (NE) is an important poultry disease, responsible for an estimated global 60 economic loss of approximately $6 billion annually (1). NE can be classified into two forms: 61 clinical and subclinical. Clinical NE is characterized by severe intestinal lesions, diarrhea, 62 microbiota shifts, and high mortality rates that can reach up to 50% (2). In contrast, subclinical 63 NE often goes undetected but leads to decreased weight gain and feed efficiency. Due to its high 64

Eimeria spp. , the causative agents of coccidiosis, are recognized as a major predisposing 72 factor for NE in commercial poultry production (2, 3). Eimeria infection causes epithelial 73 damage, exposing extracellular matrix components such as collagen, which serve as binding sites 74 for CP and facilitate the production of virulence factors like NetB toxin (4). Eimeria infection stimulates mucus production, creating a nutrient-rich environment that 76 supports the proliferation of mucolytic bacteria, including C. perfringens (5). Consequently, 77

NE is associated with extensive alternations in the intestinal 82 microbiota, often marked by a reduction in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria and 83 an enrichment of pathobionts such as Escherichia and Enterococcus (6-8). This study aims to dissect the specific roles of CP and EM in driving intestinal dysbiosis 87 and disease progression in NE. By characterizing the structural and predicted functional changes 88 in the ileal and cecal microbiota following single and combined infections, we seek to elucidate 89 the microbial dynamics underlying NE disease progression. These insights will inform the 90 development of microbiome-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for NE and other enteric 91 diseases in poultry. 92

Differential response of chickens to CP, EM, and NE infections 94 To evaluate relative contributions of CP and EM to NE-induced dysbiosis, we orally challenged 95 Cobb broiler chickens with CP, EM, or both pathogens to induce NE ( Fig. 1A ). CP infection 96 alone resulted in no mortality, while EM challenge led to a survival rate of approximately 93%. 97 In contrast, co-infection with CP and EM significantly reduced survival to 26% ( Fig. 1B ). 98 Among the 39 surviving co-infected chickens, 18 exhibited mild intestinal lesions (score 1), 17 99 showed severe necrotic lesions along the jejunum and ileum (score 6), and four had moderate 100 lesions (scores 2–3) based on a 6-point scoring system (13). 101 Given marked microbiome differences between mild and severe NE cases (7), we 102 stratified co-infected birds into mildly affected (NEm, score 1) and severely affected (NEs, score 103 6) groups for downstream microbiome analyses. The four birds with intermediate lesion scores 104 were excluded due to limited sample size. No NE-characteristic lesions were observed in the 105 mock, CP, or EM groups, all of which received a lesion score of 0 ( Fig. 1C ). Although all groups 106 began with comparable body weights ( Fig. 1D ), significant growth retardation was observed in 107 the EM, NEm, and NEs groups by the end of the trial, but not in the CP group ( Fig. 1E , 1F ). 108

Functional shifts in the cecal microbiome in response to CP, EM, and NE infections 186 To investigate functional alterations in the cecal microbiota in response to infections with CP, 187 EM, or NE, we performed shotgun metagenomic sequencing on six cecal microbial DNA 188 samples per treatment group. After filtering out host-derived reads, a total of 798 Gb of high-189 quality paired-end sequences were obtained, averaging 13.3 Gb per sample. Contigs from both 190 individual and co-assembled metagenomes were binned, yielding 3,448 bins, of which 1,959 191 were of high quality, with estimated  80% completeness and  5% contamination. DISCUSSION 279 NE, primarily caused by C. perfringens , is characterized by necrotic lesions in the small intestine 280 of broiler chickens (2). However, C. perfringens alone is insufficient to induce NE, and 281 additional predisposing factors are required to trigger clinical NE (3). Among these, Eimeria 282 infection is widely recognized as the most significant contributor to NE development in 283 commercial poultry production. Eimeria is a large genus of obligate intracellular parasites that 284 invade the enterocytes upon ingestion. Approximately 3–4 days post-infection, the parasites 285 undergo several rounds of asexual and sexual reproduction, causing rupture of epithelial cells, 286 inflammation, and nutrient malabsorption, which peaks at 6–7 dpi following the formation and 287 shedding of oocysts (15). Consequently, co-infection models with sequential Eimeria and C. 288 

perfringens challenges are commonly employed in experimental studies (3). 289

Both Eimeria infection and clinical NE are associated with intestinal dysbiosis, with the 290 extent of microbial disruption closely correlating with the severity of intestinal pathology (7). 291 Despite this, few studies have directly assessed relative contributions of Eimeria and C. 292 

perfringens to NE-associated dysbiosis. Of the four published studies addressing this question, 293 three reported only mild intestinal lesions and no associated mortality across treatment groups 294 (9-11), while the fourth documented a moderate mortality rate of approximately 22% in the NE-295 challenged group (12). These studies more closely resemble subclinical NE and may not fully 296 capture the pathophysiological features of clinical NE, which is characterized by severe intestinal 297 lesions, profound dysbiosis, and mortality rates that can reach up to 50% (2). Furthermore, most 298 existing studies focused on either the ileal/jejunal or cecal microbiota, often limiting taxonomic 299 resolution to the genus level rather than more informative ASV or species level. These 300 limitations in low disease severity, minimum sampling sites and insufficient taxonomic 301 resolution, have contributed to inconsistent findings across studies and hindered a clear 302 understanding of the respective roles of Eimeria and C. perfringens in NE-induced dysbiosis and 303 disease progression, particularly in the context of clinical NE. 304 In the present study, we experimentally infected chickens with E. maxima , C. 305 

perfringens , and their combination to delineate their respective impacts on the structural and 306 functional dynamics of the intestinal microbiota in chickens with NE. Our NE co-infection 307 model resulted in 74% lethality, with 43.6% of surviving birds exhibiting severe intestinal 308 lesions consistent with clinical NE, and the remainder showing mild lesions indicative of 309 subclinical NE. We analyzed structural changes of the microbiome in both the ileal and cecal 310 samples, stratifying NE-infected birds by disease severity and enhancing taxonomic resolution 311 down to the ASV level. maxima , C. perfringens , and their combination enabled clear dissection of the relative 504 contributions of each pathogen to microbiota disruption during NE.

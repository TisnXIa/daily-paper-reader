Abstract 27 

Host–associated microbiomes play vital roles in organismal health, ecological interactions, and 28 

evolution, yet little is known about how microbial communities shift during the transition from 29 

aquatic to increasingly terrestrial habitats. Here, we characterize the skin microbiomes of three 30 

combtooth blenny species ( Blenniella paula , Praealticus labrovittatus , and Alticus arnoldorum )31 

that occupy distinct positions along the intertidal gradient in Guam—from fully subtidal (and 32 

exclusively aquatic) to intertidal (amphibious) and supratidal environments (exclusively 33 

terrestrial). Using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, we compared skin-associated bacterial 34 

communities with those in surrounding seawater and substrate biofilms to assess habitat 35 

influences on microbiome structure. Skin microbiomes were distinct from environmental 36 

microbial communities, indicating strong ecological filtering by the host. The divergence 37 

between skin and substrate microbiomes in the three species parallels their distribution along 38 

progressively higher zones of the intertidal gradient. The most divergent skin microbiome was 39 

that of the supratidal fish A. arnoldorum , characterized by higher Gammaproteobacteria 40 

abundance and enrichment of epiphytic and mucus-associated taxa. Across all species, we 41 

identified 32 microbial orders significantly enriched on the skin relative to environmental 42 

samples, including taxa commonly associated with fish mucosa (e.g., Vibrio , Alteromonas ,43 

Cetobacterium ) and others rarely reported in aquatic marine fish (e.g., Rubritalea ,44 

Granulosicoccus ). Several rare taxa with potential pathogenicity were also detected at low 45 

abundances. Together, these findings suggest that habitat-specific selective pressures strongly 46 

shape fish skin microbiomes along subtidal (aquatic) to supratidal (terrestrial) habitats and 47 

suggest that microbial symbionts may contribute to the ecological and physiological adaptations 48 

enabling amphibious lifestyles. This study provides the first comparative assessment of skin 49 

microbiome divergence across amphibious fish species along an intertidal gradient and offers a 50 

framework for predicting microbiome responses to environmental change. 51

Introduction 56 

Microbiome is increasingly recognized as an essential player in shaping the host’s 57 

function and fitness. Host and microbiome form the “holobiont”, which has been proposed as the 58 

unit of evolution upon which natural selection can act (Brucker and Bordenstein, 2012). In 59 

particular, skin microbiomes are essential for organisms’ overall health, survival, and adaptation 60 

to environmental conditions (Ross et al. , 2019; Callewaert et al. , 2020; Sehnal et al. , 2021). The 61 

skin acts as a physical barrier that protects the body from harmful environmental factors and 62 

potential pathogens, and the skin-associated bacteria affect resistance and/or susceptibility to 63 

infections (Ross et al. , 2019; Sehnal et al. , 2021; Akat et al. , 2022). In some fish species, the skin 64 

fulfills additional physiological roles, such as gas exchange (Glover et al. , 2013). Skin 65 

microbiomes contain beneficial bacteria that enhance immunity and commensal bacteria that 66 

protect against pathogens through competitive exclusion (Llewellyn et al. , 2014; Reverter et al. ,67 

2018; Sehnal et al. , 2021). Often, fish skin is covered by a mucous layer that serves as a barrier 68 

and a site for microbial exchange, as it is in constant contact with the surrounding water 69 

(Esteban, 2012; Llewellyn et al. , 2014). Thus, skin microbiomes are often more similar to the 70 

environment than internal gut microbiomes, the latter reflecting species-specific adaptations and 71 

being likely vertically inherited (Minich et al. , 2022). Skin microbiomes may be used to study 72 

environmental perturbations, whereas gut microbiomes are more appropriate to identify 73 

phylosimbiosis—the correspondence between host phylogeny and microbiome (Sylvain et al. ,74 

2020). 75 

While phylosymbiosis is crucial for skin microbiome composition, the environment plays 76 

an important role in microbiome shifts and modifications (Ross et al. , 2019; Lim and 77 

Bordenstein, 2020; Bell et al. , 2024). In aquatic animals, microbiomes are influenced by water 78 

salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen (Ross et al. , 2019; Sehnal et al. , 2021; McMurtrie et 79 

al. , 2025). In commercially relevant fish, these have been extensively studied for their immediate 80 

implications in aquaculture (Gomez & Primm, 2021; Debnath et al., 2023; Bell et al., 2024; 81 

McMurtrie et al., 2025), but analyses in wild settings that investigate ecological and evolutionary 82 

implications are less common, despite their importance for understanding host–microbe 83 

interactions. 84

One of the most extreme ecological shifts in fish is the conquest of intertidal and 85 

supratidal environments, or, more broadly, the transition from aquatic to terrestrial environments 86 

(Ord and Cooke, 2016). The importance of microbiomes in the process of terrestrialization has 87 

been proposed (Cannicci et al. , 2020) but to date, only extremely fragmentary data exist, leaving 88 

this hypothesis largely untested. An analysis of gill-associated microbiomes in brachyuran crabs 89 

identified species-specific microbiomes, but the differences were not clearly linked to breathing 90 

adaptations (Bacci et al. , 2023). Another study analyzed the microbiome of aestivating lungfish 91 

and identified granulocytes as key for pathogen defense (Heimroth et al. , 2021). Aestivation, 92 

however, is a highly derived, extreme adaptation in some lungfish species that, besides 93 

adaptation to terrestrial stressors, it involves metabolic lethargy and cocoon secretion, additional 94 

aspects that can act as confounding factors in understanding the role of the microbiome in 95 

adaptation to out-of-water conditions. 96 

Among fish, the family Blenniidae includes species with some of the most extreme 97 

amphibious adaptations to the supratidal environment (Ord and Cooke, 2016). Combtooth 98 

blennies are benthic fish that inhabit shallow coastal waters, including the intertidal and 99 

supratidal zones (Hundt et al ., 2014). They lack scales and secrete thick mucus layers, two 100 

features that could have been adaptive for species that venture out of water. For example, they 101 

could facilitate cutaneous respiration and immune function in high‑oxygen environments 102 

(Lemopoulos and Montoya-Burgos, 2021), provide protection against pathogens and desiccation, 103 

or reduce friction for locomotion out of water (Akat et al., 2022; Esteban, 2012). Importantly, the 104 

lack of scales and the presence of a thick mucus layer could be associated with differences in the 105 

skin-associated microbiomes. 106 

To date, there are no comparative studies of the skin microbiomes of combtooth blennies. 107 

However, three studies on the gut and gill microbiome of blennies provide some insight into the 108 

environmental drivers of their microbiomes. Hundt et al. (2017) analyzed the gut microbiota of 109 

two species with divergent diets: Chasmodes saburrae , which feeds on benthic or epiphytic 110 

crustaceans, and Scartella cristata , which feeds on plants and detritus. The increased abundance 111 

of Vibrionaceae , Methylococcaceae , and Fusobacteriaceae in S. cristata was linked to 112 

differences in their diets. Yoshida et al. (2022) compared the gut microbiomes of Andamia 113 

tetradactylus , a highly amphibious species feeding exclusively on algae, and Entomacrodus 114

species than between organs across different species. This pattern suggests that host-specific 122 

factors, potentially interacting with environmental exposure, play a stronger role than organ type 123 

alone in shaping bacterial communities in fish. 124 

Despite extensive work on morphological and physiological adaptations (Hsieh, 2010; 125 

Egan et al., 2021), predation pressures (Ord et al., 2017), and abiotic conditions in rockpools 126 

(Ord et al., 2024), the role of the skin microbiome in the terrestrialization of combtooth blennies 127 

remains unexplored. Here, we study the skin microbiomes of three combtooth blenny species 128 

that occupy distinct habitats along the intertidal gradient in the Pacific island of Guam. 129 

Blenniella paula resides in the subtidal zone and water pools below the lowest tide line (Egan et 130 

al ., 2021), representing the ancestral aquatic conditions of combtooth blennies (Ord and Cooke, 131 

2016). Praealticus labrovittatus prefers the intertidal zone and is often found in rock pools above 132 

the tide waterline, as well as frequently out of the water entirely, and is regarded as amphibious 133 

(Platt et al ., 2016). Alticus arnoldorum is considered exclusively terrestrial and inhabits rocks in 134 

the supratidal zone above the high tide line, which are constantly splashed but not submerged 135 

(Ord and Cooke, 2016; Platt et al. , 2016). These three species exhibit various degrees of 136 

dependence on water, and thus could be considered as representatives of evolutionary 137 

“snapshots” of a hypothetical evolutionary path of increasingly amphibious lifestyles (Ord and 138 

Cooke, 2016). 139 

The intertidal and supratidal zones are characterized by very variable and extreme 140 

conditions, including high water velocities and turbulence, fluctuating salinity and/or oxygen 141 

levels, and intermittent air exposure that can lead to desiccation; all factors that exert intense 142 

selective pressures on fish morphology and physiology (Hsieh, 2010; Egan et al., 2021). By 143 

contrast, subtidal habitats offer greater stability and structural complexity, allowing for niche 144

broader implications for understanding evolutionary transitions from aquatic to terrestrial life, 153 

suggesting that microbial communities may have played a role in such adaptations. Moreover, by 154 

linking habitat characteristics to microbiome composition, this research offers a framework for 155 

predicting microbiome responses to future environmental changes in coastal ecosystems. 156

Materials and methods 158 

Sampling 159 

Ten individuals each of A. arnoldorum, P. labrovittatus, and B. paula, were caught in 160 

Pago Bay, Guam, on May 18 and 19, 2023. Individuals were caught in the intertidal zone using 161 

hand-held nets and transferred into new zip-lock bags, each containing 50 mL ( A. arnoldorum 162 

and P. labrovittatus ) or 100 mL ( B. paula ) of autoclaved seawater. Fish were left in the bags for 163 

30 seconds to rinse off external surfaces that could have been contaminated during fishing and 164 

handling. Water was removed, and fish were swabbed (both ventral and dorsal areas) to sample 165 

mucus microbiomes. To assess the relative contribution of the environment to skin microbiomes, 166 

we sampled the biofilms on the substrates where the three species were found: subtidal rock 167 

holes for B. paula , rock pools for P. labrovittatus , and supratidal rocks for A. arnoldorum . In the 168 

case of B. paula , we sampled the exact substrate on which each fish was residing; thus, the 169 

number of fish skin samples equals the number of fish substrates. For the other two fish species, 170 

which are more mobile, we sampled rocks or rockpools where the fish were found, but the 171 

numbers of skin and substrate do not exactly correspond. For comparison, we also collected 3 L 172 

of seawater samples from our general sampling area. Specifically, we sampled nearshore surface 173 

water near the A. arnoldorum and P. labrovittattus habitat and again approximately 10 m from 174

Microbiome profiles 179 

DNA was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA extraction kit (Zymo Research) 180 

following the manufacturer's protocol. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using 181 

modified forward 515F (Parada et al. , 2016) and reverse 806R (Apprill et al. , 2015) primers. The 182 

product from the first PCR was used in the second PCR, which was designed to complete the 183 

addition of Illumina adapters and the 6-mer dual indices as previously described by (Kozich et 184 

al. , 2013). Both PCRs were generated using Takara PCR buffer and Hot Start polymerase 185 

(Takara). The first PCR was carried out in 30-μL reactions with the following final 186 

concentrations: dNTPs at 0.8 mM, each primer at 0.5 μM, and 30-100 ng of total DNA. The first 187 

PCR cycling conditions were as follows: a hot-start activation step for 2 min at 95 °C, followed 188 

by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 40 s, 58 °C for 2 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension of 5 189 

min at 72 °C. The PCR was purified using GeneJet (ThermoFisher). The second PCR was 190 

performed in a 20-μL reaction volume using the same components and final concentrations as 191 

above, with 30 ng of the first PCR product serving as the template. The second PCR cycling 192 

conditions were as follows: a hot-start activation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by five cycles of 193 

95 °C for 40 s, 59 °C for 2 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, and concluded with a final extension at 72 194 

°C for 7 min. All samples were pooled and gel-purified using the Qiagen gel purification kit. The 195 

V4-16S library pool was sent to Michigan State University Research Technology Support 196 

Facility Genomics Core for Illumina MiSeq sequencing, using a 2 x 250 bp cycling kit. 197 

Data quality of the raw sequencing reads for each sample was evaluated using the FastQC 198 

tool version 0.12.1 (Brown et al. , 2017). The remaining analyses were conducted in R v. 4.5.1 199 

using multiple packages, including dada2 v. 1.34 (Callahan et al. 2016), phyloseq (McMurdie 200 

and Holmes 2013), microbiome (Leo Lahti 2017), and microViz (Barnett D.J.M.). Specifically, 201 

after quality inspection, the raw reads were trimmed and filtered using the dada2 package with a 202 

minimum Phred quality score of 10. The same package was used to generate the ASV (Amplicon 203 

Sequences Variants) sequence table and to remove chimeric ASVs. Taxonomy was assigned 204

Divergence of skin microbial profiles reflects increasingly terrestrial lifestyles 230 

In this study, we generated bacterial microbial profiles for the skin of three combtooth 231 

blenny fish species that inhabit increasingly higher zones of the intertidal gradient: B. paula in 232 

the subtidal areas, P. labrovittatus in intertidal rockpools, and A. arnoldorum in supratidal rocks. 233

Fish skin as an ecological filter on environmental microbiomes 382 

The skin microbiomes were significantly different and less diverse than the 383 

bacterioplankton in seawater and the biofilms on the substrates on which fish lived. This 384 

suggests that fish skin acts as an ecological filter for the bacterial species available in the 385 

environment. Our findings contrast with those of Pratte et al. (2018), which found higher alpha 386 

diversity in the gill and gut microbiomes of fish than in the environment. However, the species, 387 

geographical location, and tissues in Pratte et. al . (2018) differed from ours. The ecological filter 388 

is further corroborated by the marked differences between fish skin microbiomes and the 389 

substrates on which they live. This pattern was consistent across all three host fish studied. 390 

Moreover, skin microbiomes increasingly diverged among species inhabiting increasingly higher 391 

areas along the intertidal gradient, while substrate microbiomes were largely overlapping. 392 

Substrate and skin microbiomes were most similar for the subtidal B. paula, which lives in a 393 

tight association with the substrate in rock holes and crevices in the reef flat. Substrate and skin 394 

microbiomes diverged for P. labrovittatus, which inhabits intertidal rockpools, and were most 395 

dissimilar between substrate and skin of A. arnoldorum, which typically inhabits supratidal rocks 396 

(Myers, 1999). This suggests that the intertidal gradient imposes a second ecological filter on the 397 

bacterial species that occur on fish skin, a filter that increases with the increasingly terrestrial 398 

lifestyles of fish hosts. 399

Habitat shapes skin microbiome in combtooth blennies 401 

The three fish species inhabiting the subtidal, intertidal, and supratidal zones diverged 402 

markedly in their skin microbiomes, mirroring their level of terrestriality. Host-specific profiles 403 

indicate that skin microbiomes are largely shaped by intrinsic factors, in agreement with previous 404 

research (Gomez and Primm, 2021; Sehnal et al. , 2021). Microbiomes often display a 405

phylosymbiotic signal, meaning that microbial communities reflect the evolutionary divergence 406 

of host species. For example, skin microbiomes were more similar within rather than between 407 

fish host families in a study of seven wild fish species of four families (Labridae, Sebastidae, 408 

Sparidae, and Rajidae) off the southern coast of Korea (Han et al. , 2024). Other studies found 409 

that environmental factors such as habitat and diet can significantly alter the phylosymbiotic 410 

signal (Bell et al. , 2024). In our study, the skin microbiome profiles of B. paula and P. 411 

labrovittatus were more similar to each other than those of A. arnoldorum . This pattern does not 412 

reflect phylogenetic affinities, as P. labrovittatus is more closely related to A. arnoldorum than to 413 

B. paula (Figure 1; Hundt et al. 2014; Muñoz-Sánchez, et al. 2026). Instead, skin microbiomes 414 

reflect habitat similarity in the intertidal ecotone: B. paula and P. labrovittatus remain largely 415 

submerged, respectively, in the subtidal and in intertidal rock pools, whereas A. arnoldorum has 416 

a terrestrial lifestyle on rocks above the waterline, consistent with its more divergent microbiome 417 

profile. This suggests that environmental conditions on rocks above the water line are different 418 

enough from those in the subtidal or rockpools to override potential phylosymbiotic signal in the 419 

microbiome composition. Adult A. arnoldorum live out of water and their activity typically 420 

peaks around mid-tide and with moderate temperatures, individuals retreating to shelter in rock 421 

holes and crevices at low tides (Ord and Tonia Hsieh 2011; Ord and Cooke 2016). Thus, A. 422 

arnoldorum likely experiences markedly different conditions in the supratidal, with higher light 423 

and UV exposure, more variable temperatures, increased osmotic stress and desiccation risk, and 424 

distinct food resources and pathogen communities. Differences between the skin microbiomes of 425 

B. paula and P. labrovittatus are likely associated with particular environmental conditions in 426 

their preferred habitats. Unlike subtidal waters, tidal pools like those inhabited by P. labrovittatus 427 

can have large fluctuations in temperature and dissolved oxygen and other abiotic factors (Ord et 428 

al. , 2024), which could affect the skin microbiome composition. 429

statistically higher relative abundance in A. arnoldorum than in the other two hosts. This class 435

The dominant Enterobacterales (2.8–48.5%) genera were Vibrio (0.4–19%), Alteromonas 458

another two combtooth blenny species. They isolated and sequenced the genomes of two Vibrio 463 

strains that encode multiple digestive enzymes, including alginate lyases, amylases, chitinases, 464 

and mannosidases, which could help degrade algal polysaccharides in fish guts. In our study, 465

the possibility of them being opportunistic pathogens. 480

Other bacterial classes enriched in fish skin microbiome 506 

Fusobacteria were a third bacterial class enriched in the blennies' skin microbiomes. In 507 

some cases, it constitutes a significant proportion of the microbiome (7–34%), showing a 508 

statistically higher relative abundance than in seawater (2–4%) or on substrates (0–6%). 509 

Fusobacteria were significantly more prevalent in P. labrovittatus (13%) than in A. arnoldorum 510 

(5%) and B. paula (2%). They were represented by a single order, Fusobacteriales , and four 511 

genera: Cetobacterium (0.74–11.59%), Propionigenium (1.23–1.59%), Fusobacterium (0– 512 

0.46%), and Psychrilyobacter (0.01–0.08%). Cetobacterium is one of the best-studied fish 513 

bacterial symbionts (Tsuchiya et al. , 2008; Xie et al. , 2022; Tsuchiya and Sugita, 2023; Zhao et 514 

al. , 2023; M. Wang et al. , 2025; S. Wang et al. , 2025) commonly found in the gut and skin of 515 

many freshwater fish (Ramírez et al. , 2018; Arias et al. , 2019; Krotman et al. , 2020; Coetzer et 516 

al. , 2021) and occasionally on marine fish skin (Han et al. , 2024). Cetobacterium isolates from 517 

the guts of aquatic animals can synthesize vitamin B12, helping their hosts meet vitamin 518 

requirements (Tsuchiya et al. , 2008; Suzuki et al. , 2022). Supplementing fish diets with 519 

Cetobacterium improves growth and overall health and enhances resistance to bacterial 520 

infections (Xie et al. , 2022; Zhou et al. , 2022; M. Wang et al. , 2025; S. Wang et al. , 2025). In the 521 

KEGG classification, vitamin B12 production was grouped with porphyrin metabolism, and 522 

Cetobacterium was predicted to be among the top-contributing genera to this functional category 523 

(Figure 5). Cetobacterium was also predicted to contribute the highest proportion (7.25%) to 524 

butanoate metabolism, whose primary product is butyric acid, a short-chain fatty acid found in 525 

the guts of omnivorous and herbivorous fish that has been shown to inhibit pathogens and 526

abundance decreases following Vibrio infection (Cámara-Ruiz et al. , 2021), which suggests 539

prediction suggested a role in the degradation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and flavonoids. 544 

Since GAGs are major components of skin mucus (Tiralongo et al. , 2020), and bacteria have 545 

GAG-degrading enzymes (Zhang et al. , 2020), Rubritalea is most likely an important contributor 546 

to the maintenance and recycling of the fish mucosal surface. Rubritalea is significantly enriched 547 

in A. arnoldorum compared to the other two fish species, which may be related to the specific 548 

composition or thickness of its mucus coating. Two other genera within Verrucomicrobiia were 549 

enriched in fish skin: Haloferula, which is sensitive to salinity changes and reduces its 550 

abundance at lower salinities (Lai et al. , 2022), and Roseibacillus, found on lumpfish skin 551 

(Jacobsen et al. , 2025). Multiple Roseibacillus species have been isolated from algae, indicating 552 

that fish skin microbiomes can be temporarily colonized by epiphytic bacteria, particularly in 553 

species that spend time in algal-covered habitats. 554

Conclusions 656 

The skin microbiomes of three combtooth blenny species were studied in the context of 657 

adaptation to increasingly amphibious lifestyles. Skin-associated communities were distinct from 658 

those on surrounding seawater and rock substrates, indicating that fish skin is an important 659 

ecological filter. A second ecological filter is imposed by the intertidal gradient, as evidenced by 660 

fish skin microbiomes that diverge progressively with the increasing terrestriality of host fish, the 661 

supratidal amphibious Alticus arnoldorum harboring the most distinct skin microbiome. 662 

Pseudomonadota (Alpha and Gammaproteobacteria ) dominated the skin microbiome and the 663 

balance between Alpha - and Gammaproteobacteria shifted with habitat: Alphaproteobacteria 664 

were more abundant in the subtidal B. paula and intertidal P. labrovitattus , whereas 665 

Gammaproteobacteria were enriched in the supratidal A. arnoldorum. Skin communities also 666 

showed enrichment of epiphytic-associated taxa, consistent with benthic lifestyles and frequent 667 

contact with algae-covered substrates. Functional predictions suggest that the skin microbiomes 668 

of these fish differ not only in taxonomic composition but also in metabolic potential. Pathways 669 

related to algal-derived compound metabolism (e.g., photosynthesis and sulfoquinovose-670 

associated carbohydrate metabolism) were comparatively enriched in A. arnoldorum , consistent 671 

with greater exposure to light in supratidal rocks. In contrast, subtidal and intertidal species 672 

showed a higher predicted representation of xenobiotic degradation pathways, which may be 673 

explained by their higher exposure to dissolved and sediment-associated contaminants in 674 

underwater environments. While these functional inferences require validation using 675

with increasingly terrestrial lifestyles, positioning the intertidal as a natural experiment in 684 

microbiome assembly under variable exposures to water, oxygen, and environmental chemicals. 685 

Future work should resolve taxa at strain level and test functional mechanisms—such as vitamin 686 

and micronutrient synthesis, antioxidant production, mucin/glycan utilization, and xenobiotic 687

ABSTRACT 49 Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs) carry 10 to 30% mortality. Current empirical 50 antimicrobial guidance derives almost entirely from Western cohorts dominated by Streptococcus 51 

pyogenes and aerobic-anaerobic consortia, yet whether this microbial paradigm applies to tropical, 52 high-antimicrobial-pressure settings has not been tested with culture-independent methods. We 53 did a prospective cohort study of 169 patients with intraoperatively confirmed NSTI at a North 54 Indian tertiary center (2021 to 2024). Wound tissue underwent aerobic and anaerobic culture, 55 QIIME2-based 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (V3-V4), and targeted SYBR Green 56 quantitative PCR (qPCR) for Acinetobacter baumannii and S. pyogenes . The wound microbiota 57 was overwhelmingly Gram-negative and polymicrobial, anchored by A. baumannii (culture, 58 33.7%; metagenomics, 49.1%; qPCR, 37.9%), Escherichia coli (32.0%), and Klebsiella 59

IMPORTANCE 66 Necrotizing soft tissue infections kill rapidly, and physicians must choose antibiotics before 67 laboratory results are available. Globally, treatment guidelines assume the dominant pathogen is 68 

Streptococcus pyogenes , a Gram-positive organism, because nearly all molecular evidence comes 69 from Western countries. We show that in a large Indian cohort, the infection is instead dominated 70 by Gram-negative bacteria, especially Acinetobacter baumannii , with S. pyogenes rarely present. 71 Polymicrobial status, not wound location or injury mechanism, most strongly determines 72 community composition. These findings challenge the one-size-fits-all antibiotic approach and 73 argue for region-specific empirical regimens prioritizing Gram-negative coverage in tropical, high-74 antimicrobial-pressure settings. 75

INTRODUCTION 78 Necrotizing soft tissue infections (NSTIs) are rapidly progressive infections of the subcutaneous 79 fascia and muscle that carry a mortality of 10 to 30% despite prompt surgical debridement and 80 broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy (1, 2). Outcome depends critically on the speed of source 81 control and on the appropriateness of empirical antibiotic cover initiated before microbiological 82 confirmation. The historical microbiological framework distinguishes type I disease 83 (polymicrobial, aerobic-anaerobic) from type II disease (monomicrobial, predominantly 84 

Streptococcus pyogenes ) (1, 3, 4) and underpins current empirical antimicrobial guidance 85 worldwide. This taxonomy, and the antibiotic regimens built upon it, were constructed almost 86 entirely from Western, temperate-climate cohorts. 87

The most rigorous molecular characterization of NSTI to date is the multicenter INFECT study, 88 which combined 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing with dual host-pathogen RNA-seq in 148 89 Scandinavian patients and reaffirmed S. pyogenes dominance in monomicrobial disease alongside 90 anatomically structured anaerobic communities in polymicrobial infection (5). This single dataset, 91 together with smaller Western series, anchors current understanding of the NSTI wound 92 microbiome. The broader wound-microbiome literature, however, indicates that the microbial 93 composition of soft tissue infection is shaped by host comorbidity, environmental exposure, and 94 prior antimicrobial pressure (6, 7), and that polymicrobial community structure rather than any 95 single isolate often predicts clinical trajectory (8). Whether the INFECT pattern extends to 96 populations outside northern Europe is therefore an empirical question, not a settled one. 97 Indian and broader South Asian case series consistently report aerobic Gram-negative organisms, 98 particularly Acinetobacter baumannii , Escherichia coli , and Klebsiella pneumoniae , as the 99 predominant culture isolates from necrotizing soft tissue disease (9, 10). A. baumannii is of 100 particular clinical concern: it is environmentally persistent, frequently multidrug resistant, biofilm 101 forming, and has emerging recognition as a primary pathogen in necrotizing fasciitis (11–13). Yet, 102 to our knowledge, no published study had applied culture-independent 16S metagenomics to NSTI 103 in an Indian cohort, and the diagnostic performance of culture, quantitative PCR (qPCR), and 104 amplicon sequencing in tropical NSTI had not been compared in a chance-corrected framework. 105 This evidence gap directly affects empirical antimicrobial choice in the highest-mortality phase of 106 clinical care. 107 We therefore did an integrated, multimodal characterization of the NSTI wound microbiome from 108 the Indian subcontinent. In a prospective cohort of 169 patients at a North Indian tertiary referral 109 center, we combined aerobic and anaerobic culture, QIIME2-based 16S rRNA gene amplicon 110 sequencing, and targeted SYBR Green qPCR to address three aims: (i) identify the organisms that 111 dominate North Indian NSTIs and compare this with the Western paradigm, (ii) determine which 112 clinical variable, anatomic site, traumatic etiology, or polymicrobial status, most strongly shapes 113 the wound microbial community, and (iii) compare culture, qPCR, and amplicon sequencing as 114 diagnostic modalities using both one-directional sensitivity and chance-corrected Cohen’s κ. 115

Study design and patients 117

This prospective observational cohort study enrolled patients with intraoperatively confirmed 118 NSTIs at the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, 119 North India, between 2021 and 2024. The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study (IEC-120 INT/2022/PhD-406), and the work was done in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; 121 written informed consent was obtained from all participants. NSTI diagnosis required 122 intraoperative confirmation of necrotizing infection involving fascia or muscle as determined by 123 the operating surgeon. Of 270 patients evaluated, 169 met the inclusion criteria after excluding 124 those with insufficient sample material, those who were pregnant, or those who declined consent 125 (see Fig. S1 in the supplemental material). Patients were stratified by anatomic site (group A, 126 extremities, n = 126; group B, nonextremity, n = 43), traumatic etiology (traumatic, n = 68; 127 nontraumatic, n = 101), and polymicrobial status, defined by culture as at least two distinct 128 pathogenic species recovered from a single biopsy. Sample size was determined by feasibility; 129 consecutive eligible patients were enrolled over three years, and no formal power calculation was 130 done. The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement (see Table S1 in the 131 supplemental material). 132 

Microbiological culture 133 Intraoperative tissue biopsies were inoculated onto sheep blood agar and MacConkey agar (aerobic 134 incubation, 37°C, 24 to 48 h) and anaerobic blood agar in GasPak chambers (48 to 72 h). Bacterial 135 isolates were identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight (MALDI-136 TOF) mass spectrometry (Vitek MS; bioMérieux, France). 137 

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing 138 Genomic DNA was extracted from RNAlater-preserved biopsies using the DNeasy blood and 139 tissue kit (Qiagen, Germany) and quantified by NanoDrop and Qubit fluorometry. A universal 16S 140 rRNA PCR screen (primers DG74/RW01, 379 bp) excluded 34 samples without amplifiable 141 bacterial DNA. The hypervariable V3-V4 region was amplified using primers 357F/805R 142 according to the Illumina 16S metagenomic library preparation protocol and sequenced on a MiSeq 143 instrument (2 × 300 bp). Three sequencing runs were batch corrected using MBECS v1.4.0 in R 144 v4.3.1. Extraction blanks and PCR no-template controls were not included in this sequencing run; 145 this limitation is discussed below. 146

Raw paired-end reads underwent quality assessment with FastQC v0.12.1 (29) and adapter 148 trimming with Trimmomatic v0.39 (20); aggregate quality control was generated with MultiQC 149 (21). Reads were imported into QIIME2 v2023.2 (16) and processed with the DADA2 plugin (17) 150 using truncation lengths of 275 bp (forward) and 260 bp (reverse) determined from per-base 151 quality profiles. DADA2 performed phiX read removal, error-model-based denoising, paired-end 152 merging, and de novo chimera filtering. DADA2 processing yielded 182 ASVs from 3102 raw 153 input sequences; after low-count filtering, 170 genus-level features were retained for downstream 154 analysis. Counts were normalized by total-sum scaling. Taxonomy was assigned using a naive 155 Bayes classifier trained against the SILVA 138 reference database within QIIME2; features were 156 collapsed to genus level for all downstream analyses. The pipeline is summarized in the 157 supplemental material. 158 

Statistical analysis 159 Alpha diversity (Shannon H′, Simpson 1 − D, Chao1 estimated richness, observed amplicon 160 sequence variants [ASVs]) was computed from the rarefied feature table at a rarefaction depth of 161 900 reads per sample. Continuous variables were not assumed to be normally distributed; group 162 differences were tested by the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, with Benjamini-Hochberg false-163 discovery-rate (FDR) correction applied across all multiple comparisons. Where parametric 164 comparison was appropriate (after visual and Shapiro-Wilk assessment), Welch’s t test or one-way 165 analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis 166 dissimilarity and visualized by principal-coordinate analysis (PCoA); group differences were 167 tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations. 168 Homogeneity of multivariate group dispersions was tested by PERMDISP (30) on the first five 169 PCoA axes (18.5% cumulative variance) with 999 permutations. Differential genus abundance 170 between polymicrobial and monomicrobial communities was assessed by linear discriminant 171 analysis effect size LEfSe; linear discriminant analysis with a LDA score of ≥2.0 used as the 172 primary discriminant criterion. Co-occurrence networks were inferred from genus-level relative 173 abundances using Spearman rank correlation with SparCC permutations ( n = 100); only edges with 174 absolute correlation ≥0.3 and adjusted P ≤ 0.05 were retained. Culture-metagenomics agreement 175 was quantified by both one-directional sensitivity (true positives/[true positives + false negatives]) 176 and chance-corrected Cohen’s κ with Landis-Koch interpretation bands; the Matthews correlation 177 coefficient was computed as a complementary, class-imbalance-robust measure (see the 178

qPCR validation 183 Targeted SYBR Green qPCR was performed for A. baumannii (omp gene; all 169 samples) and 184 for S. pyogenes (M1 protein; 139 samples with sufficient residual DNA). Primer sequences are 185 listed in the supplemental material. Primer sequences and confirmed producte sizes are listed in 186 Table S1 in the supplemental materials. Reactions (10 μL) contained 5 μL of 2× SYBR master 187 mix, 0.5 μM each primer, and 2 μL of template DNA. Cycling conditions were 95°C for 3 min, 188 followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 54°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s. Specificity was confirmed 189 by melt-curve analysis from 65°C to 95°C. A CT value of ≤35 was considered positive. 190 

RESULTS 191 

Patient cohort 192 A total of 169 patients (138 [81.7%] male) were enrolled between 2021 and 2024; the mean age 193 was 42.8 (standard deviation [SD], 16.2) years. Diabetes mellitus was the dominant comorbidity 194 (52.1%). Group A (extremity infections) comprised 126 patients, and group B (nonextremity) 195 comprised 43. A traumatic etiology was identified in 68 patients (40.2%). Culture classified 98 196 infections (58.0%) as polymicrobial, 65 (38.5%) as monomicrobial, and 6 (3.6%) as sterile; of the 197 98 polymicrobial cases, 97 yielded amplicon sequencing data (1 sample failed postfilter read-depth 198 thresholds). Of the 98 culture-classified polymicrobial cases, 97 yielded sequencing data passing 199 post-filter depth thresholds; the 6 sterile samples and remaining sequencing failures were excluded 200 from the beta-diversity analyses (PERMANOVA n=163). Necrotizing fasciitis accounted for 201 55.0% of presentations. In-hospital mortality occurred in 28 of 147 patients with documented 202 outcomes (19.05%; overall cohort mortality, 16.6%). Mortality outcomes were unavailable for 22 203 patients (13.0%) who were transferred or left against medical advice before discharge. 204

16S metagenomics: taxonomic overview and core microbiome 213 Of the 169 enrolled patients, 167 yielded sequencing libraries that passed initial quality and 214 contamination filters; 2 samples were excluded because of insufficient postfilter read depth. 215 Sequencing returned 977,546 reads in total (mean, 5,818 per sample). After DADA2 denoising, 216 chimera removal, and low-count filtering, 170 genus-level features were retained for downstream 217 analysis. Acinetobacter was the dominant classified genus (mean relative abundance, 15.18%; 218 sample prevalence, 49.1%), followed by Escherichia (7.44%; 31.7%), Streptococcus (6.74%; 219 30.5%), Proteus (5.86%; 19.2%), Klebsiella (3.53%; 18.0%), Bacteroides (3.25%; 24.6%), and 220 

Staphylococcus (2.32%; 26.9%). Unclassified taxa comprised 20.3% of reads (Fig. 1). Applying a 221 core-microbiome threshold of at least 20% prevalence and at least 0.01% relative abundance, seven 222 genera qualified as core members: Acinetobacter , Escherichia , Streptococcus , Staphylococcus ,223 

Bacteroides , Anaerococcus , and unclassified sequences. Proteus and Klebsiella fell marginally 224 below this threshold (see the supplemental material). 225 

Alpha diversity 226 Shannon diversity ranged from 0 to 4.24 (mean, 2.50 [SD, 0.88]), Simpson from 0 to 0.98 (mean, 227 0.86 [0.16]), and observed ASV richness from 0 to 164 (median, 19 [interquartile range {IQR}, 9 228 to 39]). Polymicrobial NSTIs harbored significantly higher alpha diversity than monomicrobial 229 infections: Shannon (2.59 [0.86] versus 2.33 [0.88]; P = 0.048), observed ASVs (median, 21 versus 230 13.5; P = 0.046), and Chao1 ( P = 0.045). Simpson diversity showed a nonsignificant trend (0.87 231 [0.15] versus 0.83 [0.17]; P = 0.051). Neither anatomic site (Shannon P = 0.243) nor traumatic 232 etiology (Shannon P = 0.391) had a measurable effect on alpha diversity (Table 2; Fig. 2). 233 Benjamini-Hochberg FDR adjustment did not alter these conclusions. 234

Bray-Curtis PCoA axes 1 and 2 explained 6.19% and 4.34% of total variance, respectively 236 (cumulative, 10.53%), consistent with substantial interindividual heterogeneity. PERMANOVA 237 revealed significant compositional divergence between polymicrobial and monomicrobial NSTIs 238 (pseudo-F = 85.55; R2 = 0.511; P = 0.010), with polymicrobial status accounting for 51.1% of 239 explained compositional variance. By contrast, neither anatomic site ( R2 = 0.389; P = 0.876) nor 240 traumatic etiology ( R2 = 0.483; P = 0.626) influenced community composition (Table 3; Fig. 3). 241 The large R 2 values for non-significant comparisons most likely reflect unbalanced group sizes 242 (Group A: n=123 vs Group B: n=44), which can inflate pseudo -F variance partitioning without 243 generating statistical significance. PERMDISP (999 permutations) confirmed homogeneous 244 within-group dispersions for the polymicrobial-versus-monomicrobial comparison ( F = 2.85; P =245 0.081) and the traumatic-versus-nontraumatic comparison ( F = 0.28; P = 0.598), indicating that 246 the significant PERMANOVA result for polymicrobial status reflects a genuine centroid shift 247 rather than dispersion heterogeneity. 248 

Co-occurrence network and differential abundance 249 Genus-level co-occurrence patterns were inferred from 16S abundance data. The resulting network 250 revealed a tightly interconnected cluster of Gram-negative aerobes, Acinetobacter , Escherichia ,251 

Klebsiella , Enterobacter , and Proteus , linked by significant positive correlations across 252 polymicrobial specimens (Fig. 4B). Staphylococcus and Streptococcus exhibited negative 253 associations with this Gram-negative consortium, consistent with their predominance in 254 monomicrobial infections. These patterns are consistent with synergistic interactions among 255 enteric Gram-negative organisms, potentially mediated through proteolytic, hyaluronidase, and 256 biofilm-related virulence determinants. 257 LEfSe analysis (LDA score, ≥2.0) identified genera differentially abundant between groups (Fig. 258 4A; see the supplemental material). Acinetobacter displayed the highest LDA score among 259 polymicrobial-enriched genera (mean relative abundance, 18.1% versus 11.3% in monomicrobial 260 infections), with Proteus , Bacteroides , Escherichia , and Anaerococcus also enriched in 261 polymicrobial disease; Streptococcus and Pseudomonas were enriched in monomicrobial 262 infections. 263

Genus-level agreement between aerobic culture and 16S metagenomics was evaluated by both 265 one-directional sensitivity and chance-corrected Cohen’s κ (Fig. 5C; see the supplemental 266 material). The two metrics diverged substantially for several genera. Escherichia and Klebsiella 267 showed almost perfect agreement (κ = 0.849 and 0.828; sensitivity, 88.9% and 80.0%), and 268 

Proteus showed substantial agreement (κ = 0.708; sensitivity, 71.0%). By contrast, the high one-269 directional concordance for Bacteroides (100.0%) and Acinetobacter (70.2%) corresponded to 270 only fair κ values (0.327 and 0.289) once chance agreement was accounted for. Streptococcus (κ 271 = 0.131) and Enterococcus (κ = 0.023) exhibited essentially no chance-corrected agreement, 272 reflecting frequent metagenomics-positive/culture-negative discordance. Despite the modest κ for 273 

Acinetobacter , quantitative agreement at the load level was strong: qPCR CT values correlated 274 tightly with metagenomic relative abundance (Spearman ρ = −0.92; P < 0.0001; see the 275 supplemental material). 276 qPCR for A. baumannii was positive in 64/169 patients (37.9%), compared with 57/169 (33.7%) 277 by culture and 82/167 (49.1%) by metagenomics (Fig. 5A). For S. pyogenes , qPCR was positive 278 in 22/139 tested patients (15.8%), compared with 8/169 (4.7%) by culture and 51/167 (30.5%) by 279 genus-level metagenomics (Fig. 5B). Thirty samples could not be tested by qPCR for S. pyogenes 280 because of insufficient residual DNA following the Acinetobacter and 16S screening assays. The 281 3-fold qPCR-versus-culture gap and the 6-fold metagenomics-versus-culture gap together indicate 282 substantial underdetection of S. pyogenes by conventional methods in this population, even where 283 the genus contribution to total community burden remains modest. 284 

DISCUSSION 285 This study provides, to our knowledge, one of the first culture-independent characterizations of 286 the NSTI wound microbiome from the Indian subcontinent. The dominant clinical signal is a 287 Gram-negative, polymicrobial paradigm anchored by A. baumannii (33.7% by culture; 49.1% by 288 metagenomics) and E. coli (32.0%), with S. pyogenes contributing only 4.7% of cases. 289 Polymicrobial status, rather than anatomic site or traumatic etiology, was the principal determinant 290 of community composition. The contrast with the Western evidence base is considerable: in the 291 INFECT study, S. pyogenes accounted for 29 of 41 monomicrobial cases (5, 24), and 292 polymicrobial infections were dominated by oral and gastrointestinal anaerobes (5, 12). The Gram-293 negative dominance we observe likely reflects tropical climate and soil contamination facilitating 294

A. baumannii environmental inoculation through traumatic wounds, compounded by nosocomial 295 acquisition during prolonged preoperative hospitalization (9–11, 28). 296 Alpha and beta diversity analyses collectively indicate that polymicrobial NSTIs represent a 297 clinically and microbiologically distinct entity, not a quantitative extension of monomicrobial 298 disease. A nonsignificant PERMDISP test ( F = 2.85; P = 0.081) confirms that the PERMANOVA-299 detected separation reflects a genuine centroid shift in composition rather than heterogeneity of 300 dispersion. Diversity metrics, particularly Shannon entropy, might therefore serve as culture-301 independent surrogates of polymicrobial status, a hypothesis warranting prospective validation. 302 The chance-corrected diagnostic comparison reveals a sharper picture than one-directional 303 concordance alone permits. Escherichia , Klebsiella , and Proteus showed substantial to almost 304 perfect agreement (κ ≥ 0.71), indicating that for these genera, the two modalities are largely 305 interchangeable. By contrast, the apparently high concordance of Bacteroides (100% by 306 sensitivity) and Acinetobacter (70.2% by sensitivity) shrinks to fair agreement once chance is 307 accounted for (κ = 0.327 and 0.289), because metagenomics detects each genus in a large 308 additional set of culture-negative samples. The very low κ for Streptococcus (0.131) and 309 

Enterococcus (0.023) similarly reflects a wide one-way detection gap. This pattern, high sensitivity 310 coupled with low κ, is the molecular signature of fastidious, anaerobic, or nonviable organisms 311 that aerobic culture systematically underrecovers (14, 15). 312 From an empirical-therapy perspective, in settings where A. baumannii dominates, broad-spectrum 313 Gram-negative cover, typically a carbapenem-class agent paired with a glycopeptide for 314 staphylococcal cover, should be prioritized, with antimicrobial stewardship adjustments as cultures 315 finalize (1, 2, 25). The negligible role of S. pyogenes in this cohort indicates that intravenous 316 immunoglobulin therapy is unlikely to yield benefit in this population, consistent with the negative 317 INSTINCT trial (18). The Gram-negative co-occurrence cluster identified in our network analysis 318 aligns with synergistic models in which enteric organisms cooperatively breach fascial planes 319 through proteolytic, hyaluronidase, and biofilm-related virulence factors (22, 23). Because A. 320 

baumannii is a recognized biofilm former and the polymicrobial cluster includes additional 321 biofilm-competent Enterobacterales, biofilm-mediated antimicrobial tolerance is a plausible 322 contributor to high empirical-therapy failure rates in NSTI and merits direct investigation. 323

Strengths of this study include the large prospective cohort ( n = 169), the multimodal diagnostic 324 integration with chance-corrected concordance metrics, and the application of culture-independent 325 methods to Indian NSTI. This study has several limitations. Amplicon-based 16S rRNA 326 sequencing offers genus-level rather than species-level resolution, precluding direct attribution of 327 antimicrobial resistance determinants. The cross-sectional design prevents temporal assessment of 328 microbiome dynamics. The single-center nature, although partially mitigated by a wide geographic 329 catchment area covering Punjab, Haryana, and Himachal Pradesh, might limit generalizability. 330 The absence of shotgun metagenomics precludes functional annotation and resistome profiling. 331 Extraction blanks and PCR no-template controls were not included in this sequencing run, a 332 recognized concern for low-biomass interpretation; near-universal detection of Enterococcus and 333 

Enterobacter should therefore be interpreted with caution. 334 Longitudinal shotgun metagenomics integrated with dual host-pathogen RNA-seq, as pioneered 335 by the INFECT investigators (5), would resolve virulence mechanisms within Gram-negative 336 polymicrobial NSTIs. Quantitative point-of-care detection of Acinetobacter represents a pragmatic 337 translational target for resource-limited settings. Multicenter Indian datasets are needed to confirm 338 geographic generalizability and to inform region-specific empirical antibiotic guidelines. In 339 summary, North Indian NSTIs present a microbial picture that is fundamentally distinct from the 340 Western paradigm, with immediate implications for empirical antimicrobial therapy and for the 341 design of region-specific clinical guidelines. 342

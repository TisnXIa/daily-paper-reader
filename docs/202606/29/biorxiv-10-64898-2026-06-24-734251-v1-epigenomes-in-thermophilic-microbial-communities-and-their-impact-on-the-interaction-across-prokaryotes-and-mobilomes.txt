DNA  chemical modifications , including  methylation , are  widespread  in prokaryotes  and  mobilomes ,21 

including v iruses,  plasmids, and other  extrachromosomal DNAs,  and play  important  roles in  their ecology and 22 

interactions . However,  current knowledge of these modification systems  and their association with 23 

interactions between hosts  and  mobilomes  across  communities , including those  in extreme environments,  is 24 

severely limited . Here, using single -molecule real -time sequencing  and single -cell genome sequencing 25 

technologies , we conducted  a culture -independent ‘metaepigenomic’ analys is of microbial communities  in  hot 26 

spring  biofilms . A total of  248 , 332 , and 465 genomes were constructed from diverse prokaryotes,  viruses,  and 27 

extrachromosom al  circular  DNAs , respectively , from  10  biofilm samples collected from  3 hot  spring sites . In28 

total , 1106 candidate  methylated  motifs and 3280 g enes  ass ociated  with the  restriction –modification  (RM) 29 

system, including  DNA methyltransferases (MTases) , were identified.  In contrast to the varied methylated 30 

motifs, the nucleotide -level modification ratios were consistent with those of a common  Escherichia coli 31 

genome,  and  an environment -dependent epigenomic preference attributed  to  the lack of  C5 -methylcytosine 32 

was observed , as supported by direct measurements of modified bases  by l iquid chromatography –tandem 33 

mass spectrometry . A  systematic survey revealed various defense systems  in the genome , and almost half of 34 

the MTase genes  were  estimated to  be genetically involved in defense mechanisms against extracellular DNA, 35 

such as  RM systems . The  mobilomes and their predicted  host s shared  epigenomic patterns  within each 36 

interactive subnetwork, suggesting that mobilome DNA was modified by host MTase during the current 37 

infection or transfection , rather than serving  as historical  records . Our findings highlight  that DNA 38 

modification  shapes multiple e colog ical and  evolutionary strategies in interactions between prokaryotes and 39 

mobilomes, and that epigenomes serve as a potential signature for  accurate prediction of current  host –phage 40 

interactions .41

Chemical modifications  of DNA are found in diverse prokaryotes , viruses, and eukaryotes. DNA 43 

methylation is a representative DNA modification catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases (MTases) , in  which 44 

S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) provides the methyl group 1. In prokaryotes, three methylation  types  (i.e.,N6 -45 

methyladenine  [m6A ], C5 -methylcytosine  [m5C ], and N4 -methylcytosine  [m4C ]) have been investigated in 46 

detail 2. DNA methylation plays  a role in  regulating gene expression and DNA mismatch repair 3–5. These 47 

systems perform various physiological functions , including asymmetric cell division 6,7 , ultraviolet (UV) 48 

tolerance 8, motility 9, virulence 10 –12 , and adaptation to environmental changes under epigenetic control 13 . DNA 49 

methylation also facilitates cell protection from extracellular DNA (e.g., viral infection and plasmid 50 

transfection), known as  restriction –modification (RM) system s14,15 . The RM systems are classified into four 51 

types based on  their subunit compositions and cofactor requirements.  Types  I, II, and III are composed of both 52 

MTase  and  restriction endonuclease s (REase)  and specify non -methylated DNA  in most cases , whereas Type 53 

IV consists of only  RE ase  and specifies modified DNA substrates 16 . Some viruses possess MTases and modify 54 

their genomic DNA to escape the host Type I, II, and III RM systems. In contrast, Type IV systems have 55 

evolved to counter the viral anti -RM system, resulting in a co -evolutionary phage –host arms race 2,17 .56 

Moreover, evidence of  the  horizontal gene transfer  of RM system  gene s within and between domains and 57 

changes in MTase  motif  specificity  have been frequently noted in the evolution of prokaryotes 18,19 . RM 58 

systems encoded on plasmid s also  facilitate  host  defense  against  phage infection,  in  association  with 59 

methylation -mediated  transcription regulation  and their copy number 20,21 , and the RM systems play  a co -60 

evolutionary  interaction between host and p lasmids 22 . There has been  increasing  interest in exploring the 61 

various epigenomic systems amongst diverse prokaryotes and  mobile genetic elements , or  ‘mobilomes ’ (e.g., 62 

viruses  and  extrachromosomal genomes such as  plasmids ) owing to their importance in microbial physiology, 63 

genetics, evolution, and disease pathogenicity 23 –25 . However, most studies  have rel ied on a small number of 64 

culturable prokaryotic strains ; whereas , the majority of microbes  remain uncultured , and the epigenomes in 65 

mobilomes have been  overlooked overall . This small sample size  skews our  understanding of microbial 66 

epigenomics, particularly regarding  its diversity, distribution, and  effect on  biological interactions .67 

Recent technological advances have led to the development of single -molecule real -time (SMRT) 68 

sequencing technology as a useful method for detecting  DNA modifications. Its implementation  on PacBio 69 

sequencing platforms has yielded an array of DNA modifications among prokaryotic 26 –31  and viral strains 32,33 .70 

Based on this innovative technique,  a culture -independent shotgun metagenomic and epigenomic analytical 71 

approach  referred to as  metaepigenomics  has  been developed  (Figure 1) . Using this methodology, community -72 

level epigenomic analyses  ha ve been conducted on several environmental samples (e.g.,  freshwater  and 73 

seawater) , demonstrating diverse DNA modifications in prokaryotes 34 –37  and viruses 34,38  that may be 74 

associated with microbial ecology and evolution .75 

Here, we conducted  a meta -epigenomic analysis of hot spring microbial communities  to reveal  the 76 

epigenom es  of  diverse  thermophilic  prokaryotes and  mobilomes.  Hot spring biofilms establish semi -closed, 77

The  HiFi reads were  used in  a two -step  de novo  assembly  workflow  (Fig ure  S2). First, the reads 129

Community structure and  MTase gene  distribution  in hot spring  biofilm communities 252 

Ten  biofilm samples  were collected from  three hot springs (Nakabusa, Shiriyaki, and  Dewanoyu ) in 253 

Japan  (Figure S 1; Table S 1).  The three hot springs are geographically distant and  ha ve different water 254 

chemical characteristics and temperature (51 –86  ℃) (Note S1 ; Table S2) . For each sample, PacBio Sequel  II 255 

produced 0. 44 –3.0  million ( 5.5 –31.1  Gb) HiFi reads with >  99% accuracy , with the average length rang ing 256 

from  7667 –12934  bp ( Figure S 3; Table S 3). The HiFi reads were estimated to cove r 90 –97 % of  the 257 

community diversity per sample, indicating  that  the sequencing data covered almost  the entire  metagenome 258 

(Figure S 4). The taxonomic assignment of the HiFi reads  showed that  the p rokaryotic community structures 259 

differed markedly  between samples  (Figure s 2A –C; Note S2) . In  samples from  Nakabusa hot spring,  where 260 

living  thermophilic  microbes have been studied for decades, the community composition  was consistent with 261 

that  of  previous studies  based on 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing 40,72 , suggesting  low  temporal  fluctuations in 262

In addition to the defense system, methylation systems may play  a role  in gene regulation  and DNA 562 

repair . During DNA replication in  E. coli , DNA methylation functions as a marker of the original (parental) 563 

DNA strand and dictates mismatch repair on newly synthesized (daughter) unmethylated strands, a process 564 

known as methyl -directed mismatch repair 4,5 . High  temperatures can compromise DNA stability; therefore, 565 

rapid and accurate DNA repair is crucial for survival in hot spring  environments . In addition, because many 566 

phototrophic microbes  are  present  in hot spring  biofilms 72,89 , UV radiation  may  affect microb ial DNA , similar 567 

to  that in  other aquatic environments.  It is anticipated that  DNA methylation may play a role in DNA repair, 568 

helping organisms adapt to  thermal  aquatic environments . However, further experimental and proteomic 569 

analyses are required to confirm the epigenetic regulation of the genes involved.  In addition, we cannot 570 

exclude the  possibility that methylation facilitates physicochemical  DNA stability in high -temperature 571 

environments, as DNA methylation has been reported to increase  the  DNA melting temperature 90,91 .572 

Notably,  hot spring biofilm microbes overall lacked m5C modification s in their genomes, as 573 

supported by MTase gene annotation  (Fig ure  2F ), epigenomic analysis  (Data S2 ), and  nucleoside  analysis 574 

with LC -MS /MS  (Fig ure  S8). Consistent with our findings, m5C modification has been reported to be rarely 575 

observed in thermophilic bacterial  isolates 92 . This trend is  in sharp contrast to  previously analyzed eukaryotes 576 

and many prokaryotes , including  E. coli  and marine metagenomes 34,93 , suggesting  the universality of the m5C 577 

modification system through a wide range of  environmental microbes , except  for  thermophiles . We 578 

anticipated that  high  temperatures would affect the thermostability of the m5C -type MTase or the m5C base, 579 

in which methyl residues are deaminated to thymine residues  by heat, rendering them unusable  in 580 

thermophiles .581

Challenges  of experiments and bioinformatics in environmental  metaepigenomics 583 

Experimental validation is a crucial  step in linking  modified motifs  identified by epigenomic 584 

analyses to modification systems identified by metagenomic analyses , and in determining each modification 585 

system  and  its  specificity.  For MTases, conventional  in -vivo  methodologies rely on heterogeneous protein 586 

expression in  E. coli  and methylation -sensitive restriction assays, which suffer from cytotoxicity, low 587 

throughput, and limited assay conditions; whereas,  in -vitro  enzym e assays based on protein  purifi cation 588 

require substantial effort and cost . An  in -vitro  one -pot  assay using a  reconstituted  cell -free expression system 589 

that  we previously developed  enables rapid high -throughput screening , and is therefore  an ideal  approach  for 590 

evaluat ing  community -wide  MTase genes 94 . However,  no detectable MTase activities have been 591 

experimentally confirmed using either  in -vitro  or  in -vivo  approaches for multiple promising MTase genes and 592 

motifs identified from hot  spring communities, indicating  technical difficulties associated  with  thermophilic 593 

MTases  (Methods S1 ; Note S4 ). Methodological  improvements in the assay would  bridge the gap  between 594 

epigenomic  phenomena and the molecular systems surrounding  DNA  modification , and thus be key to 595 

elucidating these  systems in nature, including in  extreme environments. 596

Long -read metagenomics  is  currently  gaining  increasing  attention. Indeed, as  in  th e present  study, 597 

many studies  have reported MAGs with higher connectivity and quality  using metagenomic long reads  across 598 

diverse environments, which are  generally  difficult  to obtain  using conventional short -read sequencing 599 

technologies alone . However, there  remains considerable room for improvement in both experimental 600 

methodology and  data analysis. In this study , we encountered widely used binning tools, all of which were 601 

designed for short  read s, making them difficult to apply to  our long HiFi read dataset. This was  most likely 602 

due to differences in read length and contig connectivity driven by  the comparatively low  taxonomic  diversity 603 

in  extreme  thermophilic environments and a rich set of sequencing reads, distinguishing it from typical 604 

metagenomic  study settings. In addition, we  encountered lineages with multiple  short -repeat regions in their 605 

genomes,  shaping  significant heterogeneity within clonal populations and making it difficult to obtain 606 

representative assembled contigs using only long reads  (Note S3 ; Figure  S5 ). W e further realized that 607 

multipartite genomes  comprising multiple chromosomes in a single  organism  were difficult to bin into a single 608 

MAG  using only long reads despite the reconstructed contigs being circular  (Note S3 ). T hese issues  may  not 609 

be captured in conventional short -read -based metagenomic analyses, which generate shorter, unconnected 610 

contigs, highlighting  the specific challenges  of long -read metagenomics in low -biodiversity samples . L ineages 611 

whose genomes are difficult to reconstruct using only long reads, yet for which it is possible to obtain highly 612 

fragmented  MAGs with short reads,  may be  widespread in nature and could be problematic in the  era of long -613 

read metagenomics. 614 

Current SMRT sequencing technology  supports a  limited number of DNA modifications with 615 

sufficient reliability (i.e., m4C and m6A), although several  DNA  modifications occur in nature 2. In addition to 616 

DNA modification s, RNA modifications  have also received  attention.  Similar to dsDNA viruses and 617 

prokaryotes, DNA/RNA modifications are believed to play significant roles in ssDNA  and RNA viruses .618 

Nanopore sequencing technology  is a potential approach for detecting  both  DNA /RNA  modifications 95,96 ,619 

allowing  broad modifications to be  measure d beyond  limited methylations,  as well as  to  examine  the 620 

epigenomes of  dsDNA and RNA  viruses. However, because  the sensitivity of  DNA  modification  detecti on  is 621 

currently much  lower than that of  PacBio platforms 97 , f urther  improvement s are needed.  It would be 622 

interesting to investigate the epigenomes of these overlooked microbes as well as  the  community -level 623 

diversity  in  a wide  range of  environments  using a  metaepigenomic approach. 624

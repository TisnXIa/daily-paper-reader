Abstract 6

Animals harbor diverse gut microorganisms that influence host health and fitness. Synthetic 7

microbial communities have been instrumental in enabling reductionist studies of host -8

microbiome interactions, but some questions require microbial communities with more natural -9

like complexity while preserving experimental tractability, in vivo monitoring, and quantitative 10 

analysis. Here, we describe a method optimized for longitudinal studies of host -microbiome -11 

environment interactions in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans . In this approach, complex 12 

microbial extracts (CMEs) are generated from environmental samples and applied to worm culture 13 

plates, providing a diverse yet experimentally convenient microbial environment. We show that 14 

CME composition remains stable during cold storage, enabling reproducible longitudinal 15 

experiments while minimizing confounding environmental drift over time. As a proof of principle, 16 

we apply this method to examine age -dependent changes in the worm gut microbiome , providing 17 

support for previous reports of age -dependent increase in the abundance of gut 18 

Enterobacteriaceae . CMEs provide a practical and reproducible framework that complements 19 

experiments using monocultures or synthetic communities, enabling longitudinal studies of host –20 

microbiome interactions under conditions that better approximate natural microbial complexity. 21 

Keywords. Caenorhabditis elegans, microbiome, synthetic communities, ecology 22 

Introduction 23 

It is now well appreciated that most if not all a nimals harbor complex gut bacterial communities 24 

[1] . Over the past two decades , research ha s highlighted the critical role s of these communities 25 

in animal health, evolution, and ecological interaction s [2 –6] . Simple model organisms such as 26 

Drosophila melanogaster , Zebrafish and Caenorhabditis elegans have emerged as powerful 27 

models for interrogating fundamental aspects of host -microbe interaction s [7 –11] . Their genetic 28 

tractability, short generation times, and well -characterized biology enable experimental 29 

manipulation and mechanistic analysis . In C. elegans , for example, microbiome research has 30

In nature, a nimals are continuously exposed to diverse microbial communities yet maintain 33 

characteristic gut microbiomes that represent only a subset of the surrounding microbial diversity ,34 

acquired through feeding , environmental contact or social interactions . C. elegans , for example, 35 

inhabits microbe -rich niches such as rotting fruit and decomposing plant material [5,17 –20] , where 36 

it encounter s highly complex and dynamic microbial communities , yet maintain a gut microbiome 37 

distinct from its environment [21 –23] .38 

To model host -environment interactions under controlled conditions, microbiome s tudies in C. 39 

elegans employed defined synthetic communities spanning different levels of diversity , including 40 

CeMbio [22] SC20 [24] , and BIGbiome [25] , composed of bacterial gut isolates represent ative of 41 

the worm’s native microbio me . Similar strategies have been applied across a range of host 42 

systems, with host -specific synthetic communities (SynComs) developed for Drosophila [26] ,43 

mosquitos [27] , honeybees (Kešnerová et al. 2017), as well as for vertebrate system s [29] . These 44 

approaches offer experimental control and reproducibility but necessarily simplify the complexity 45 

of natural microbial environments. Conversely , more naturalistic systems such as environmental 46 

microcosms can better capture ecological complexity but but present practical challenges in 47 

sampling, observation, and qua ntificatio n [21,30 –33] . This trade -off is particularly pronounced in 48 

hosts with highly complex microbiomes, where simplified SynComs may inadequately represent 49 

the diversity and functional capacity of native microbial communities. 50 

Here, we describe a complementary approach for microbiome research based on complex 51 

microbial extracts (CMEs), which preserve natural -like microbial diversity while enabling 52 

reproducible sampling, quantification, visualization, and long -term storage suitable for longitudinal 53 

studies. We demonstrate the utility of this method by applying it to characterize age -dependent 54 

changes in the gut microbiome of C. elegans . CMEs therefore provide an experimentally tractable 55 

intermediate between simplified synthetic communities and ecologically complex microcosm 56 

approaches for studying host –microbiota interactions. 57

seeded on plates. 62

Compost microbial extracts (CMEs) 63 

Soil samples were collected from different locations across the Berkeley campus. To enrich and 64 

diversify bacterial communities, collected soils were supplemented with organic matter in the form 65 

of chopped apple, banana peels, or a combination of both and composted at room temperature 66 

for around two weeks, mixing every other day. 67 

Preparation of m icrobial extracts was initiated by mixing 45 -50 grams of compost with 20 -30 mL 68 

M9 buffer in a 50 mL conical tube , followed by vigorous vortexing (Fig . 1A). Soil particles were 69 

then pelleted by a two -minute centrifug ation at 1800 rpm. Lower -density particles (and native 70 

worms, if present in the original soil samples) were further removed by filtering t he supernatant 71 

through a double layer of sterile gauze , and the resulting extract , typically around 20 mL , was 72 

collected in a separate conical tube. This procedure was repeated with more compost until ~ 200 73 

mL of extract were collected. Filtered extract was aliquoted into 1.5 mL microfuge tubes and 74 

centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for two minutes to pellet bacteria . Supernatant was discarded and CME 75 

pellets were combined to double the quantity per tube, air dried for 30 minutes and stored at 4°C 76 

until use . When used in experiments, CME pellets were resuspended in 200 μL M9, plated on 77 

PFM plates, and used as a source of food and commensals for approximately 100 worms, which 78 

were monitored to ensure development to maturity. 79 

Worm aging experiment 80 

Aging experiments were carried out essentially as in [24] . Briefly, synchronized worm populations 81 

were prepared by bleaching gravid worms and releasing eggs that hatched in M9 salt solution to 82 

provide germ -free L1 larvae. L1 worms were raised at 20 °C on plates freshly seeded with CME ,83 

transferred daily to fresh plates to separate the original cohort from their progeny, enabling the 84 

continuation of experiments into what could be considered as middle age .85 

Sampling for microbiome analysis 86 

Samples of CME stock communities kept as aliquots at 4°C, were taken out of storage in 87 

designated time points and either used for DNA extraction or resuspended in M9 and seeded onto 88 

PFM plates from which they were harvested the following day . Samples were collected and kept 89 

at -20 °C until DNA extraction. 90 

Gut microbiomes of aging worm s were analyzed in synchronized worm populations raised on 91 

CME -seeded plates . Worms were washed off with M9 into 15 ml conical tube s and pelleted by 92 

light centrifugation (1800 rpm) on a clinical centrifuge . Following aspiration of supernatant , worm s93 

were transferred onto the center of a sterile 100 mm 2% agar plate, allowing worms to disperse 94

DNA was extracted from worm and CME samples as previously described, using the DNeasy 102

Paired -end sequencing was performed on an in -house Illumina Miniseq platform using the High -107

Results 135 

CME compositional stability 136 

To examine whether stored CME can serve as a stable microbial source for longitudinal 137 

microbiome studies, we analyzed the microbiome composition of a concentrated microbial extract 138 

(CME) prepared with soil composted with apples and banana peels that was stored at 4 oC over a 139 

week (Fig 1A). CME aliquots, resuspended in M9, were used for DNA extraction and 16S analysis 140 

immediately from storage, or used to seed plates, and harvested from plates for DNA extraction 141 

one day later, examining community composition after settling on plate s (Fig 1 B). No apparent 142 

difference was observed in community composition of CME samples over a period of over a week 143 

(Fig. 1 B left) . Analysis of communit y composition on seeded plates may expose more nuanced 144 

changes , as growth on plates (which is minimal on the peptone -free media , but not completely 145 

negligible ) may allow inter -bacterial competition resulting in small changes in the original stock 146 

that are then amplified by competition. Indeed, c ommunities on seeded plates have somewhat 147 

changed during the 24 hours , compared to the original stock , which primarily involved expansion 148 

of Pseudomonadaceae bacteria, similar to previous reports [6] . Yet, community composition in 149 

seeded plates largely remained stable over the eight -day course of CME sampling .150 

In analysis of microbial diversity performed on CME stock samples shown in Fig. 1 B, all alpha 151 

diversity indices showed no significant change during the eight -day storage (Welch t -test, p > 0.05 152 

Fig. 1 C). A s imilar trend was observed in seeded plates (Fig 1D) . Principle component analyses, 153 

assessing beta diversity, highlighted the differences between CME stock samples and their plate -154 

settled version (Fig. 1E and F) , but again show ed no significant changes in community 155 

composition in longitudinally sampled CME stock aliquots, assessed either with Euclidean 156 

distance or with weighted UniFrac distances . While some statistically significant differences were 157 

found among seeded plates communities, this was apparent only with weighted UniFrac distance 158

Using CME s in a longitudinal aging experiment 161 

The stability of CME over a prolonged period of time makes it a useful resource for longitudinal 162 

studies requiring a microbially -diverse yet stable source for host colonization. To test its 163 

usefulness , we used CMEs in an experiment aimed at characterizing the gut microbiome of aging 164 

worms . To this end , we raised worms on plates seeded with CME aliquots , transferring aging 165 

worms every two days away from their progeny to fresh plates with aliquots of the same stored 166 

CME, and sampling both lawns and worms for microbiome analysis .167 

Two independent experiments were performed , each with a separately prepared CME generated 168 

with distinct fruit enrichments. The resulting microbial composition of the two CME s was 169 

accordingly very different (Fig. 2A and B). One was dominated by members of the families 170 

Acetobacteraceae , Alcaligenaceae, Brevibacteriaceae, Moraxellaceae and Microbacteriaceae 171 

(CME -1, Fig. 2A) ; the other, by Alteromonadaceae , Bacillaceae, Crocinitomicaceae , and 172 

Verrucomicrobiaceae (CME -2, Fig. 2A) .173 

Previous work has shown that worms harbor ed a characteristic gut microbiome, distinct from their 174 

microbial environments but similar among worms raised in different environments [10,21,24] . This 175 

trend held true here as well (Fig. 2 C). In addition, microbiome composition across time points 176 

revealed age -associated shifts in the worm gut community (Fig. 2C). Those differed between the 177 

two experiments but shared an age -dependent increase in the abundance of bacteria of the 178 

Enterobacteriaceae family (Fig . 2A) , as observed previously [24] . Moreover, the gut bacterial 179 

diversity declined overall in aging worms (Fig. 2D and E) , consistent with other studies reporting 180 

age -associated reduction in gut microbiome diversity [24,41] .181 

Discussion 182 

Experiments using monocultures and synthetic communities have been invaluable for dissecting 183 

mechanisms of animal -microbe interactions. The CME -based approach described here 184 

complements these reductionist systems by enabling investigation of emergent microbial 185 

community properties within the host over time, including succession, resilience, and competitive 186 

dynamics. 187 

In natural environments , microbial communities are highly dynamic, making it difficult to 188 

disentangle host -driven changes from environmental variation . By maintaining compositional 189

Our m icrobiome analysis in aging worms demonstrated age -related declines in microbial diversity 194 

and temporal changes in composition consistent with previous work [24] , including a recurring 195 

increase in Enterobacteriaceae in older worms . The reproducibility of this pattern across CME -196 

based experiments reinforces its robustness as an aging -associated signature . Notably, the 197 

consistent emergence of Enterobacteriaceae across monocultures, synthetic communities 198 

containing this family, and now naturally complex CME communities suggests that this shift is not 199 

an artifact of experimental simplification but a general feature of the aging C. elegans gut 200 

environment. 201 

While the results described here use CMEs to focus on the host -associated microbiome, two 202 

additional studies utilized CMEs to study eco -evolutionary aspects of the worm gut microbiomes. 203 

In these studies, CMEs were useful to demonstrate that regardless of microbial diversity, stress -204 

resistant C. elegans dauer larvae were largely devoid of gut bacteria , and that worm population 205 

growth can shape their respective microbial environments [6,34] . Together with the present study, 206 

these studies highlight the broad applicability of CME in microbiome research in relevant 207 

ecological contexts .208 

Although our experiments focused on C. elegans , CMEs could be used for other useful nematode 209 

models such as P pacificus [42] or adapted across more distant animal models. CME s could be 210 

applied to modify environment s or diets of other animals such as Drosophila , or in earthworm’s 211 

habitats , where they could contribute to stud ies of processes s uch as nutrient cycling or host -212 

microbio me interactions. CMEs could also serve as natural microbiome “inocul um” for aquatic 213 

systems or animal -housing environments, to expose animal models to ecologically relevant 214 

microbial communities. Overall, CMEs provide a flexible and broadly applicable framework for 215 

studying host -associated microbiomes across biological systems of varying complexity. 216

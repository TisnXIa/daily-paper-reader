20 21 Gut bacteria shape the metabolism of their host and play an important role in human health. 22 However, systems biology approaches to study their intracellular metabolic fluxes are largely 23 underdeveloped. We present an experimental and computational workflow to quantify 24 metabolic flux ratios in gut bacteria using 13 C-labeled nutrient supplementation and a newly 25 developed machine learning-based Flux Ratio Prediction Python PackagE (FRAPPPE). We 26 apply FRAPPPE to investigate central carbon metabolism in two prevalent gut Bacteroidota, 27 Bacteroides uniformis and Phocaeicola vulgatus , in comparison to Escherichia coli . FRAPPPE 28 revealed altered tricarboxylic acid cycle bifurcation in Bacteroidota compared to E. coli under 29 anaerobic conditions. Further, we used FRAPPPE to investigate co-metabolism of 30 nucleosides and carbohydrates by B. uniformis and P. vulgatus . We found distinct species-31 specific patterns in how nucleosides affected growth and were utilized depending on the co-32 fed compound. We quantified co-metabolism and showed that the tested nucleosides were 33 mainly contributing to anabolic metabolism closely related to the specific co-fed nucleoside. 34 Together, these findings provide insights into central and nucleoside metabolism of two gut 35 Bacteroidota, and showcase FRAPPPE as a generalizable workflow to investigate metabolic 36 fluxes in gut bacteria. 37 38 Keywords: 13 C metabolic flux analysis, central carbon metabolism, metabolic modelling, 39 machine learning, gut Bacteroidota, nutrient co-utilization 40 41 42 43 44

245 Introduction 

46 The gut microbiota is a diverse community of microorganisms shaped by metabolic 47 interactions occurring among community members and their host. Such interactions include 48 community members competing for available nutrients and producing metabolic byproducts 49 alongside biomass components and energy. Energy production strategies in the gut 50 microbiota include polysaccharide breakdown, protein digestion (Zeng et al. 2022; Salyers et 51 al. 1977; Yao et al. 2016), and organic acid utilization (Belenguer et al. 2007; Duncan et al. 52 2002), resulting in various fermentation products dominated by organic acids, such as the 53 short-chain fatty acids acetate, propionate, and butyrate (Mukhopadhya and Louis 2025; Koh 54 et al. 2016). Central carbon metabolism (CCM) consisting of glycolysis, pentose phosphate 55 pathway (PPP) and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle provides both energy and essential biomass 56 building blocks, ultimately orchestrating metabolic activities of a bacterial cell. Identifying 57 which metabolic pathways are used to convert substrates to products and how their activity 58 changes upon perturbations is essential to understand, predict and eventually control 59 metabolic interactions within communities and with the host. 60 Metabolic pathway activity is usually expressed as metabolic fluxes, or metabolite turnover 61 rates. Metabolic fluxes cannot be directly measured from metabolite or protein abundance 62 data, and have to be inferred using metabolic modeling approaches (Sauer 2006). 13 C-63 metabolic flux analysis ( 13 C-MFA) (Wiechert 2001) emerged as a key methodology to resolve 64 CCM fluxes. Experimentally, an isotopically labeled carbon source is provided to the culture 65 of interest and the labeling patterns of intracellular metabolites are quantified with mass 66 spectrometry or nuclear magnetic resonance techniques (Long and Antoniewicz 2019). Since 67 carbon atom transitions between individual compounds are reaction-specific, activity of 68 reactions can be inferred from the measured intracellular metabolite labeling patterns. 69 Labeling patterns of neighboring metabolites can be used to estimate flux ratios, e.g. relative 70 contributions of alternative reactions to a metabolite pool, that can be calculated analytically 71 (Fischer and Sauer 2003) or with machine learning workflows (Kogadeeva and Zamboni 2016; 72 Wu et al. 2022). Combined with extracellular metabolite uptake and secretion rates, 73 intracellular metabolite labeling patterns enable the absolute quantification of metabolic fluxes 74 through specific pathways (Rahim et al. 2022; Weitzel et al. 2012; Quek et al. 2009; Foguet 75 et al. 2019). 76 13 C-MFA workflows are well established for bacterial model organisms such as Escherichia 

77 coli and Bacillus subtilis , and have been extensively used to investigate CCM activity across 78 different strains, environmental and genetic perturbations (Gonzalez et al. 2017; Crown et al. 79 2015; He et al. 2014; Okahashi et al. 2017; Rühl et al. 2012; Fischer and Sauer 2005). Their 80 application to non-model organisms, such as gut bacteria, remains challenging. First, it can 81 be challenging to design defined minimal culture media for controlled labelling experiments. 82 Second, the metabolic network structure may be partially unknown, which hampers accurate 83 fitting of the metabolic fluxes based on the labelling data. Previous studies in 84 Bacteroides fragilis and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron using 14 C or 13 C labelled carbon 85 sources indicated that biosynthesis of succinate, propionate and alpha-ketoglutarate (AKG) 86 in these organisms are different from E. coli (Macy et al. 1978; Schofield et al. 2018). 87 Bacteroidota diversity in ability to sustain growth in different minimal media (Tramontano et al. 88 2018) and on different saccharides (Salyers et al. 1977; Feng et al. 2022) motivates 89 development of systematic and generalized workflows to investigate their CCM and peripheral 90 pathway activity across species and conditions.

391 We propose an experimental and computational workflow for 13 C flux ratio analysis tailored to 92 non-model organisms, and demonstrate its applicability by investigating CCM activity in 93 B. uniformis and P. vulgatus under anaerobic conditions, two of the most abundant and 94 prevalent members of the western gut microbiota (Maier et al. 2018). The key features that 95 ensure flexibility and generalizability of our workflow are the focus on flux ratios and the use 96 of machine learning to build flux ratio predictors trained on simulated datasets. Conceptually, 97 this approach is inspired by the MATLAB-based tool SUMOFLUX (Kogadeeva and Zamboni 98 2016), which we expand and implement as an open source Flux Ratio Prediction Python 99 PackagE (FRAPPPE). FRAPPPE also enables flux ratio estimation in cases for which exact 100 knowledge of the network structure may be limited and the experimental measurements may 101 be scarce, while offering diagnostic metrics to assess the prediction robustness. Applied to B. 

102 uniformis and P. vulgatus in comparison to E. coli grown in glucose minimal medium ,

103 FRAPPPE identified differences in the TCA cycle operation that may hint towards different 104 optimization of the TCA cycle. To demonstrate the applicability of FRAPPPE in more complex 105 nutrient conditions, we tested B. uniformis and P. vulgatus ability to co-utilize nucleosides 106 along with glucose and show that while they do not substantially contribute to energy 107 production, they are used to replenish nucleoside intermediates pools. Taken together, 108 labeling experiments in combination with 13 C flux ratio analysis with FRAPPPE offer a 109 generalizable platform for the investigation of metabolic fluxes and their adaptation to 110 changing conditions in non-model organisms. 111 Methods

The FRAPPPE workflow was developed in Python 3.10.18. 285 Metabolic models with reactions and atom transitions were built using the FreeFlux software 286 package (version 0.3.6) (Wu et al. 2023). Model reactions for E. coli and 287 B. uniformis/P. vulgatus models are provided in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6 . For flux 288 sampling, the hopsy package (version 1.6.1) (Paul et al. 2024) was used to formulate a 289 problem in the form of Ax <= b by implementing steady state constraints alongside upper and 290 lower bounds constraints on the fluxes. The problem was constrained using the stoichiometry 291 matrix derived from the FreeFlux model object. The lower bounds and upper bounds for all 292 fluxes were (0, 100), except for glucose uptake (10,10). In simulations for strains grown under 293 anaerobic conditions, an additional constraint of CO 2 uptake (90, 100) was formulated. The 294 initialization point for flux sampling was chosen as the Chebyshev center of the convex 295 polytope constraining the sampling space. By default, a Markov chain was instantiated and 296 100,000 samples were drawn with a thinning parameter of 1,000. After flux sampling, 297 exchange fluxes were constrained by re-sampling of the exchange flux from a uniform 298 distribution between 0 and the net flux value of the reaction for aerobic simulations and the 299 20-fold of the net flux for anaerobic simulations. For the nucleoside cofeeding condition, three 300 distinct datasets were generated via flux sampling to represent different co-utilisation 301 scenarios: (i) 5,000 flux samples (thinning parameter = 1,000) under baseline conditions, 302 wherein nucleoside uptake was set to zero and flux ratio constraints on CCM were applied; 303 (ii) 5,000 flux samples (thinning parameter = 1,000) wherein nucleoside uptake was 304 constrained to the interval [0, 1] and the flux ratios for nucleoside uptake and phosphorylation 305 were discretized into bins; and (iii) 1,000 flux samples (thinning parameter = 1,000) wherein 306 the dephosphorylation reaction flux was fixed at zero and exchange fluxes were resampled 307 independently in the same manner as the default case. Simulated flux ratios were calculated 308 from simulated fluxes according to the equations in Supplementary Table S7 . 361 Establishing a generalized workflow for 13 C-flux ratio analysis in gut bacteria 

362 We first set out to establish a generalized workflow for flux ratio quantification in gut bacteria 363 focusing on two prevalent commensal bacteria, Bacteroides uniformis and Phocaeicola 

364 vulgatus . As the frequently used Varel-Bryant glucose minimal medium contained methionine 365 and cysteine which in theory could serve as additional carbon sources (Varel and Bryant 1974; 366 Bacic and Smith 2008), we first optimized it to support growth without these additives. We 367 replaced methionine with vitamin B 12 , which is required for methionine biosynthesis in some 368 Bacteroidota and thus enables growth in the absence of methionine (Varel and Bryant 1974) 369 and cysteine with DTT and Na 2S2O3, inspired by (Schofield et al. 2018) and (Varel and Bryant 370 1974) respectively. As intracellular metabolism can rewire within seconds to minutes, keeping 371 the time between sampling and extraction as short as possible is important. The sampling part 372 of our workflow is therefore based on a previously established fast filtration protocol for 373 bacterial batch culture to extract intracellular metabolites (Link et al. 2012; Schofield et al. 374 2018), and subsequent measurement of labeling patterns using an untargeted LC-MS/MS 375 workflow (Materials and methods). 376 To ensure that the computational part of the flux ratio analysis workflow is flexible and 377 generally applicable to different bacteria and conditions, we decided to design it based on the 378 surrogate modelling and machine learning workflow proposed in SUMOFLUX (Kogadeeva 379 and Zamboni 2016). We expanded on SUMOFLUX functionalities and implemented our 380 workflow in Python built upon available tools for flux sampling and metabolite labeling pattern 381 simulations, which we called FRAPPPE (Flux Ratio Prediction Python PackagE). The 382 FRAPPPE workflow consists of three main parts ( Figure 1A ): 1) random flux sampling in a 383 given metabolic network using hopsy (Paul et al. 2024); 2) calculation of the mass distribution 384 vectors (MDVs) for intracellular metabolites based on the substrate label using freeflux (Wu 385 et al. 2023); and 3) preparing the dataset and training predictors for flux ratios of interest using 386 random forest regression models from scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al. 2011). To validate our 387 computational workflow, we compared FRAPPPE results step by step with results from 388 SUMOFLUX using simulations and published experimental data for E. coli grown aerobically 389 in glucose minimal medium (Kogadeeva and Zamboni 2016; Fischer and Sauer 2003). In the 390 first step, across 10,000 flux sampling iterations, the distributions of sampled fluxes were in 391 good agreement between the two workflows, with the notable exceptions of CO 2 uptake and 392 release, as well as the reactions pyc (PEP + CO 2 → OAA) and pck (OAA → PEP + CO 2), as they 393 are formulated as two independent reactions in the model and thus constrained differently in 394 SUMOFLUX and hopsy flux sampling steps ( Figure S1 ). In the second step, using both 395 workflows to calculate MDVs from the same set of flux simulations produced MDVs with high 396 agreement (mean absolute delta in MDV relative abundances of 0.0113) ( Figure S2 ). To 397 validate the third step, we first compared the distributions of flux ratios derived from the 398 sampled fluxes used as input to the machine learning training step. The flux ratio distributions 399 in FRAPPPE were less uniform compared to SUMOFLUX distributions, which was improved 400 by binning and subsampling simulation iterations based on the calculated flux ratio before 401 training the regression models. Finally, we built predictors for five flux ratios and applied them 402 to the experimental data for E. coli wild type and seven mutant strains with gene deletions in 403 central carbon metabolism reported in (Fischer and Sauer 2003). 429 B. uniformis and P. vulgatus are among the most abundant and prevalent bacteria of the 430 human gut, yet to our knowledge no previous studies have investigated their central carbon 431 metabolism using 13 C metabolic flux analysis methodologies. We first performed a baseline 432 characterization of B. uniformis and P. vulgatus central carbon metabolism and compared it 433 with E. coli , a species which has been extensively studied with 13 C-MFA (Sauer et al. 1999; 434 Crown et al. 2015; Gonzalez et al. 2017). We cultured all three species in the modified Varel-435 Bryant minimal medium with 10 mM glucose in three different labeling states under strictly 436 anaerobic conditions: 100% [U-13 C], 100% [1-13 C] and a mixture of 50% [U-13 C] and 50% [1-437 13 C] in triplicates. We collected samples after 11 hours for E. coli and 17 hours for B. uniformis 

438 and P. vulgatus in the mid-to-late exponential growth phase ( Figure 2A ), and quantified 439 intracellular metabolite labeling patterns with an untargeted LC-MS/MS workflow. This 440 workflow, on the one hand, offers accurate identification of metabolites present in the 441 compound library (Materials and methods), while on the other hand can identify differences in 442 labeling patterns of metabolites between species and conditions that were not targeted a 443 priori. In total, we detected labeling patterns of 51 metabolites found in all conditions with the 444 workflow described above ( Supplementary Table 4 ). 445 Upon growth on 100% [U-13 C], the fraction of labeled carbon was significantly lower in 446 B. uniformis and P. vulgatus compared to E. coli metabolites (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank 447 test p = 1.15 × 10 -12 and p = 3.61 × 10 -11 , respectively) ( Figure 2B, C ) with an average labeled 448 fraction of 0.635 in B. uniformis , 0.678 in P. vulgatus and 0.791 in E. coli . This effect was less 449 prominent in the 50% [U-13 C] and 50% [1-13 C] condition, and not observed in the 100% [1-13 C] 450 condition ( Figure S3 ), likely due to the fact that the majority of carbons in the network are 451 unlabeled and thus indistinguishable between the input carbon sources. We next quantified 452 the cosine similarity of the labeling patterns between the three different species and observed 453 that metabolite labeling patterns in B. uniformis and P. vulgatus were consistently different to 454 the ones in E. coli (Figure S4 ). Visual inspection of metabolite labeling patterns in the 100% 455 [U-13 C] condition reveals a common pattern. In most E. coli metabolites, the MDV is dominated 456 by a single highly abundant isotopologue. Mapped to the central carbon metabolism network, 457 these metabolites can be separated into metabolites upstream of anaplerosis, which remain 458 fully labeled, and metabolites downstream of anaplerosis, which acquired a single unlabeled 459 carbon, derived from the carbonate buffering system and the CO 2 in the anaerobic 460 atmosphere. In contrast, in most B. uniformis and P. vulgatus CCM metabolites, more than 461 one highly abundant isotopologue is observed. Already in glucose-6-phosphate, while the 462 most abundant isotopologue is the fully labelled one, more than 20% of molecules in the pool 463 contain one unlabeled carbon. In the TCA cycle intermediates, we observe a more even 464 distribution across two labeling states (either with one or with two unlabeled carbons), while 465 one labeling state (with one unlabeled carbon) is prevalent in E. coli (Figure 2D ). The 466 presence of a single unlabeled carbon can be explained through the incorporation of CO 2 via 467 anaplerosis, but the origin of the labeling state with two unlabeled carbons remains unclear.

13 478 Flux ratio estimation with FRAPPPE reveals differences in TCA operation between the 

479 three species 

480 We next applied the FRAPPPE workflow to investigate which differences in CCM fluxes could 481 explain the observed differences in metabolite labeling patterns. CCM networks of E. coli and 482 B. uniformis and P. vulgatus have several structural differences: while the E. coli network 483 contains the Entner-Doudoroff-Pathway, which has not been identified in the two Bacteroidota, 484 the network of B. uniformis and P. vulgatus has two amphibolic reactions pyruvate 485 carboxylase and oxaloacetate decarboxylase absent in E. coli , in addition to 486 phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase and malic enzyme, which are present in all three species. 487 Further, while E. coli converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA via pyruvate formiate lyase under 488 anaerobic conditions, B. uniformis and P. vulgatus have genes annotated as ferredoxin-489 dependent pyruvate oxidoreductase. We therefore constructed two input CCM networks 490 differing in the abovementioned reactions for E. coli and B. uniformis (which is the same for 491 P. vulgatus ) ( Supplementary Tables S5 and S6 ). We used FRAPPPE to simulate flux 492 distributions and calculate the corresponding MDVs for 15 metabolites in the networks 493 (Supplementary Table 8 ) for the three experimental conditions with differently labeled 494 glucose (100% [U-13 C], 100% [1-13 C] and a mixture of 50% [U-13 C] and 50% [1-13 C]). We 495 compared simulated MDV distributions to the experimental measurements and confirmed that 496 the experimentally observed labeling states are better represented when the simulations are 497 performed using the corresponding species-specific model ( Figure 3A, B ). Since we were not 498 able to quantify the labeling pattern of citrate, we used the cis-aconitate (intermediate between 499 citrate and isocitrate) MDV in its place. Using these simulated datasets, we next trained flux 500 ratio predictors for a total of 14 flux ratios across both networks and all three labeling states 501 (Figure 3C, Supplementary Table 7 ). We observed that only in 2 out of 13 flux ratios in the 502 B. uniformis model predictors achieved MAE <= 0.05. In contrast, in 5 out of 12 flux ratios in 503 the E. coli model predictors achieved MAE <= 0.05. For each flux ratio, we selected the best 504 performing predictor based on MAE, and estimated the flux ratio based on the experimental 505 MDVs measured in the corresponding condition ( Figure 3D ). Our predictions based on both 506 the B. uniformis and E. coli models indicate that glucose is largely utilized through the 507 glycolysis pathway in all three species. This prediction is in agreement with previous 508 descriptions of E. coli glucose utilization when cultivated under anaerobic conditions (Sauer 509 et al. 1999; Gonzalez et al. 2017). In the TCA cycle, the predictors highlighted prominent 510 differences between E. coli and the two other species: while AKG is produced from citrate 511 through the oxidative branch of the TCA cycle in E. coli , it is predominantly produced from 512 succinate through the reductive branch in B. uniformis and P. vulgatus (Figure 3D, E ), which 513 is also in agreement with the literature (Allison et al. 1979).

528 Having established the relative flux distribution in CCM upon growth on glucose, we next 529 investigated how B. uniformis and P. vulgatus adapt to the presence of more than a single 530 carbon source. 17 604 Discussion 

605 Here, we present an experimental and computational workflow for 13 C flux ratio analysis that 606 is also suitable for applications to non-model organisms and complex conditions. One of its 607 advantages is the use of an untargeted LC-MS/MS platform to quantify metabolite labelling 608 patterns. While it allows confident identification of metabolites of interest commonly used for 609 13 C-MFA using in-house and public spectra databases (mzCloud), such as CCM intermediates 610 and amino acids, it can provide a list of candidate metabolites which labeling patterns change 611 between conditions, that can be further investigated and included into the modelling workflow. 612 We use nucleoside co-feeding to showcase how the information from the most differentially 613 labelled metabolites detected by untargeted LC-MS/MS could be incorporated into the network 614 to quantify nucleoside biosynthesis and metabolism flux ratios in B. uniformis and P. vulgatus 

615 with FRAPPPE. 616 FRAPPPE’s generalizability is ensured by predicting flux ratios with machine learning instead 617 of fitting fluxes to the experimental data, which offers several advantages. First, it allows 618 flexible definitions of the flux ratios of interest, which can be formulated as any combination of 619 the network fluxes to be used as targets for building machine learning predictors. Second, it 620 allows us to identify which of these ratios could be confidently resolved even with scarce 621 measurements, incomplete network information, or complex media with multiple carbon 622 sources. Third, FRAPPPE provides diagnostic metrics and plots that help to assess the 623 distributions of fluxes and flux ratios in the training dataset, the coverage of the experimentally 624 measured MDVs by the simulated ones, and which flux ratios can be accurately predicted 625 from the available data and experimental setup. We demonstrate these advantages by 626 investigating CCM fluxes and nucleoside co-utilization fluxes in gut bacteria. By adjusting the 627 metabolic network reactions and simulating the uptake of unlabeled carbon from CO 2, we 628 ensure that experimental measurements from B. uniformis and P. vulgatus are represented in 629 the simulated dataset, which was not the case when E. coli -specific model was used for the 630 simulation ( Figure 3A, B ). We further extend the CCM network to include the main parts of 631 nucleoside biosynthesis and metabolism pathways, formulate biosynthesis and metabolism 632 ratios, and assess whether experimental measurements are sufficient to confidently predict 633 these ratios. Adding new reactions to the network however is a challenging task, as one needs 634 to add not only reactions, but carbon transitions as well, which is usually done manually. For 635 E. coli , genome-scale 13 C-MFA models with carbon transitions have been published 636 (Gopalakrishnan and Maranas 2015; He et al. 2016), and carbon transition information across 637 domains of life is provided in databases such as MetAMDB (Starke and Wegner 2022) and 638 MetaCyC (Caspi et al. 2020), offering possibilities for an automated expansion or construction 639 of 13 C-MFA models for other bacteria in the future. Further, with the acceleration of MDV 640 simulations (Stratmann et al. 2025) and the development of high-throughput experimental 641 workflows for 13 C-MFA (Nießer et al. 2022; Gurdo et al. 2023) FRAPPPE can be used both 642 for experimental design to select substrate labels and the most informative metabolites to be 643 measured, and for fast flux ratio analysis across thousands of experimental datapoints, if they 644 become available, since the number of samples does not affect the speed of flux ratio 645 estimation by FRAPPPE predictors. 646 We showcase FRAPPPE by investigating CCM flux ratios in two prominent gut bacteria, 647 B. uniformis and P. vulgatus , in comparison with a well studied model organism E. coli . Using 648 our experimental setup and available measurements, we could estimate two tested flux ratios 649 with very high confidence (MAE<0.05): glycolysis versus PPP and alpha-ketoglutarate from

Anaplerosis 656 as the sole means of carbon assimilation explains the labeling patterns observed in E. coli 

657 (oxaloacetate being largely produced from pyruvate). High anaplerotic flux is also consistent 658 with labelling patterns of oxaloacetate and other TCA cycle intermediates in B. uniformis and 659 P. vulgatus, which have a high fraction of isotopologues with two unlabeled carbons, since 660 pyruvate label shows similar pattern. However, how the pyruvate [M+2] isotopologue is 661 produced could not be resolved with our labelling conditions and measurements. The fact that 662 the growth capacity of multiple species from the gut microbiome is sensitive to bicarbonate 663 concentration (Franke and Deppenmeier 2018) might indicate that CO 2 assimilation 664 contributes to the high levels of [M+2] in pyruvate and TCA intermediates. Since higher 665 fractions of less labeled isotopologues are already observed in glycolysis intermediates, the 666 incorporation of unlabeled carbon could also happen due to high reshuffling in the 667 transketolase reactions in the PPP. However, we cannot exclude that dithiothreitol, which we 668 provide as a reducing agent to our minimal medium, contributes to the total unlabeled carbon 669 pool, although E. coli does not show any signs of assimilation of unlabeled carbon in the same 670 condition. In case the additional unlabeled carbon originates from CO 2, either anaplerotic 671 products are circulated to a greater extent than previously thought, or additional mechanisms 672 through which CO 2 is assimilated are at play. 673 We demonstrated how the FRAPPPE workflow can be extended to more complex nutrient 674 conditions by investigating nucleoside co-utilization with glucose in the two Bacteroidota 675 species. Ribose scavenging systems for the utilization of ribose from nucleosides have been 676 identified across multiple Bacteroidota species (Glowacki et al. 2020), yet the extent to which 677 such riboses contribute to central carbon metabolism in presence of glucose has not been 678 investigated. When provided with equimolar amounts of adenosine and glucose, B. uniformis 

679 and P. vulgatus both showcased a severe reduction in growth. Previous reports show growth 680 delay in both E. coli and Salmonella enterica upon supplementation with adenosine (Kao et 681 al. 2017; Kitzenberg et al. 2022). Whether physiologically relevant concentrations of 682 adenosine have a growth-inhibiting effect on B. uniformis and P. vulgatus still needs to be 683 validated. Interestingly, while adenosine shows a consistent growth inhibitory effect in both 684 species, supplementation of thymidine only inhibits B. uniformis growth. Double thymidine 685 block is a well-established method to synchronise cell cycle in various cell types by inhibiting 686 the synthesis of dCDP (Bostock 1971; Chen and Deng 2018), but whether this exact 687 mechanism is also inhibiting growth of B. uniformis , and why this does not inhibit P. vulgatus 

688 growth, remains to be investigated. Despite providing various alternative potential carbon 689 sources in the form of nucleosides, labeling patterns in downstream TCA cycle metabolites 690 remained largely unaffected, which indicates a clear preference of glucose over ribose from 691 nucleosides. 692 Overall, our work provides a scalable and generalizable workflow for 13 C metabolic flux ratio 693 analysis, and sheds light on the specificities of CCM operation and nucleoside utilization in 694 two prominent gut bacteria, B. uniformis and P. vulgatus .

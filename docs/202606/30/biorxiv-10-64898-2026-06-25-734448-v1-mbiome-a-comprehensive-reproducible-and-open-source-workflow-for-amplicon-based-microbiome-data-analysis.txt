Microbiome analysis has become a pivotal tool in understanding the role of microbiota in human health and 8

disease. However, the lack of standardized workflows, together with the limitations of proprietary software 9

solutions, hampers reproducibility and flexibility. Here, we present Mbiome , an open-source, user-friendly 10 

and automated workflow designed to streamline amplicon-based microbiome analysis. Built upon QIIME2, 11 

Mbiome supports both bacterial (16S rRNA) and fungal (ITS) profiling, and is compatible with raw fastq files 12 

generated by Ion Torrent (IT) and Illumina (IL) sequencing platforms. 13 

The workflow guides users through an interactive setup process via a simple configuration file, enabling 14 

researchers with minimal bioinformatics experience to perform comprehensive analyses without writing 15 

code. Once configured, Mbiome automates major steps including quality control, taxonomic assignment, 16 

α and β-diversity analyses, functional predictions (via q2-metnet ), and customizable visualizations and 17 

statistical analyses. 18 

Mbiome has been validated using real-world datasets from multiple sclerosis research projects, performing 19 

a comparison between different microbiome analysis approaches, including 16S hypervariable region 20 

reconstruction, amplicon-based strategies, and cross-platform sequencing (IT and IL), as well as against 21 

results obtained with Ion Reporter (IR) commercial software. This evaluation demonstrated its versatility 22 

and effectiveness across different sequencing platforms. Moreover, Mbiome provided improved flexibility, 23 

transparency, and taxonomic resolution compared to IR. By combining accessibility, reproducibility, 24 

and cross-platform compatibility, Mbiome lowers the barrier to microbiome data analysis and facilitates 25 

high-quality, standardized workflows in both research and applied settings. Mbiome is publicly available at 26 

https://github.com/MGorostidi/mbiome .27

Microbiome research has emerged in the last decade, uncovering key interactions between microbial communities 31 

and host physiology (Lloyd-Price et al., 2016; iHMP, 2014). Several studies have demonstrated the effect of 32 

microbiota on health and disease, especially regarding gut microbiota, which constitutes the most complex and 33 

extensive microbial ecosystem in the human body (Moles and Otaegui, 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Afzaal et al., 2022) 34 

In this context, high-throughput sequencing technologies have become indispensable for investigating these complex 35 

microbial ecosystems. Advances in sequencing have opened the door to multiple strategies to study microbiota, from 36 

whole-metagenome shotgun sequencing to targeted approaches that focus on specific phylogenetic markers (Knight 37 

et al., 2018; Caporaso et al., 2010). 38 

Among the various sequencing approaches, targeted amplicon sequencing—especially focused on the 16S rRNA 39 

gene for bacteria and archaea, and ITS intergenic regions for fungi—remains the method of choice for taxonomic 40 

profiling of microbial environments. The targeted approach provides multiple advantages, such as reduced 41 

sequencing costs by focusing only on specific regions, enabling robust multiplexing of hundreds of samples in a 42 

single run (quicker analysis), higher sequencing depth per target region resulting in improved taxonomic resolution, 43 

and low-biomass requirement (Liu et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2016). 44 

Regarding amplicon sequencing workflow, this comprises the following key stages (Liu et al., 2020): sample 45 

collection and DNA extraction, amplification of specific phylogenetic marker regions using PCR, preparation of 46 

sequencing libraries and sequencing by high-throughput techniques (typically on platforms like Ion Torrent (IT) or 47 

Illumina (IL)). Here, the bioinformatic processing starts: the resulting raw reads undergo quality control and filtering 48 

to remove low-quality sequences and artifacts; then, reads are either denoised to resolve exact amplicon sequence 49 

variants (ASVs) or clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs), followed by chimera removal to eliminate 50 

sequencing artifacts. Representative sequences are subsequently assigned taxonomic classifications using curated 51 

reference databases. Finally, community structure is explored using diversity analyses ( α and β-diversity), differential 52 

abundance testing, and visualization techniques to interpret ecological patterns within the microbiota. 53 

While laboratory and sequencing protocols have become increasingly streamlined, a persistent challenge remains: 54 

the lack of standardized, transparent, and flexible computational pipelines for microbiome analysis. This situation 55 

leaves researchers uncertain about where to start, which parameters or algorithms to use, and makes it difficult to 56 

reproduce and compare results between studies. Furthermore, previous studies, have already addressed dramatic 57 

differences in the resulting microbial community composition depending on the pipeline followed (Straub et al., 58 

2020). Moreover, the information available related to the analysis usually depends on the sequencing platform 59 

used, habitually IL, leaving almost out of scope the analysis of samples sequenced with other equipment, such as 60 

IT. 61 

Thermo Fisher Scientific provided a commercial software called Ion Reporter (IR) for microbiome analysis of IT 62 

sequencing files. These can be directly chosen from the sequencing equipment to be analyzed with the software. 63 

The software offers a user-friendly interface, however, it functions as a "black box", limiting parameter customization. 64 

The dependence on external technical support, and lack of analytical transparency motivated the development of an 65 

in-house solution. 66 

Open-source platforms like QIIME2 (Bolyen et al., 2019) have emerged as the gold standard in microbiome analysis 67 

due to their modularity and strong community support. Nonetheless, QIIME2’s command-line interface poses a 68 

barrier for researchers without bioinformatics expertise, especially when dealing with complex datasets such as 69 

those generated by the Ion 16S Metagenomics Kit, which pools multiple hypervariable regions in single fastq files. 70 

To address some of these challenges, we developed Mbiome, a comprehensive and modular bioinformatics workflow 71 

for amplicon sequencing analysis built upon QIIME2. Mbiome covers all major steps from initial fastq files through 72 

to publication-ready figures and results tables. It is designed to maximize reproducibility, transparency, and ease 73 

of use, integrating state-of-the-art quality control, denoising, taxonomic classification, diversity estimation, functional 74 

predictions, and visualization modules. Around 250 stool samples were used from current projects in our lab to 75 

demonstrate Mbiome ’s utility in real life (Gorostidi-Aicua et al., 2024; Moles et al., 2024; Otaegui-Chivite et al., 76 

2025). In addition, ten stool samples were used to compare the four microbiome analysis pipelines implemented 77 

in Mbiome and to benchmark its performance against an established workflow, the commercially available software 78 

IR. By making the workflow and all scripts fully available, we aim to provide the scientific community with a robust 79 

tool for standardized, reproducible amplicon sequencing analysis, democratizing access to advanced bioinformatics 80 

workflows for the broader research community. 81

# Results 82 

Overview of Mbiome 83 

The primary outcome of this project is the development of Mbiome , an internally designed bioinformatics workflow 84 

for amplicon sequencing analysis. This tool has been progressively refined over the past years through extensive 85 

development efforts. 86 

Workflow Components and Analytical Pipelines. Mbiome is a comprehensive and automated workflow built upon 87 

QIIME2. It covers all major steps of microbiome analysis, from raw fastq files to publication-ready figures and 88 

result tables, and is designed to be accessible even to researchers without prior bioinformatics expertise. The 89 

overall structure of Mbiome (v1.0.0) is illustrated in Figures 1 and 2. 90 

As shown in Figure 1, Mbiome integrates multiple internal pipelines. This modular design ensures that each 91 

experiment is processed with parameters tailored to its specific characteristics, including: 92 

• Sequencing platform (e.g., IT or IL) 93 

• Amplification strategy (commercial kit or custom primers) 94 

• Sequencing run configuration (single or multiple runs) 95 

• Targeted analysis type (16S rRNA or ITS) 96 

To facilitate and automate the analysis, users fill a configuration file that contains all the relevant information about 97 

the experiment. This file enables Mbiome to execute the appropriate workflow based on the specified parameters. 98 

Table 1 summarizes the parameters included in this configuration file, which is unique for each experiment. 99 

Once the configuration file, the QIIME2 manifest.tsv (for file import), and the metadata.tsv files (sample-related 100 

information) are created (described in Methods section), the analysis can be initiated. The workflow selected for 101 

each experiment depends on its specific characteristics. As depicted in Figure 1, Mbiome comprises four pipelines 102 

that share the same overall structure, but with subtle yet critical differences: 103 

1. IT Metagenomics Kit :104 

This pipeline processes data generated using the 16S Metagenomic Kit for DNA amplification and the IT 105 

sequencing platform. Because the proprietary primer sequences of this Kit are unknown, primer trimming 106 

(15 bp) is performed during the DADA2 step. After quality control and merging of feature tables across 107 

runs (if applicable), taxonomic classification is conducted. As no splitting by 16S V hypervariable region 108 

(hypervariable regions are described in Figure 14) is possible, only one abundance table is produced, and 109 

the taxonomic analysis follows a region-based approach (no SIDLE reconstruction is available—described in 110 

Methods section). 111 

2. IT Metagenomics Kit (by V region) :112 

This pipeline also processes IT 16S Metagenomic Kit data, but applies the MetagenomicsPP plugin to remove 113 

proprietary primer sequences and reorganize reads by V region (Maki et al., 2023). Subsequent importing, 114 

DADA2 processing, and merging are performed separately for each region (shown in red color in the workflow 115 

diagram). Two taxonomic analysis strategies are available: (i) independent region-based analysis or (ii) global 116 

taxonomic profiling through SIDLE reconstruction. 117 

3. IT known primers :118 

In this pipeline, IT sequencing is used with known commercial primers. Primer removal and splitting by V region 119 

is performed with cutadapt following recommended parameters (Maki et al., 2023). Since forward and reverse 120 

primers generate distinct amplicons, cutadapt and DADA2 are executed separately for each orientation 121 

(shown in green color in the workflow diagram). Depending on whether a single or multiple amplicons were 122 

amplified, the workflow performs either a direct analysis or a split by region/amplicon. Taxonomic profiling can 123 

then be performed either by region or via SIDLE reconstruction. 124

targeting the V3–V4 region. Primer removal is carried out with cutadapt . Although most IL experiments target 126

EXPERIMENT_NAME Name of the experiment folder created in ./mbiome/EXPERIMENTS folder. SEQUENCER Sequencing platform used in the experiment (Options: IT (Ion Torrent) / IL (Illumina)). SEQUENCING_RUNS Number of sequencing runs that the experiment includes (integer). AMPLIFICATION Amplification type performed in the experiment (Options: Primers / Kit). SPLIT_BY_REGION This parameter is for the experiments that used 16S Metagenomic Kit, which includes different regions, for deciding if a splitting of regions wants to be performed. If splitting is not desired or primers were used for amplification, ‘No’ should be selected. (Options: Yes / No). AMPLICONS Name of the amplicons amplified (any name can be given to the amplicon, for example: "amp1" / "V3V4" / "V2,V3,V67"). If the Ion 16S Metagenomic Kit was used this parameter will be empty, since all the Vregions will be analyzed. _fwd_primer Here the Forward primer sequences need to be defined, one per amplicon used. TAXA_ANALYSIS Type of taxonomic characterization to perform: by region, SIDLE reconstruction (described in Methods section) or both (Options: byregion, sidle or both). ANALYSIS_TYPE Analysis type, between bacterial or fungal kingdoms (Options: 16S / ITS). SIDLE_REF_DB SIDLE reconstruction requires a considerable amount of time and resources, so only one database will be selected for performing this step (Options: Silva / GG (GreenGenes)). AMPLICONS_ANALYSIS Once the abundance table is obtained, the analysis of this can be performed. It is possible to analyze specific amplicons or regions individually or collectively by specifying the desired amplicon(s). For example, if the workflow was executed with AMPLICONS="V2,V3,V8" but only the abundance analysis of a single region is required, the parameter can be set to the target region, such as AMPLICONS_ANALYSIS="V2".) TAX_LEVEL_ANALYSIS Taxonomic level in which the analysis needs to be performed (Options go from Kingdom to Genus: Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus). GROUP_METADATA_COLUMN Metadata group column to analyze in the taxonomic abundance analysis (The name entered must match exactly the corresponding column name in the metadata file). FUNC_ANALYSIS If a functional analysis using q2-metnet wants to be performed, this parameter need to be filled with a "Yes" (Options: "" / Yes). METNET_WORKFLOW_TYPE Choose the abundance table in which Metnet funtional analysis needs to be performed (Options: Region / Sidle) (For IT-kit pipelines this parameter won’t be necessary). METNET_WORKFLOW_REGIONS If the workflow type chosen is "Region", but the functional analysis is only necessary for selected regions, specify them (Example: "V2,V3V4,V6") METNET_TAX_LEVEL Select the Taxonomy level to perform the analysis on (Options: k / p / c / o / f / g) METNET_GROUP Metadata column that contain the 2 groups for comparative analysis (Example: "sex"). METNET_CONDITION_GROUP Name of the group that refers to "condition" or disease group. Or, failing that, the name of one of groups. METNET_CONTROL_GROUP Name of the group that refers to "control". Or, failing that, the name of the other group.

Figure 3 A shows the distribution of unclassified reads across samples. These reads correspond to sequences that 137 

could not be assigned to any taxonomic group. Figures 3 B and 3 C present the Shannon diversity index (SDI) and 138 

richness, respectively. The SDI is an α-diversity metric that captures both richness and evenness within sample 139 

types, whereas richness refers only to the number of taxa detected. Together, these indices illustrate intra-group 140 

diversity and facilitate comparisons of microbial variation across metadata-defined categories. Another indicator of 141 

an overview of microbiota current state is the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, shown in Figure 3 D, which 142 

reflects the balance between the two most dominant bacterial phyla in the gut microbiome. This ratio has been 143 

proposed as a proxy for gut dysbiosis, with elevated values associated with metabolic alterations such as obesity 144 

and insulin resistance, while lower values are generally linked to a healthier microbial composition (Ley et al., 2006; 145 

Turnbaugh et al., 2006; Magne et al., 2020). The boxplot illustrates the distribution of F/B ratios across the different 146 

sample groups, revealing both central tendency and variability within each category. Differences in the median and 147 

interquartile range across groups may suggest shifts in the overall community structure that are not captured by 148 

α-diversity metrics alone. 149

A statistical analysis of the taxonomic information can also be performed through Mbiome . Figure 6 illustrates 157 

the differential abundance analysis outputs automatically generated by the pipeline. Figures 6 A and 6 B display 158

Evaluation of Sequencing Platforms and Analytical Pipelines 176 

To assess the accuracy, robustness, and generalization of Mbiome , a comparative analysis involving different 177 

sequencing platforms and computational approaches was performed. This included Mbiome pipeline variants, as 178 

well as the external commercial solution, IR software. The goal was to understand how these factors affect taxonomic 179 

classification accuracy and microbiome diversity metrics. 180 

In order to facilitate the understanding of these results, the nomenclature used for naming each pipeline and a 181 

description of each of them is described in Table 4 (in Methods section). 182 

Total reads obtained per sample differ by method. Figure 8 shows the total reads per samples obtained by each 183 

method. It is noteworthy that when using IL sequencing platform, the total count of reads per sample shows much 184 

higher variation, followed by ITkitNoSplit . The rest of the methods, however, show a remarkably similar total 185 

amount of reads per sample, coincidentally, the three methods that use the splitting by region approach for the 186 

analysis. This might be due to a normalization when performing the reconstruction of regions. 187 

Figure 8. Total read count at genus taxonomic level per sample by method. 

Mbiome achieves superior genus-level classification compared to commercial software. The taxonomic classification 188 

efficiency at the genus level was evaluated. In microbial community profiling, some reads cannot be confidently 189 

assigned down to the genus rank due to limitations in sequence quality, reference databases, or inherent ambiguity. 190 

These are referred to as unclassified reads at the genus level. Figure 9 A shows the proportion of unclassified reads 191 

for each method. Although the commercial IR pipeline does not display unclassified reads in its main output—since 192 

they are stored in a separate file—it ultimately classified the smallest fraction of reads at the genus level, meaning a 193 

larger share of reads remained unresolved at this taxonomic rank. 194 

Figure 9 B compares the total number of unique genus-level taxa detected by each method across varying abundance 195 

thresholds. Methods without region splitting (ITkitNoSplit and IL) identified the greatest number of unique taxa, 196 

followed by the IT-based Mbiome pipelines. The IR method reported the fewest unique taxa, likely reflecting 197 

pre-processing steps that eliminate unclassified taxa and assign reads to the deepest taxonomic rank. 198

analyzed. Different prevalence thresholds were applied, requiring taxa to appear in at least 10%, 20%, or 30% 201

To further explore how these taxa are distributed across methods, Figure 11 illustrates the intersections of shared 204 

genera under the three prevalence thresholds (10%, 20%, and 30%). Across all thresholds, the largest intersection 205 

consistently corresponds to taxa shared by all five pipelines simultaneously, indicating a high degree of consensus 206 

among the methods. The IR workflow, which differs the most in its processing and analytical approach, is the one 207 

most frequently absent from the core consensus group. In contrast, the IL method shows the highest number of 208 

unique taxa. 209 

The taxonomic similarity was also quantified, using the Jaccard index (Figure 12). The three IT Mbiome 210 

pipelines—ITkit, IT, and ITkitNoSplit—exhibited the highest similarity scores across all prevalence thresholds, 211 

reflecting consistency within the software framework despite differences in splitting strategy. In contrast, the 212 

commercial IR pipeline consistently showed the lowest similarity to all other methods, reinforcing its distinct 213 

classification profile. Jaccard scores between non-IR pipelines generally ranged from 0.70 to 0.90, suggesting 214 

broadly comparable genus-level profiles. Comparisons involving IR rarely exceeded 0.50, further highlighting its 215 

divergence from the remaining pipelines. 216 

Genus-level diversity and richness differ across classification methods. To complement taxonomic presence analyses, 217 

ecological diversity metrics at the genus level were compared using a 30% prevalence threshold. This threshold was 218 

selected to balance the inclusion of common taxa while maintaining robustness given the modest sample size (10 219 

samples). Filtered abundance tables were used to focus comparisons on taxa consistently detected across methods. 220 

The SDI and richness were calculated for each method; statistical results are provided in Tables 2 and 3. As shown 221 

in Figure 13 A, SDI values were broadly comparable across the IT-based pipelines (ITkitNoSplit, ITkit, IT) and IL, with 222 

medians ranging approximately from 2.6 to 3.1. IR, however, showed markedly lower SDI values (median ≈ 2.0), 223 

further corroborating its distinct classification profile. ITkitNoSplit also displayed slightly lower SDI compared to the 224 

remaining IT-based methods. 225 

Richness differences were more pronounced (Figure 13 B) consistent with the significant p-values reported in Table 3. 226 

IR exhibited the lowest richness (median ≈ 25 genera), whereas IL showed the highest values (median ≈ 60 genera), 227 

consistent with the broader detection profile observed in the UpSet analysis (Figure 11). 228

# Discussion 229 

The development of an automated workflow for microbiome analysis represents an important step toward 230 

the democratization of bioinformatics, enabling a broader community of researchers and clinicians to perform 231 

reproducible and transparent analyses. By integrating QIIME2 as the core framework, Mbiome combines robustness 232 

and accessibility, eliminating the need for programming or command-line expertise. This effort aligns with the 233 

increasing demand for open, standardized, and reproducible computational tools, particularly as microbiome 234 

research expands into clinical and translational applications (Xia and Sun, 2023; Rojas-Velazquez et al., 2024). 235 

Strengths and contributions of Mbiome 236 

The comparative evaluation demonstrated that analyses conducted with Mbiome achieved more consistent and 237 

robust taxonomic profiles than those obtained using the IR commercial software, which is commonly employed for 238 

Ion Torrent data. Although Mbiome is built upon the QIIME2 framework, its automation of all necessary steps and 239 

parameter optimization enhances usability and reduces human error, while maintaining transparency and flexibility. 240 

The full automation of microbiome analysis—from raw fastq files to final abundance tables, functional predictions, 241 

and visual outputs—greatly facilitates accessibility and interpretability. Moreover, the inclusion of metabolic 242 

pathway inference provides a more comprehensive understanding of microbial communities, highlighting the 243 

microbiome’s functional role beyond taxonomic composition. This systems-level perspective is essential for 244 

elucidating microbiota-driven processes in health and disease (Dakal et al., 2025). 245 

Another key advantage of Mbiome is its flexibility: it supports data from multiple sequencing platforms (Illumina and 246 

Ion Torrent), different molecular targets (16S and ITS), and various primer designs, including kit-specific approaches. 247 

Such versatility makes it suitable for a wide range of research contexts, from clinical studies to environmental 248 

microbiology, while ensuring methodological rigor through optimized parameters for each step (Maki et al., 2023). 249 

Impact of methodological choices on results 250 

Application of Mbiome to real datasets revealed that methodological variations in sample processing and 251 

bioinformatic pipelines can markedly affect the outcomes of microbiome analyses. Even in the absence of direct 252 

biological comparisons, the observed discrepancies in the number of unique taxa or in the percentage of unclassified 253 

reads highlight how methodological inconsistency can lead to divergent interpretations. These findings reinforce the 254 

importance of standardizing workflows, documenting analytical decisions, and ensuring reproducibility—principles 255 

central to the FAIR data movement. Mbiome directly addresses these challenges by providing a transparent, 256 

traceable, and user-guided framework. This aligns with recent efforts to improve reproducibility in microbiome 257 

research through transparent and well-documented pipelines (Rojas-Velazquez et al., 2024). 258 

Transparency and user empowerment 259 

Microbiome studies frequently rely on highly automated “black-box” software, which obscures analytical decisions 260 

and hinders reproducibility. Mbiome overcomes this limitation by combining automation with user control. Users can 261 

visualize quality plots, adjust parameters interactively, and select among integrated methodological options, fostering 262 

a deeper understanding of how analytical choices influence results. This user-centered approach encourages more 263 

critical and informed data interpretation. 264 

Limitations and future perspectives 265 

While Mbiome provides a comprehensive and reproducible solution for amplicon-based microbiome analysis, 266 

the field itself is rapidly evolving. Emerging standards such as the nf-core pipelines—particularly 267 

nf-core/ampliseq —offer modular, scalable, and community-curated workflows built on Nextflow DSL2. These 268 

pipelines share many design principles with Mbiome , including support for multiple sequencing platforms (Illumina, 269 

Ion Torrent, PacBio), diverse molecular targets (16S, ITS, CO1, 18S), and integration with QIIME2 for taxonomic 270 

profiling and diversity analysis (Straub et al., 2020). Future developments of Mbiome will focus on interoperability with 271 

these frameworks, expansion to shotgun metagenomics and metabolomics, and incorporation of machine learning 272 

modules for predictive and integrative analysis (Langer et al., 2025) 273

The workflow encompasses the subsequent steps: 280 

Requirements. In order to run a experiment, it is necessary to prepare three main files ( Mbiome includes a EXAMPLE 281 

FILES folder with all the necessary information): 282 

• samples-manifest.tsv file: as specified by QIIME2, this file is necessary in order to import fastq files. 283 

Depending on the characteristics of the experiment and the previous sample processing, this file will include 284 

different columns. Since samples can be processed in different sequencing runs, a manifest file for each run 285 

will be necessary and these should be renamed as "samples-manifest-runXXX.tsv", being XXX a number from 286 

1 to X (example: if only 1 run was performed, samples-manifest-run1.tsv will be enough, but if 2 sequencing 287 

runs were necessary, samples-manifest-run1.tsv and samples-manifest-run2.tsv will be created). 288 

• samples-metadata.tsv file: this file collects all the metadata information about the samples. Whatever 289 

information can be added, but the format is important (check EXAMPLE FILES folder). A unique file will 290 

contain all the information about all the samples, even if different sequencing runs are present. 291 

• config.env file: the configuration file that contains all the information about the characteristics of the experiment 292 

(already described in Table 1). 293 

Data input. Mbiome accepts demultiplexed fastq files from IT or IL sequencing platforms, either from 16S 294 

Metagenomic Kit or specific primer sequences DNA amplification. Data is imported using samples-manifest.tsv 295 

file, as specified by QIIME2. 296 

Quality control. If primer sequences are known, primer removal is performed using cutadapt (Martin, 2011). For 297 

the "IT known primers" pipeline, the cutadapt trim-single command is applied with parameters optimized for 298 

5’ primer detection (e.g., –p-front with the “^” anchor, as recommended in (Maki et al., 2023)), and reverse 299 

primer trimming is enabled through –p-adapter . Additional options such as –p-indels and –p-error-rate 300 

0.1 are included to improve mismatch tolerance during adapter detection, together with wildcard handling and 301 

untrimmed-read discarding (Qiime2-developers and Gorostidi-Aicua, 2022b). For the "IL paired-end known 302 

primers" pipeline, the same parameters are applied using the cutadapt trim-paired command, which requires 303 

explicit specification of forward and reverse primers ( –p-front-f , –p-front-r ). In the "IT Metagenomics Kit 304 

(byVregion)" pipeline, primer removal and region splitting are conducted using the proprietary MetagenomicsPP 305 

plugin (ThermoFisher), as described in Maki et al. (2023). Finally, in the "IT Metagenomics Kit" pipeline without 306 

primer information, primer sequences are implicitly handled during denoising with DADA2 (explained in Denoising 307 

sequences section). 308 

Denoising sequences. Sequence quality is first assessed using the demux plugin in QIIME2, and reads are 309 

subsequently processed with DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016). This step includes read trimming (when required), error 310 

correction, quality-based truncation, merging of paired-end reads, and chimera removal, resulting in the generation 311 

of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The quality-based truncation step is interactively determined by the user from 312 

the demultiplexed quality plots generated during pipeline execution, following the criteria described in the workflow. 313 

For pipelines with known primers, as well as for the "IT Metagenomics Kit (byVregion)" pipeline, only the 314 

–p-trunc-len parameter is applied, since primer sequences has already been removed. In IL paired-end datasets, 315 

forward and reverse reads are truncated to the same length to ensure compatibility. This truncation step removes 316 

low-quality bases from read tails, ensuring uniform sequence length. 317 

In the "IT Metagenomics Kit" pipeline, an additional trimming of 15 bp is performed with –p-trim-left , following IT 318 

specific recommendations (Qiime2-developers and Jones, 2020; Qiime2-developers and Gorostidi-Aicua, 2020). 319 

When experiments include multiple sequencing runs, DADA2 is executed independently per run, and feature tables 320 

and representative sequences are merged afterwards (Qiime2-developers and Gorostidi-Aicua, 2022a). Prior 321 

to merging, denoising statistics are evaluated by the user to confirm adequate quality; if necessary, truncation 322 

parameters are adjusted and the denoising step is repeated. 323

Taxonomic assignment. Representative sequences were taxonomically classified using the qiime 324 

feature-classifier classify-consensus-vsearch plugin implemented in QIIME2 (Rognes et al., 2016). 325 

For 16S rRNA gene data, either the SILVA (Quast et al., 2012) or Greengenes (DeSantis et al., 2006) reference 326 

databases were employed, while the UNITE database (Nilsson et al., 2018) was used for ITS sequences. 327 

Sequences were aligned against the selected reference database using local similarity searches, and taxonomy 328 

was assigned according to the consensus of the best hits. Unless otherwise specified, default QIIME2 parameters 329 

were applied, including a minimum sequence identity of 80% and a minimum query coverage of 80%, following 330 

recommended guidelines. In cases where multiple hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were sequenced 331 

separately, taxonomic profiles were reconstructed using the SIDLE (SMURF Implementation Done to acceLerate 332 

Efficiency) reconstruction, through q2-sidle plugin (Debelius, 2020). SIDLE is a python version of the Short 333 

MUltiple Reads Framework (SMURF) algorithm (Fuks et al., 2018) with a novel tree-building approach, that allows 334 

the reconstruction of multiple short, fragmented amplicons against a known database to improve the resolution of 335 

the reconstructed community over single amplicons (Debelius, 2020). 336 

Metabolic pathway inference. To infer the metabolic potential of microbial communities from 16S rRNA gene 337 

sequencing data, the q2-metnet plugin was used within the QIIME2 framework (Balzerani et al., 2024). This 338 

tool contextualizes taxonomic profiles into curated metabolic reconstructions, specifically AGORA and AGREDA, 339 

enabling functional interpretation of microbial composition. Based on the relative abundances of taxa, q2-metnet 340 

computes normalized activity scores for metabolic reactions and subsystems. These scores were used to perform 341 

differential activity analyses and exploratory visualizations, including PCA and hierarchical clustering, to identify 342 

functional differences between sample groups. 343 

Abundance table analysis. Microbial diversity was evaluated through both α- and β-diversity metrics. α-diversity 344 

was quantified using the SDI, which incorporates richness and evenness, and by genus richness, defined as the 345 

number of taxa with non-zero abundance per sample. The SDI was computed from relative abundances obtained by 346 

normalizing taxon counts and applying the Shannon entropy formula (SDI and richness are explained in Evaluation 347 

metrics section). β-diversity was assessed using pairwise distance matrices, primarily the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity 348 

metric, which captures compositional differences in community structure between samples. 349 

Statistical analysis. Group comparisons were performed based on data distribution and the number of groups defined 350 

by the dependent variable. Normality within groups was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally 351 

distributed data, comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-sample t-test, while comparisons 352 

involving more than two groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. For non-normally distributed data, the 353 

Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) and the Kruskal–Wallis H test (more than two groups) were applied. Group 354 

sizes and total sample counts were documented for each analysis. To correct for multiple testing, p-values were 355 

adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. 356 

Visualization and reporting. Summary figures and tables were generated in Python using matplotlib and seaborn 357 

for taxonomic barplots, boxplots, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) plots, and comparative analyses. UpSet 358 

diagrams were produced with the upsetplot package. 359 

Reproducibility. The complete workflow is implemented as a Bash and Python pipeline under version control. All 360 

scripts and configuration files are publicly available at https://github.com/MGorostidi/mbiome .361

Taxonomic feature names were systematically standardized by extracting primary taxonomic terms across ranks 385 

(domain to genus) and removing extraneous characters such as underscores, hyphens, and bracketed annotations. 386

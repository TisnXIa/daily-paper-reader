Background: Host sex is a critical determinant of microbial community structure across many 8host species, influenced by hormonal profiles, physiology, and sex-stratified behaviors. Despite 9its importance, sex metadata is frequently missing in microbiome studies, including for animal-10 associated samples. Host chromosomal sex can be inferred from the host-derived reads 11 present in metagenomic data, but existing genomic sex prediction tools rely on fixed coverage 12 thresholds calibrated for human XY chromosomes and require relatively high host reads, limiting 13 their use on low host-biomass samples such as stool and on organisms with other sex-14 determination systems. 15 

Results: Here, we present SCiMS (Sex Calling in Metagenomic Sequences), a bioinformatic 16 tool that leverages host-derived DNA within shotgun metagenomic data to predict host 17 chromosomal sex, even at low host coverage. SCiMS uses a multinomial likelihood computed 18 from observed read counts under each sex and reports chromosomal sex calls. Because the 19 expected read distribution is derived directly from chromosome lengths and ploidy under each 20 candidate karyotype, SCiMS applies to any organism with a heterogametic sex-determination 21 system. We benchmarked SCiMS against existing tools on simulated metagenomic data, 22 human metagenomic samples spanning multiple body sites, and metagenomic samples from 23 seven animal species. SCiMS matched or outperformed existing tools, with its noticeable 24 advantage at low host read conditions. 25 

Conclusions: SCiMS provides an accurate, scalable, and cross-species generalizable solution 26 for host chromosomal sex classification, even when host DNA is minimal. By enabling recovery 27 of missing sex metadata, it serves as a quality-control tool analyses in microbiome research. 28 SCiMS is freely available at http://github.com/davenport-lab/SCiMS. 29

Several genomic approaches infer sex from whole-genome sequencing data by 54 comparing the proportions of host reads that map to the sex chromosomes, or sex 55 chromosomes relative to the autosomes, including BeXY [9], Rx [10], and Ry [11]. BeXY 56 classifies sex-linked scaffolds by modeling differences in chromosomal ploidy (copy number) 57 across samples using sequencing read counts [9]. Rx calculates the average ratio of reads 58 mapping to X chromosome relative to reads mapping to each autosome [10]. Ry calculates the 59 fraction of reads mapping to Y chromosome relative to reads mapping to both X and Y 60 chromosomes [11]. However, these methods were developed primarily for whole-genome 61 applications in humans, and they share two properties that limit their use on host-derived 62 metagenomic reads. First, most rely on fixed coverage ratio thresholds calibrated for the human 63 X and Y chromosomes, which do not necessarily transfer to organisms with different sex 64 chromosome architecture or to ZW sex-determination systems. Second, Rx and Ry in particular 65 require moderately high host read depth to produce confident calls. This poses a challenge for 66 metagenomic samples where host reads can be sparse, particularly in microbe-rich samples 67 such stool where host-derived reads typically comprise only 1% of total sequences [12]. While 68 the feasibility of calling host chromosomal sex from metagenomic reads has been demonstrated 69 [8, 13], no readily usable tools currently exist that support this task across the range of 70 organisms and sequencing conditions encountered in microbiome studies. Additionally, existing 71 methods have not been systematically benchmarked under realistic metagenomic conditions, 72

Together, these limitations motivate the need for methods that reliably infer host 74 chromosomal sex information from metagenomic sequencing data alone, even when host read 75 coverage is minimal. 76 Here we introduce SCiMS, Sex Calling in Metagenomic Sequences, a command-line tool that 77 determines host chromosomal sex directly from shotgun metagenomic data, even when only a 78 few hundred host reads are present. SCiMS evaluates the multinomial likelihood of the 79 observed chromosome read counts under each candidate karyotype and reports a likelihood-80 ratio statistic, from which a chromosomal sex is inferred in organisms with heterogametic sex 81 determination systems (XY or ZW). We benchmark SCiMS against BeXY, Rx, and Ry on 82 simulated metagenomic data spanning a range of host read depths and microbial-to-host read 83 ratios, on human metagenomic samples from multiple body sites and populations, and on 84 metagenomic samples from seven animal species. SCiMS provides output as a simple text file 85 of sex calls that can be incorporated into downstream analyses as covariate or used as a 86 metadata quality-control check. Notably, the host-read removal step that is standard in most 87 metagenomic processing pipelines is itself the natural point at which SCiMS can be applied: 88 host reads identified during this step can be passed to SCiMS to recover host chromosomal sex 89 before they are discarded. SCiMS is therefore useful for researchers processing their own 90 human metagenomic data and for animal-associated metagenomic studies. 91

5Materials and methods 92 SCiMS workflow and sex-inference algorithm 93 SCiMS infers host chromosomal sex by comparing the observed distribution of reads across 94 chromosomes to that would be expected of a male versus a female host. Under each sex 95 hypothesis, the expected fraction of reads on each chromosome is determined by that 96 chromosome’s length and ploidy: a chromosome with two copies contributes twice as much 97 expected read density (i.e. reads mapped per base pair) as one with a single copy. For 98 example, in a female sample (XX), the X chromosome should produce roughly twice the read 99 density of the same chromosome in a male sample (XY), while the Y chromosome should 100 produce no reads under the female hypothesis. SCiMS compares observed counts against 101 these expectations under both hypotheses and reports the more likely sex. Because SCiMS 102 relies on chromosome-wide read density patterns, it should be applied to raw, quality-controlled 103 metagenomic sequencing data prior to host read filtering. Host read removal procedures can 104 distort the relative representation of chromosomes, particularly sex chromosomes, making the 105 remaining reads unsuitable for reliable sex inference. 106 SCiMS computes the expected read distribution across chromosome under each sex 107 hypothesis, assuming reads are distributed in proportion to each chromosome’s effective size, 108 that is, its length multiplied by its ploidy under that sex. Finally, we used SAMtools’ function idxstats to extract the number of reads 204 mapped to each chromosome or scaffold, generating per-sample read count profiles that served 205 as the primary input for SCiMS. 206 Results 207 Overview of SCiMS 208 We developed SCiMS, a command-line tool designed to recover host chromosomal sex directly 209 from metagenomic alignments. Unlike methods relying on fixed thresholds for sexing, SCiMS 210 uses per-chromosome read counts as a multinomial distribution and compares the observed 211 distribution against the ploidy-weighted expectation for each sex. Specifically, for a given 212 chromosome + under sex ;, the expected probability of a read coming from + is proportional to 213 its ploidy 〰〠 (2 for autosomes; 2 or 1 for the homogametic chromosomes; 1 or 0 for the 214 heterogametic chromosome) times its length 〰 , normalized over all chromosomes considered. 215 A likelihood-ratio test across these per-chromosome counts yields the log-odds of male versus 216 female, which is converted to a posterior probability under a uniform prior. To remain robust to 217 the spurious cross-specifies alignments characteristic of metagenomic data, where a single 218 misaligned microbial read against a zero-ploidy chromosome would otherwise force an infinite 219 likelihood ration, SCiMS applies a small mismapping constant <  10 ⡹⡲  to expected probabilities 220 before evaluating the test. Because the model is analytical and parameter free, it generalizes 221 across any heterogametic species (XY or ZW) and any reference genome. 14 As expected, all four methods classified host sex almost perfectly when host reads were 260 abundant (>10,000 reads; Fig. 2a ), and the methods were therefore distinguished primarily by 261 their behavior at low host read depth. Specifically, SCiMS and BeXY were broadly comparable 262 and both substantially exceeded Rx and Ry. Overall accuracy for SCiMS and BeXY rose from 263 0.52 and 0.53 at 150 host reads to 0.97 and 0.99 by 1,000 reads ( Fig. 2a ), whereas Rx and Ry 264 remained considerably lower across the same range. The difference between these two groups 265 of methods reflects their use of the available signal. SCiMS and BeXY evaluate the joint 266 distribution of reads across chromosomes, whereas Rx and Ry rely on a single ratio and abstain 267 when coverage of the relevant chromosome is insufficient. SCiMS and BeXY, nonetheless, 268 differed in a sex-specific manner ( Fig. 2b–c ). SCiMS achieved higher accuracy on female than 269 on male samples at low read depths, while BeXY achieved slightly higher accuracy on male 270 samples. These patterns were reflected in per-class recall ( Fig. 2c ). SCiMS recovered more 271 female (0.89) than male (0.77), consistent with a tendency of abstain when the heterogametic 272 chromosome is sparsely covered, whereas BeXY showed the opposite, with marginally higher 273 male (0.82) than female (0.80) recall. Rx recovered relatively few samples of either sex (male 274 recall 0.55, female recall 0.62). Ry exhibited the most noticeable asymmetry, with perfect female 275 recall (1.00) but low male recall (0.26). This behavior follows directly from its decision rule, 276 which treats the absence of Y-mapped reads as evidence of female sex, which recover females 277 reliably but misclassifies males as females when host reads, and thus Y-mapped reads, are 278 scarce. 279

By evaluating 464 the multinomial likelihood of the observed host read counts under each candidate karyotype, 465 SCiMS generalizes across heterogametic sex-determination systems (XY or ZW). 28 Finally, SCiMS can also serve as a valuable quality control (QC) step in metagenomic analyses 493 even when sex information has been collected, identifying potential sample swaps or metadata 494 inconsistencies. 495 However, SCiMS has some limitations and areas that could be further developed or 496 benchmarked. Its accuracy depends on the presence of reads mapping to host sex 497 chromosomes. While it shows high performance at low host read depths, it will still report 498 inconclusive sex calls for samples with extremely low read depths or where host sex 499 chromosome reads are absent. We benchmarked SCiMS performance using a robust posterior 500 probability threshold of 0.95, which was selected to balance precision and recall. However, 501 users wishing for even higher precision have the option to increase that threshold. Additionally, 502 since sex resolution requires sufficient sex-chromosome signal, in the bacteria-rich samples, 503 such as stool, a confident chromosomal sex call could still be unreliable when too few reads 504 mapped to the sex chromosomes. Accuracy in these samples was lower for all methods. Next, 505 while we benchmarked SCiMS on a diverse set of host species, including several cases where 506 only a closely related reference genome was available, performance varied among taxa. SCiMS 507 relies on accurate representation of chromosome structure within the reference genome, and its 508 performance may decline when the reference is highly divergent from the organism being 509 analyzed. For optimal performance, users should benchmark SCiMS on their focal organism 510 and reference genome whenever possible. This consideration may be particularly relevant for 511 applications involving ancient DNA or highly divergent populations, where structural variation 512 and long-term evolutionary change may result in chromosome architectures that differ 513 substantially from those represented in modern reference assemblies. Similarly, SCiMS may have limitations in accurately 526 identifying sex in individuals with sex chromosome mosaicism, where two or more genetically 527 distinct cell populations are present within the same individual. Finally, SCiMS may not 528 recapitulate the reported sex of individuals whose sex-related biological characteristics do not 529 align with binary definitions of male or female.

Abstract 

Biofilms on aquaculture infrastructure harbor diverse microbial communities that may influence water quality and 

fish health, yet the temporal dynamics of these communities remain poorly characterized. Here, we used 16S rRNA 

gene amplicon sequencing to pro file biofilm communities on concrete raceway surfaces across an 80 -day rainbow 

trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) indoor hatch -house production period . One hundred twenty -three wall swab samples 

from 19 raceways at six time point s (9, 23, 38, 53, 65, and 80 days) were analyzed after stringent quality control. 

Beta diversity analyses revealed that biofilm communities at each time point were significantly distinct 

(PERMANOVA, p < 0.001 for all pairwise comparisons), with early communities exhibiting greater variability than 

late -stage biofilms. Total bacterial load increased approximately 2.5 -fold from early to late stages (qPCR, p < 

0.0 01). Differential abundance testing (ANCOM -BC) identified 57 differentially abundant genera between early -

and late -stage biofilms, and random forest classification distinguished early from late communities with over 93% 

test accuracy. A clear su ccessional trajectory emerged: early biofilms were dominated by pioneer taxa including 

Pseudomonas, Caulobacter, and Flavobacterium ; mid -succession communities featured predatory Bdellovibrio and 

the methylotroph Methylotenera ; and mature biofilms were enriched in saprophytic Saprospiraceae and 

Haliscomenobacter , polysaccharide -degrading Verrucomicrobiaceae, and cooperative predatory myxobacteria. 

Flavobacterium columnare , a pathogen of concern in aquaculture, was detected at low levels throughout the 

produ ction period . These results demonstrate predictable ecological succession in freshwater built environment 

biofilms and provide a foundation for understanding the role of surface -associated microbial communities in 

hatchery management.

The colonization of a surface by microbes begins the process of community assembly and biofilm 2

formation. This initial attachment phase sees microbial members from the seeding community adhere, 3

proliferate, and manipulate the local microenvironment through the production and secretion of 4

extracellular polymeric substance s (EPS) , nucleic acids, primary and secondary metabolites, and waste 5

products (1 –4) . These so -called founder species play an important role in influencing the maturing 6

develop, new constituents enter the community, further increasing diversity while often displacing or 8

begins to shed constituents leading to dispersal of member cells to other environments (2, 8) .10 

This process has been studied in vitro through the use of confocal microscopy and flow cells which allow 11 

for real time monitoring of biofilm development (9, 10) . Additionally, in vivo profiling of microbial 12 

community assembly on surfaces in a variety of environments including marine (11 –13) , freshwater (14, 13 

15) , sewage systems (16) , and others (17, 18) have displayed the interesting variability and , often ,14 

unpredictability of microbial community assemblages. These biofilm communities present serious 15 

problems and provide interesting solutions as they relate to the processes of biofouling and 16 

bioremediation (19, 20) . Biofilms also act as reservoirs for pathogenic microorganisms (21 –24) and have 17 

been shown to facilitate the spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) (25 –27) , highlighting the 18 

importance of aquatic biofilms in human healthcare and thus for One Health (28, 29) .19 

In aquaculture systems, biofilms forming on infrastructure surfaces have received growing attention for 20 

their roles in water quality maintenance and disease ecology. In recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS), 21 

biofilter biofilms are essential for nitrification and ammonia removal, and their microbial community 22 

composition has been characterized across a range of production systems (30, 31) . Beyond biofilters, 23 

biofilms colonizing rearing infrastructure (tank walls, screens, pipes, and other surfaces in direct contact 24 

with fish ) harbor diverse bacterial communities that can include opportunistic fish pathogens (32, 33) .25 

An initial multi -year survey of microbial communities at the facility examined in the present study 26 

characterized biofilm and planktonic assemblages across surface types and environmental conditions, 27 

and demonstrated that the fish pathogen Flavobacterium columnare resides within abiotic surface 28 

biofilms and is seeded from the natural water source (34) . However, most studies of aquaculture -29 

associated biofilms have characterized communities at single time point s or across broad temporal 30

period (the period beginning when water and fish fry are added to a raceway and ending once the 32

maturation , remain largely unexplored. Understanding these dynamics is important because biofilm 34 

management decisions such as cleaning frequency and chemical treatment regimens are currently made 35 

without knowledge of how biofilm communities develop over time or at what stage they might become 36 

problematic or potentially beneficial. 37 

In the present study, we sought to characterize the successional dynamics of biofilm communities on 38 

built surfaces within a freshwater aquaculture environment . Wall swabs were collected from 19 39 

individual rainbow trout ( Oncorhynchus mykiss ) raceways within an indoor hatchery facility, the same 40 

facility described in an initial characterization of hatchery surface communities (34) . Biofilms on these 41

production period , that occurs prior to the transfer to outdoor raceways for grow out . Through the use 43 

of 16S rRNA gene sequencing, we probed the shifting compositional structure of these surface biofilms 44 

over time and examined the potential for biofilm community development to impact the abundance of a 45 

known fish pathogen, Flavobacterium columnare .46

Sampling of Surfaces 60 

Three surface types were sampled within each raceway: the side walls, the tailscreens, and the baffles. 61 

The side walls were the primary focus of this study because they represent the largest continuous 62 

submerged surface area within a raceway, are of uniform material (epoxy -coated concrete), and are 63 

present in a directly comparable form across all raceways, providing a standardized substrate for 64 

comparing biofilm succession between raceways. The tailscreens and baffles, which present smaller and 65 

structurally distinct surfaces, were also sampled and are reported for comparison in the Supplementary 66 

Material (Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). The raceway floor was not sampled, because settleable solids 67 

and feces accumulate on the bottom; swabbing there would have captured trapped particulate material 68 

and settled planktonic cells rather than the surface -attached biofilm that was the focus of this study. 69 

Hatchery surfaces were swabbed as previously described (34) , with each surface swabbed until visible 70 

biofilm discoloration was removed rather than over a fixed surface area . Within each raceway, the wall 71 

was swabbed at two positions along its length, one within the first third and one within the final third of 72 

the raceway. At each position, a vertical series of swabs was collected at successiv e depths, from the air -73 

water interface to the base of the wall, with sampling depth recorded in swab -length increments below 74 

the waterline . All swabs were placed on ice following sampling and then frozen at -20 °C for storage and 75 

shipped on dry ice .76

Discussion 296 

This study examined 19 rainbow trout aquaculture raceways at 6 varying time point s over an initial 80 -297 

day hatchery production period where fish fry are grown up and then eventually transferred to outdoor 298 

raceway structures for grow out . The asynchronous nature of the raceway production period allowed 299 

our group to perform extensive sampling on a single trip and still capture a temporal picture of biofilm 300 

development on surfaces within these raceways. The picture we captured was one of discrete, distinct 301 

biofilm communities that change in composition over a matter of weeks in a predictable fashion. 302 

All beta diversity metrics compared across the six time point s sampled showed significant differences 303 

when looking at the factor of biofilm maturity overall as well as for all pairwise comparisons between 304 

the six time point s. This is a strong signal that deterministic succession is occurring, with discrete time 305 

point s mere weeks apart still yielding significantly different microbial community structures. When 306 

analyzing overall variance of these communities via beta dispersion analysis, early communities were 307

Shannon and Faith PD values. This points to two thing s, one being that colonization of surfaces by all 313

hatchery environment. Early founder species like Pseudomonas , Caulobacter , and Flavobacterium arrive, 394 

modify the surface environment, fade, and establish habitat and metabolic niches for late -stage species 395 

like the Saprospiraceae including Haliscomenobacter . Notably, these early -stage pioneer taxa (396

like Rhizorhapis (nitrogen fixation), Methylotenera (denitrification), and Nitrosomonas (nitrification) rise 402

An important consideration when interpreting amplicon sequencing data is that relative abundance 404 

changes do not necessarily reflect changes in absolute abundance. However, qPCR estimates of total 405 

bacterial load demonstrated a significant positive correlation with biofilm age (Spearman ρ = 0.50, p < 406 

0.001), with late -stage biofilms harboring approximately 2.5 -fold more bacteria than early -stage 407 

biofilms. This confirms that the biofilm community genuinely expands over time and that late -enriched 408 

taxa such as Haliscomenobacter and Saprospiraceae represent real increases in both relative and 409 

absolute terms. Conversely, the decline of early colonizers such as Caulobacter and Pseudomonas likely 410 

reflects genuine displacement rather than simple dilution, as these taxa decreased in relative abundance 411 

far more than the approximately 2.5 -fold increase in total biomass could explain. Furthermore, ANCOM -412 

BC employs a log -ratio framework with bias correction specifically designed to address compositionality 413 

in amplicon data, lending additional confidence to the differential abundance results reported here. 414 

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the experimental design is cross -415 

sectional rather than longitudinal : biofilms of different ages were sampled from different raceways 416 

rather than tracking individual raceways over time. While this approach captures raceway -to -raceway 417 

variability and provides large sample sizes per time point , raceway -specific effects cannot be entirely 418 

excluded. Indeed, within -timepoint PERMANOVA testing confirmed that raceway identity contributed 419 

significant compositional variation at most time point s (p < 0.05 for five of six time point s; data not 420 

shown), particularly at days 38 and 53 where differing disease histories among raceways likely 421 

contributed to this effect. Nevertheless, all pairwise maturity comparisons remained highly significant (p 422 

< 0.001), indicating that the successional signal is robust to raceway -level variability. Second, late -stage 423 

raceways had undergone chemical treatments ( salt, hydrogen peroxide, and/or chloramine -T) during the 424 

production period , and some community shifts at later time point s may partly reflect treatment history 425 

rather than succession alone. Relatedly, because all sampled raceways were actively stocked, biofilm 426 

age is confounded with cumulative nutrient and organic loading: feed input and fish biomass increased 427

The incomplete effectiveness of mechanical cleaning has practical implications for hatchery 460 

management. Routine brushing reduces biofilm biomass but, as shown here, does not prevent rapid re -461 

colonization or the re -establishment of a characteristic mature community within roughly two to three 462 

weeks, indicating that brushing resets standing biomass without altering the successional endpoint. 463 

Where biofilms can act as reservoirs for fish pathogens such as F. columnare , this suggests that brushing 464 

alone is unlikely to be sufficient for biosecurity and may need to be combined with disinfection or more 465 

frequent intervention. This study was not designed to test cleaning efficacy directly; controlled before -466 

and -after or brushed -versus -unbrushed comparisons would be needed to evaluate it rigorously. 467 

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that biofilm communities on hatchery raceway surfaces 468 

undergo rapid, predictable ecological succession characterized by deterministic turnover from r -469 

strategist pioneers to a diverse, mature community enriched in saprophytes, predatory myxobacteria, 470 

and nitrifying bacteria. The predictability of this succession, as evidenced by the high accuracy of our 471 

random forest classifier, suggests that biofilm age could serve as a practical indicator of raceway 472 

condition. In a management context, significant deviations from the expected successional trajectory ,473 

such as persistent dominance of pioneer taxa in an ostensibly mature biofilm, or unusual enrichment of 474 

pathogen -associated genera , could signal disrupted biofilm development or emerging health concerns 475

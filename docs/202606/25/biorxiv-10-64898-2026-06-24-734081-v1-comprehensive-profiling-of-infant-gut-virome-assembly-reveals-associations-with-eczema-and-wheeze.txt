Abstract 30 

Infancy is a critical developmental window during which the gut ecosystem assembles 31 

and helps train the immune system, thereby setting trajectories for lifelong health. 32 

Bacteria and viruses are equally numerous in this early ecosystem, yet the gut 33 

virome’s composition, dynamics, and health relevance remain poorly understood. 34 

Here, we show that the infant gut virome is diverse, dynamic, and linked to health 35 

outcomes. We performed comprehensive virome profiling of 1,110 longitudinal fecal 36 

samples from 314 mother –infant pairs from the Dutch birth cohort Lifelines NEXT 37 

using both virus -like particle enrichment (VLP) and total metagenomic sequencing 38 

(MGS). We find only 18.9% compositional overlap between the VLP - and MGS -39 

metaviromes, with VLP recovering t he active virome and most novel species and MGS 40 

predominantly capturing temperate phages. By combining both methods, we identified 41 

8,348 novel virus species spanning diverse hosts, from bacteria to humans, and all 42 

major viral genome types (dsDNA, ssDNA, and RNA). We find that bacteriophages 43 

frequently encode metabolic functions, including genes related to B vitamin 44 

metabolism. We further observe that the development of the infant gut virome is 45 

shaped by both host factors, including delivery mode and feeding practices, and 46 

continuous switching of temperate phage lifecycles. Notably, the relative abundance 47 

of induced temperate phages is also associated with eczema development within the 48 

first year of life. Together, these findings establish the infant gut virome as a dynamic 49 

and clinically relevant component of early -life microbial development and highlight how 50 

comprehensive dual -method profiling is a necessary framework for future virome 51 

research. 52

Introduction 54 

The early -life gut microbiome is a key determinant of individual health 1,2 . Factors such 55 

as mode of delivery, feeding practices, and antibiotic exposure have been shown to 56 

strongly influence microbiome composition, with consequences for immune 57 

development and later health outcomes 3,4 . While temporal and inter -individual 58 

variation in the bacterial gut community has been profiled at large scale 1,5,6 , the gut 59 

virome —which constitutes a substantial fraction of the gut microbiome —remains 60 

comparatively under -studied. Viruses are at least as numerous as bacteria in the 61

predation, horizontal gene transfer, and modulation of bacterial fitness 8–10 .63 

Nevertheless, only a limited number of small -scale studies have specifically 64 

investigated the gut virome in the first months of life 11,12 . These studies indicate that 65 

the early -life gut virome forms a highly individual -specific community 9,12 that contains 66 

predominantly bacteriophages 13 . Infant gut viruses originate at least in part from 67 

induced prophages, temperate phages integrated into bacterial genomes 11 that are 68 

transmitted from the maternal gut 14 . Once induced, these prophages produce free 69 

virions, with temperate phages dominating the infant gut virome 9,14 . A larger cross -70 

sectional virome study in the Danish COPSAC cohort that included nearly 650 1 -year -71 

old infants identified associations between early -life viral signatures and later 72 

development of asthma, independent of bacterial effects 15 . This finding underscores 73 

the potential importance of the virome as an additional, distinct layer of microbial 74 

influence on child health. Despite these initial insights, several key aspects of the infant 75 

gut virome remain poorly characterized. These include its longitudinal dynamics at 76 

scale and at high temporal resolution, in particular the lysogeny -induction balance of 77 

temperate phages and the persistence of early -life colonizers and their functional 78 

capacity beyond predation on microbial hosts. Resolving these gaps is essential for 79 

clarifying how the early -life virome contributes to child health and disease. 80 

However, characterizing the human gut virome remains methodologically challenging, 81 

and most studies rely on one of two sequencing strategies: targeted enrichment of 82 

virus -like particles (VLPs) or total metagenomic sequencing (MGS) of the entire 83 

microbial community. Extracting VLPs and sequencing their genetic material remains 84 

the gold standard for studying the active fraction of the virome, represented by free 85 

virions 16 . However, this approach requires handling extremely low biomass samples 17 86 

and complex purification protocols 18,19 , which often limits the study sample -size. In 87 

recent years, the field has benefited from the use of total metagenomes to expand 88 

viral catalogs 20,21 . However, because many gut phages are temperate and exist as 89 

integrated prophages 22 , total MGS struggles to distinguish active viral replication from 90 

integrated phage sequences. Additionally, the MGS approach is mostly limited to 91 

dsDNA viruses due to technical limitations in recovering ssDNA and RNA genomes 18 .92 

While the performance in virus recovery from VLP vs MGS has been described in a 93 

limited number of adult and infant gut samples 16,23 , it remains unclear to what extent 94

Study design: concurrent virome annotation in VLP and MGS samples 109 

To study the developing early -life virome, we used a selection of 1,110 fecal samples 110 

from 314 families (326 infants, including 12 twin pairs and 7 sibling pairs, and their 111 

mothers) from the mother –infant cohort Lifelines NEXT 24,1 . Samples from infants were 112 

collected at months 1, 3, 6, and 12 after birth. Matched maternal samples were 113 

collected at month 3 postpartum or at another timepoint if month 3 was unavailable 114 

(Figure 1a, Supplementary Data 1). The study cohort consisted of a representative 115 

population of predominantly term -born infants. Infants had a mean gestational age at 116 

birth of 39.5 ± 1.7 weeks. 82.2% (n = 268) were born via vaginal delivery, of which 117 

29.5% (n = 79) were delivered at home. The extensive information on fee ding, delivery 118 

factors, environment, and infant health collected is described in Supplementary Data 119 

2. 120

The gut virome is a source for novel viruses 129 

To establish a high -resolution landscape of the human gut virome, we constructed the 130 

NEXT virome catalog by integrating sequences across 2,220 paired VLP - and MGS -131 

metaviromes from 1,110 maternal and infant fecal samples. We applied our virome 132 

annotation pipeline to this dataset and merged the identified putative viral sequences 133 

with viral sequences from 11 external viral databases 21,25 –34 to maximize viral 134 

identification (Figure 1b, see Methods). After sequence dereplication and removal of 135 

contamination using sequences identified in negative and positive controls and short 136 

contigs (< 3 kb), we identified 120,997 viral Operational Taxonomic Units (vOTUs), 137 

which were used for downstream analysis (Figure 1b, see Methods). These vOTUs 138 

belonged to 27,017 genus -level and 7,596 family -level clusters, indicating that the 139 

resulting catalog reflects a wide taxonomic spread of gut viruses and captures 140 

substantial diversity. 141 

We next characterized the genomic and taxonomic composition of the NEXT virome 142 

catalog (Supplementary Data 3). High quality vOTUs (HQ vOTUs, see Methods) 143 

accounted for 13.2% (n = 15,969) of all viruses and ranged from 3 to 396.8 kbp in size 144 

(Figure 1c, Supplementary Figure 1a). 88.7% (n = 107,384) of identified viruses were 145 

dsDNA Caudoviricetes phages, followed by ssDNA Malgrandaviricetes phages (3.3%, 146 

n = 4,024). The majority of phages with an assigned lifestyle were temperate phages 147 

(81.7%, n = 29,625). All phages were predicted to target a phylogenetically diverse 148 

range of bacterial hosts, with the highest representation among Bacteroides and 149 

Phocaeicola . Eukaryotic viruses accounted for 2.3% of the NEXT virome catalog, 150 

including most of the RNA viruses identified (423 out of 435). Hosts of eukaryotic 151 

viruses spanned vertebrates (47.1% of all eukaryotic viruses, n = 1,318), protists 152 

(40.5%), and plants (6.7%). Overall, the NEXT virome catalog encompasses broad 153 

viral diversity across genome types, taxonomic groups, and lifestyles, providing a 154 

representative cross -section of the major constituents of the human gut virome. 155 

Notably, 86.2% (n = 104,247) of the NEXT virome catalog consisted of study -specific 156 

sequences not found in external databases (Figure 1c), and 37.5% of these study -157

The human gut holovirome: bridging the gap between VLP - and MGS -metaviromes 229 

To assess the extent to which MGS -metaviromes recapitulate active VLP -detected 230 

viromes, we compared the virome profiles derived from 1,110 VLP and MGS paired 231 

samples (Supplementary Data 1). The main genomic characteristics, such as the 232 

numbers of quality -filtered reads and contigs, were comparable between the two 233 

metavirome types (Supplementary Figure 3a, Supplementary Data 6). VLP - and MGS -234 

metaviromes contributed equally to the NEXT virome catalog, with 30.4% of all vOTUs 235 

recovered exclusively from the VLP -metavirome and 31.7% recovered exclusively 236 

from the MGS -metavirome (Figure 3a). Both methods drastically outperformed the 237 

external databases, which contributed only 2.5% of the NEXT virome catalog 238 

sequences .239 

As genome fragmentation can artificially inflate total counts of recovered vOTUs, we 240 

evaluated how both methods contributed to the recovery of 8,348 novel HQ vOTUs 241 

(see Methods). VLP -metaviromes recovered 3.2 -fold more novel HQ vOTUs than 242 

MGS, across diverse genome types and lifestyles, with the recovery of 72.7% (n = 243 

6,071) novel HQ vOTUs being VLP -dependent (Figure 3b). While most MGS -exclusive 244 

HQ vOTUs (66.5%, n = 754) were temperate dsDNA phages, the majority (53.7%) of 245 

the exclusively VLP -recoverable H Q vOTUs (n = 3,261) were ssDNA and RNA viruses 246 

that cannot be captured by standard MGS. 247

we examined the sample -wise composition of vOTUs at the population level. MGS -249 

metaviromes had significantly higher richness than VLP -metaviromes, with a median 250 

of 718 (interquartile range (IQR): 384 –1,562) versus 305 (IQR: 152 –611) vOTUs per 251

Our results demonstrate that VLP - and MGS -metaviromes provide fundamentally 284 

different perspectives on the gut virome. While VLP enrichment excels at recovering 285 

high quality genomes and broadening the genomic landscape to include novel ssDNA 286 

and RNA viruses, MGS remains superior at capturing the integrated dsDNA phage 287 

fraction. Crucially, while both methods capture highly individual -specific signatures, 288 

they share a remarkably limited per -sample vOTU overlap and represent divergent 289 

ecological layers. The MGS -virome strongly correlates with the bacteriome 290 

composition, while the VLP -virome is largely independent of it. We therefore propose 291 

the concept of the ‘holovirome’ (Figure 3h), which combines data from the VLP -292 

metavirome (the active virome) and the MGS -metavirome (the integrated prophages) 293 

to capture the full diversity and dynamics of gut virome. 294 

The infant gut virome is dynamic and shaped by delivery and feeding modes 295 

We next explored the dynamics of the infant gut virome over the first year of life, 296 

focusing primarily on the active virome (VLP), backed up by the holovirome, as the 297 

MGS virome has already been described for these samples as part of a larger study 40 .298 

Both the holovirome and active virome richness were lower in infants compared to 299 

mothers (FDR < 0.05; Figure 4a, Supplementary Data 11), but both increased with 300 

infant age (FDR < 0.05). The infant holovirome expanded faster than the active virome 301 

(p -value = 1.4e -04), driven by the number of integrated prophages (p -value < 2.2e -16; 302 

Supplementary Figure 3b) and bacterial richness, independent of age (p -value = 4.2e -303 

27; Supplementary Figure 3c). During the first 6 months of life, viral richness was also 304 

hi gher in both the holovirome and active virome of formula -fed infants (FDR < 0.05; 305 

Supplementary Data 12, Supplementary Figure 3d,e). These comparisons suggest 306 

that the diversification of infant gut viruses is strongly driven by the pace of bacterial 307 

community assembly and shaped by infant feeding mode. 308

Discussion 477 

The early -life gut microbial ecosystem plays a critical role in human host 478 

development 1,2 . However, while the bacterial component has been extensively 479 

studied, the virome remains comparatively underexplored 19 . In this large longitudinal 480 

study of 1,110 samples from 314 families in the Lifelines NEXT cohort, we 481 

characterized the infant gut virome by combining VLP enrichment with MGS, enabling 482 

a comprehensive assessment of both the active and integrated viral fractions. Using 483 

this approach, we annotated thousands of novel viral species and genera, 484 

characterized their functional potential, described the development of the early -life 485 

virome and the lifecycle dynamics of temperate phages, and linked viral characteristics 486 

to infant health. 487 

Over the past decade, inter -individual variation in the gut virome has been 488 

characterized in increasingly large cohorts 42,40,43 , predominantly using MGS data, 489 

driving major expansions in cataloged viral diversity 20,44 . However, it has remained 490 

unclear to what extent the MGS -annotated virome represents the active, encapsidated 491 

virome. Our results indicate that VLP - and MGS -annotated viromes from the same 492 

fecal sample overlap by only 18.9 %, on average, in the viruses detected per sample. 493 

This low overlap is driven by enrichment of virulent phages and non -dsDNA viruses in 494 

the VLP -annotated virome, resulting in the MGS -annotate d virome recapitulating only 495 

40% of the active, encapsidated VLP -virome. Further, consistent with the dominance 496 

of lysogeny in the healthy human gut 22,8 and the enrichment of temperate phage 497 

recovery observed in MGS -metaviromes, the MGS -annotated virome largely reflects 498 

the bacteriome community, while the VLP -virome is mostly independent of it 499 

(correlation with bacteriome composition 0.80 vs. 0.24). This observation highlights 500 

that the VLP -virome provides an additional omics layer, largely independent from the 501 

bacteriome. The characterization of the gut virome therefore requires both VLP and 502 

MGS, which together yield the holovirome —the union of all detected viruses, resolved 503 

into their active (free virion) and latent (integrated prophage) fractions. 504

Combination of both VLP and MGS -based approaches in our study enabled recovery 505 

of novel viruses across multiple genera and families. Consistent with previous 506 

comparisons of virus genome recovery performance between VLP and MGS in human 507 

gut 23,45 and environmental 16 samples, VLP substantially outperformed MGS in 508 

recovering a diverse set of novel viruses across different genome types. The novel 509 

ssDNA viruses we identified, which were recovered almost exclusively by VLP, 510 

increased the known diversity of ssDNA viruses by more than 50% . Although ssDNA 511 

viruses constituted a limited fraction of the gut viruses detected per sample, they were 512 

abundant in both infants and adults, making them prominent players of the human gut. 513 

In addition to DNA viruses, we identified candidate novel RNA viruses of potential 514 

clinical relevance, including enteroviruses. Together, these observations expand the 515 

known diversity of the early -life virome and underscore the value of profiling viruses 516 

across genome types. 517 

Beyond the expanding genomic diversity of viruses, we also explored their functional 518 

diversity and found that gut phages frequently encode genes with auxiliary functions. 519 

Among annotated auxiliary gene groups, anti -defense systems were the largest group, 520 

despite their diversity likely being under -characterized 46 . Notably, multiple gut phages 521 

also encoded genes involved in the synthesis and metabolism of all eight essential B 522 

vitamins. These genes likely enhance phage replication, but they may also confer a 523 

competitive advantage on the bacterial host because dietary B vitamin availability in 524 

the colon is limited by upstream absorption 47 . By altering bacterial vitamin metabolism, 525 

phages could also indirectly affect vitamin availability for the human “mega -host”, as 526 

some B vitamins can still be absorbed in the colon 47 . These findings support the idea 527 

that gut viruses may influence microbiome function and the human host not only 528 

through predation, but also through gene transfer. 529 

In adults, a small fraction of an individual’s viruses persists for prolonged periods and 530 

dominates the active virome 35,41 . We show that a PPV forms within the first year of life, 531 

even though its size is smaller than that of adults 35 . Whether a virus persisted in infants 532 

across the first year was linked to whether it carried anti -defense systems, maternal 533 

sharing, and its presence at month 1, highlighting the critical role of the early -life 534 

window in shaping the gut virome. In parallel, we showed that temperate phages 535

interactions between bacteria and viruses in the infant gut. We also show that viruses 589

health outcomes. These findings position the holovirome as a foundational framework 591

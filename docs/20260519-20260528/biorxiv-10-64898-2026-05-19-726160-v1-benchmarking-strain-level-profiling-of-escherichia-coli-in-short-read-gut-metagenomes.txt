2. Abstract 31 

Metagenomes offer the potential to characterise Escherichia coli strain-level diversity 32 

within the human gut microbiome, informing our understanding of colonisation diversity 33 

and the genetic features distinguishing infection from carriage. Among numerous 34 

reference-based tools for short-read metagenomic strain-level profiling, the best 35 

approach remains unclear. Here, we benchmarked six published tools—PanTax, 36 

PathoScope, StrainGE, Strainify, StrainR2 and StrainScan—for their ability to detect 37 

co-existing strains of E. coli and estimate their relative abundance across real and 38 

simulated metagenomes of increasing complexity with varying reference database 39 

composition. In the ZymoBIOMICS ® D6331 dataset, only PanTax achieved zero error 40 

when predicting the equal abundance of five E. coli strains. In a differentially abundant 41 

four-strain mock community dataset (SRR13355226), StrainScan had the lowest 42 

mean absolute proportional error (0.89), driven by reduced sensitivity (0.5), followed 43 

by PathoScope (4.08). Across simulated metagenomes reflecting the healthy adult gut 44 

microbiome, all tools demonstrated high sensitivity (≥0.833), but specificity, precision 45 

and F1 score were selectively improved in some tools through detection thresholds to 46 

remove low abundance false positives. Outright, StrainGE achieved the highest F1 47 

score (0.978). Predicted relative abundances of the E. coli K12-MG1655 (phylogroup 48 

A) and O157:H7 Sakai (phylogroup E) strains spiked into simulated metagenomes 49 

across varying abundance ratios were generally accurate, with PanTax and StrainR2 50 

showing the lowest mean absolute proportional error (0.06). When truly present strains 51 

were removed from the reference database, out-of-phylogroup assignments were 52 

observed for some tools. Collectively, our results demonstrate that published 53 

metagenomic strain-level profiling tools vary in their ability to profile E. coli strains, 54 

indicating that method selection should be guided by intended application. These 55 

findings will facilitate characterisation of E. coli strain-level diversity within short-read 56 

gut metagenomes with greater accuracy than previously possible. 57

3. Impact statement 60 

Strain-level diversity within the human gut microbiome can be important for human 61 

health, with species such as Escherichia coli existing as both commensal and 62 

pathogenic strains. Most existing gut microbiome datasets are from short-read i.e. ,63 

Illumina, sequencing, and numerous bioinformatic tools have been developed to 64 

profile strain-level variation from these data. However, the existing literature is often 65 

difficult to navigate given that the available tools have been benchmarked in various 66 

ways and are subject to author bias. This is, to our knowledge, the first independent 67 

benchmarking of six published tools for profiling E. coli at strain-level resolution from 68 

short-read metagenomes. Using both real and simulated datasets of increasing 69

5. Introduction 118 

The human gut microbiome is a highly diverse community of microorganisms 119 

comprising >270 species[1], with substantial inter-individual variation that has been 120 

identified as a critical determinant of human health[2]. However, gut microbiome 121 

variation extends beyond species to the strain-level, with strains of the same species 122 

exhibiting genotypic and phenotypic heterogeneity[3, 4]. A prominent example is 123 

Escherichia coli , which shows substantial strain-level diversity in the human gut, with 124 

recent studies reporting an average of 2.5 unique strains per individual and dynamic 125 

strain turnover over time[5-8]. While commensal E. coli strains contribute to gut 126 

homeostasis[9, 10], other strains have acquired virulence genes from the vast 127 

accessory genome present within the gut reservoir, resulting in pathogenicity[11, 12]. 128 

Extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli strains exemplify this process and are the major 129 

cause of Gram-negative bloodstream infections worldwide[13-16]. Accurately 130 

characterising E. coli colonisation diversity in the gut microbiome would facilitate an 131 

improved understanding of the differences between commensal and disease-132 

associated strains, with a view to developing targeted interventions such as vaccines 133 

and therapeutics. 134 

Metagenomic sequencing has become the dominant method for gut microbiome 135 

analyses as it enables characterisation of all DNA within a sample[17]. However, 136 

analysing E. coli strain-level diversity within available gut metagenomes remains 137 

challenging due to the low relative abundance of E. coli in healthy individuals (typically 138 

~0.1–1.2%)[7, 18], and the historic predominance of short-read Illumina sequencing, 139 

which is limited in resolving closely related strains across repetitive genomic regions 140 

and consequently constrains the resolution of commonly used taxonomic 141 

classifiers[19]. For example, Kraken2 relies on shared k-mer matches and lowest 142 

common ancestor assignment, such that reads mapping to multiple closely related 143 

reference genomes are often classified at higher taxonomic levels[20, 21]. Similarly, 144 

E. coli –specific metagenomic typing approaches such as metaMLST infer sequence 145 

types using multilocus sequence typing, but resolve each locus in a sample to a single 146 

best-supported allele[22]. This can limit the ability to capture within-sample allelic 147 

diversity and restricts inference to dominant strains, as was seen in a recent 148 

metaMLST-centred strain-level analysis of E. coli in 5,128 healthy adult gut 149 

metagenomes[23]. 150

To address these limitations, numerous strain-level profiling tools have been 151 

developed, employing several methodologies to estimate the relative abundance and 152 

number of strains for a given species within short-read metagenomes. However, this 153 

is a rapidly evolving field, and the existing literature is often difficult to navigate given 154 

that the available tools have been benchmarked in various ways. Here, we survey 155 

existing tools, selecting six for further investigation using pre-defined criteria. We then 156 

benchmark their ability to profile E. coli strains across real and simulated 157 

metagenomes of increasing complexity, allowing us to provide recommendations for 158 

tool selection depending on the biological question. 159

6. Methods 162 

Selection of strain-level profiling tools 163 

Candidate strain-level profiling tools were identified through literature searches in 164 

PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and by examining references and citation 165 

networks of relevant tool publications. Tools were included in the benchmark if they (i) 166 

supported multi-strain-level resolution from short-read metagenomic sequencing data, 167 

(ii) reported strain-level relative abundances, (iii) accepted a user-customisable 168 

reference database, and (iv) were actively maintained in an open-source repository 169 

e.g. , GitHub. Tools which were identified but did not satisfy these requirements are 170 

listed in Table S1, alongside reasons for exclusion. Strain-level profiling tools included 171 

in this benchmark are presented in Table 1. The final versions of the six tools used 172 

were StrainScan[24] v1.0.14, Strainify[25] v1.1.0, StrainR2[26] v2.3.0, 173

Benchmarking datasets 191 

Across benchmarking datasets, the default outputs for predicted relative abundance 192 

were taken per tool, unless otherwise stated. StrainGE was an exception, where 193 

estimated strain-level abundances from the StrainGST module ( rapct ; reported relative 194 

to the entire metagenome) were normalised (rapct /total_ rapct ) to enable direct 195 

comparison across tools. 196 

Benchmarking datasets were designed to differentially test tools by evaluating: (i) 197 

different E. coli strains present at equal abundance in a low-complexity polymicrobial 198 

background (ZymoBIOMICS® D6331 gut microbiome standard), (ii) the impact of 199 

human read removal and differential abundance on E. coli strain profiling 200 

(SRR13355226 mock community), and (iii) different E. coli strains present at 201 

differential abundances in a high-complexity polymicrobial background (simulated 202 

metagenomes), with the latter anticipated to be most like a ‘real-life’ human gut 203 

microbiome. 204

Performance metrics 279 

Across all datasets, estimation metrics were calculated from predicted relative 280 

abundances using error, absolute error, absolute proportional error, mean error, mean 281 

absolute error and mean absolute proportional error, as appropriate. Detection metrics 282 

of sensitivity, specificity, precision and F1 score were calculated based on raw call-283 

type counts (true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives) for the 284 

simulated metagenomes dataset. Full equations are available in the online 285 

Supplementary Methods (S2: Eqs. S2.1–S2.10). Detection thresholds were applied at 286 

relative abundance cut-offs of 0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, and 0.005. Predicted relative 287 

abundances below each threshold were set to zero, and presence/absence calls were 288 

redefined accordingly for recalculation of sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score 289 

(see Data Summary). 290

7. Results 305 

Strain-level profiling error varies across tools despite equal abundance of five 306 

E. coli strains 307 

We selected the ZymoBIOMICS® D6331 gut microbiome standard as a low-308 

complexity benchmarking dataset. This standard comprises 21 strains representative 309 

of the human gut microbiome, including five E. coli strains present at equal ground 310 

truth relative abundance (0.2; Table S2). For this experiment, these five strains were 311 

also included in the tool-profiling reference database. 312 

All tools predicted differing estimates of relative abundance per strain, except for 313 

identical predictions of B2207 from StrainScan and Strainify (0.165; Fig.1a). The 314 

B1109 and B3008 strains were consistently overestimated (error range: 0.009–0.034 315

Detection thresholds selectively improve strain-level detection 375 

To increase benchmarking complexity, we simulated metagenomes reflecting a376 

realistic healthy adult gut microbiome. The baseline relative abundance of E. coli 377 

(1.96%) was differentially split between two strains (K12-MG1655 and O157:H7 Sakai) 378 

across a range of abundance ratios and sequencing efforts (Table S4). To reflect a 379 

more real-life scenario of profiling an unknown diversity of colonising E. coli strains in 380 

a metagenome we also increased the complexity of the reference database to 24 E. 381 

coli strains representing diversity within and across the major phylogroups (Table S5). 382 

Summary detection metrics showed that strains that were genuinely present (true 383 

positives) were rarely, if ever, missed, with all tools exhibiting high sensitivity ranging 384 

from 0.833 (StrainScan) to 1.000 (PathoScope, Strainify, and StrainR2) (Fig.3a; Table 385 

S8). This was true even at low sequencing depth of O157:H7 str. Sakai (≥0.210×; Table 386 

S4). However, the reported detection of absent strains (false positives) was a problem 387 

for some tools, with PathoScope (0.014; 0.070), Strainify (0.072; 0.074), and StrainR2 388 

(0.596; 0.160) exhibiting lower specificity and precision (Fig.3a; Table S8). For 389 

example, PathoScope and Strainify produced 1189 and 1119 false positive detections, 390 

respectively, out of 1296 possible calls (see Data summary). In contrast, other tools 391 

exhibited high specificity and precision, including PanTax (0.996; 0.944), StrainGE 392 

(0.998; 0.967), StrainScan (1.000; 1.000), and StrainScan super low depth mode 393 

(1.000; 1.000) (Fig.3a; Table S8), indicating low false positive detection rates and high 394 

positive predictive values. 395 

The F1 score, defined as the harmonic mean of sensitivity and precision, provides a 396 

balanced summary of detection performance. Here, StrainGE achieved the highest F1 397 

score (0.978), followed by StrainScan super low depth (0.947), PanTax (0.939) and 398 

StrainScan (0.909) (Fig.3a; Table S8). The remaining tools showed much lower F1 399 

scores (StrainR2, 0.270; Strainify, 0.139; PathoScope, 0.131). 400 

Applying increasing detection thresholds (0.0001–0.005) led to increases in specificity, 401 

precision, and F1 score for PathoScope, Strainify, and StrainR2 (Fig.3b). F1 scores 402 

increased to 0.733 (PathoScope), 0.476 (Strainify), and 0.952 (StrainR2) at the 0.005 403 

detection threshold, with the latter exceeding PanTax and both StrainScan modes 404 

original F1 score (Fig.3b). As sensitivity remained unchanged, we interpret these 405 

increases as being driven by the reassignment of numerous low-abundance false 406 

positive detections from the 24-strain E. coli reference database. In contrast, PanTax, 407 

StrainGE, StrainScan, and StrainScan super low depth mode were unaffected by the 408 

detection thresholds applied here (Fig.3b). 409

8. Discussion 521 

We benchmarked six tools (PanTax, PathoScope, StrainGE, Strainify, StrainR2 and 522 

StrainScan) for strain-level profiling of Escherichia coli within short-read gut 523 

metagenomes. In an equal-abundance mock community (ZymoBIOMICS ® D6331), 524 

PanTax achieved zero error, perfectly predicting the equal abundance of 5 E. coli 525 

strains. In a differentially abundant mock community (SRR13355226), human read 526 

removal had no effect on predicted relative abundance across tools. StrainScan had 527 

the lowest mean absolute proportional error (0.89), although this was driven by 528 

reduced sensitivity. Across high-complexity simulated metagenomes reflecting a529 

realistic healthy adult gut microbiome and with the E. coli strains present also included 530 

in the reference database, all tools showed high sensitivity (≥0.833). Applying 531 

detection thresholds to remove false-positive assignations at low abundance improved 532 

specificity, precision and F1 score for three tools, including StrainR2, which reached 533 

an F1 score of 0.952. Without thresholding, StrainGE achieved the highest F1 score 534 

overall (0.978). Concerningly, some tools using alignment for strain assignment 535 

(PathoScope and Strainify) reported non-zero E. coli abundance in negative controls, 536 

emphasising the need for careful interpretation . Predicted relative abundances of two 537 

true strains spiked into the simulated metagenomes were generally accurate across 538 

tools, with PanTax and StrainR2 performing best (APE = 0.06). Finally, removing true 539 

strains from the reference database resulted in divergent relative abundance 540 

reassignments across tools. 541 

To our knowledge, this represents the first independent E. coli -specific benchmark of 542 

short-read metagenomic strain-level profiling tools. The ZymoBIOMICS® D6331 543 

dataset has not previously been used, and our simulated metagenomes were 544 

generated independently using InSilicoSeq[36]. Although SRR13355226 has been 545 

used previously[24, 25, 29], it had not been evaluated across this combination of tools. 546 

SRR13355226 was most recently used to compare Strainify with StrainScan, 547 

StrainR2, and StrainGE[25]. Consistent with our findings, all tools preserved the 548 

correct ranking of strains by relative abundance, but all predictions deviated 549 

substantially from the estimated ground truth. As the authors did not quantify these 550 

deviations using an error metric, we calculated mean absolute proportional error for 551 

each tool using their reported values. StrainGE (21.99) and StrainR2 (16.59) were 552 

consistent with our results (21.75 and 16.67, respectively), whereas the recalculated 553 

StrainScan value (27.84) better reflects the true error when all strains are detected 554 

(0.89; Fig.2b). In contrast, Strainify (14.97) was lower than that observed in our 555 

analysis (26.48). The Strainify authors also used the four E. coli strains in 556 

SRR13355226 to construct simulated mock communities[25]. Consistent with our 557 

findings, StrainScan struggled to detect low-abundance strains, while its super low-558 

depth mode improved detection at the cost of substantial overestimation; an effect 559 

attributed to biasing the abundance estimate of the dominant strain[24, 25]. However, 560 

they did not assess the impact of human read removal on relative abundance 561

estimates. We found that human read removal had no significant effect on abundance 562 

prediction accuracy across tools. Host DNA typically comprises around 10% of faecal 563 

samples[50]. Therefore, the ability to remove human reads whilst remaining confident 564 

in the accuracy of strain-level relative abundance estimates will substantially reduce 565 

file size and storage requirements, while continuing to protect donor privacy by 566 

removing patient-identifiable data. 567 

Using our simulated metagenomes dataset, we noted that some tools (PathoScope, 568 

Strainify and StrainR2) reported numerous low-abundance false-positive E. coli 569 

detections, despite high sensitivity. Except for Strainify, the cumulative abundance of 570 

these false positives was typically very low (<0.01), likely reflecting ambiguous read 571 

assignments. Nevertheless, the application of detection thresholds indicates that post 572 

hoc filtering can mitigate these effects, leading to improvements in specificity, precision 573 

and F1 score. However, given the strong performance of the remaining tools without 574 

the need for thresholding, it suggests that tool selection should be influenced by the 575 

ease of implementation and intended application. For example, without needing any 576 

thresholding StrainGE (F1 score = 0.978) provides a simple and accurate approach to 577 

strain detection, and has already been successfully used to identify acquired E. coli 578 

strains in pre- and post-international travel gut metagenomes[51] and in contaminated 579 

drinking water in Kenya[52], both using larger reference databases than applied here, 580 

inferring easy scalability. In comparison, PanTax, which achieved the highest 581 

estimation accuracy in two out of three datasets, and StrainR2, which tied with PanTax 582 

as the most accurate in the simulated metagenomes dataset, are better suited to 583 

investigating accurate abundance estimation. 584 

Notably, PanTax, which also supports the increasing availability of long-read 585 

metagenomic data[28, 53], also demonstrated a comparably high F1 score (0.939). 586 

This suggests that its recent pangenome graph-based methodology, which reduces 587 

redundancy while retaining strain-level diversity in the reference database[54], may 588 

offer advantages over other approaches when both accurate detection and abundance 589 

estimation are desired within a single tool. However, it should be noted that PanTax 590 

requires an integer linear programming solver. The authors recommend Gurobi and 591 

demonstrate improved performance with this solver[28, 55]; however, this may 592 

introduce practical constraints, particularly in high-performance computing 593 

environments where internet access is restricted or unavailable on compute nodes, 594 

potentially complicating license validation. The remaining tools do not require a solver 595 

and are generally more computationally straightforward, with intuitive module- or 596 

Snakemake-based command line workflows (Table 1). 597 

We also evaluated tool performance when the true strains were excluded from the 598 

reference database, reflecting real-world scenarios in which the expected strain 599 

composition of a gut metagenome is unknown, rather than use cases where relative 600 

abundances for strains which are expected to be present are being estimated. Whilst 601 

some tools were developed to report the most similar strain in the reference 602

level profiling tool is context-dependent, and a complementary, paired-tool strategy 614 

may be advantageous for analysing novel metagenomic datasets. An initial pass with 615 

a high performing detection metrics tool (e.g., StrainGE) could be used to filter a large 616 

reference database and identify candidate phylogroups, followed by a strain-resolved 617 

method with strong abundance estimation performance (e.g., PanTax) applied to this 618 

reduced phylogroup search space. While our results support the rationale for paired 619 

tool usage, this approach is not considered as part of this evaluation. 620 

Advantages of our study include the independent benchmarking of six competitive 621 

strain-level profiling tools on various metagenomes and with different reference 622 

databases, enabling us to identify the advantages and disadvantages of each method. 623 

These conclusions are summarised in Table 2. Furthermore, we focused on the strain-624 

profiling of E. coli , a common and genetically diverse member of the human gut 625 

microbiome and a major cause of Gram-negative bloodstream infections worldwide, 626 

which is an increasing global One Health concern due to its high rates of antibiotic 627 

resistance[56]. Although focusing on E. coli may limit the transferability of our 628 

conclusions to other clinically relevant, strain-diverse gut colonisers, such as 629 

Enterococcus faecium and Klebsiella pneumoniae [57], we have provided the means 630 

to reproduce the benchmarking analysis to enable convenient repurposing to consider 631 

performance for other important bacterial targets. However, while our conclusions are 632 

valid at the time of writing, tool performance is likely to evolve as methods are updated. 633 

For example, PanTax was upgraded to v2.1.0 on 2 March 2026 [28], after completion 634 

of our benchmarking experiments. New tools will also continue to emerge, particularly 635 

for long-read metagenomics, for which dedicated approaches, like Strainberry[58], are 636 

already available. Continued benchmarking from the metagenomics community will be 637 

essential to monitor these developments. Finally, we note that our analysis focused 638 

exclusively on reference-based tools to ensure a consistent and comparable 639 

evaluation. As such, the approaches assessed here are inherently limited in their 640 

ability to detect novel or previously uncharacterised E. coli strains lacking reference 641 

genomes, a limitation that de novo strain-level profiling methods can overcome[59]. 642

9. Conclusions 647 

This study represents the most recent benchmarking of short-read metagenomic 648 

strain-level profiling tools and the first to focus exclusively on Escherichia coli , a 649 

species exhibiting substantial strain-level diversity within the human gut. The six 650 

benchmarked tools showed variable performance across datasets, suggesting the use 651 

of different methods depending on the intended application. Our findings will enable 652 

characterisation of E. coli strain-level diversity within short-read gut metagenomes in 653 

greater depth and at a larger-scale than has been achieved previously. This will 654 

advance our understanding of whether consistent genetic distinctions exist between 655 

commensal and pathogenic E. coli strains, with potential implications for the 656 

development of targeted therapeutics and vaccines. 657

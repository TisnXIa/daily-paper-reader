Abstract 6

Background 7Nanopore sequencing offers a cost-effective and portable platform for microbiome analysis, but amplicon-8based approaches remain limited by higher sequencing error rates and a lack of workflows tailored to 9mixed domain ribosomal RNA profiling. While short-read technologies dominate microbial community 10 analysis, their portability and flexibility are constrained. There is therefore a need for robust pipelines 11 designed specifically for cross-domain Nanopore amplicon data. 12 

Results 13 We introduce the Nanopore sequencing-based Amplicon Pipeline (NAP; https://github.com/Luke-B-14 Jones/NAP), an open-source workflow optimised for flexible mixed domain primer sets such as 15 515Y/926R. NAP performs adaptive quality filtering, chimera removal, centroid generation, BLAST-16 based taxonomic classification, hierarchical consensus correction, and domain-aware post-processing, 17 outputting decontaminated abundance tables suitable for downstream analysis. Initial validation against 18 two complementary commercial mock communities showed that NAP achieved strong genus-level 19 performance across both low complexity logarithmic and more compositionally complex gut mock 20 communities. Detection was most reliable above ca. 1% relative abundance, and replicate outputs showed 21 strong agreement with expected composition under Bray–Curtis, Jaccard, agreement-plot, and Bland– 22 Altman analyses. Benchmarking of NAP’s internal filtering modes showed that the default adaptive 23 setting provided the most robust balance of read quality, retained depth, and downstream taxonomic 24 fidelity across heterogeneous inputs. Direct comparison against QIIME2 and Kraken2/Bracken further 25 showed that NAP most accurately preserved expected community structure, with markedly fewer false 26 positive assignments at genus level and substantially stronger species-level behaviour under the tested 27 conditions. Species-level assignments were informative for some taxa, but remained less robust than 28 genus-level outputs with the default V4–V5 amplicon. 29 

Conclusions 30 NAP provides a robust and flexible workflow for cross-domain Nanopore amplicon profiling, with 31 strongest performance at genus level and competitive species-level behaviour for well resolved taxa. 32

Background 39 Microbiomes are increasingly studied from a cross-domain perspective, capturing interactions among 40 bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes across diverse environmental and host-associated contexts. While 41 microbiome research has historically focused on bacteria, recent work highlights that inter-domain 42 interactions are central to understanding community structure and function, and health-related outcomes. 43 In clinical settings, bacterial–fungal interactions have physical, metabolic, and immune implications in 44 disease (Arvanitis and Mylonakis, 2015). Inter-kingdom microbial networks are now recognized as 45 potential drivers of complex pathologies and may offer novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets (Li et al. ,46 2018; Wang et al ., 2023). Beyond medicine, these interactions shape ecological dynamics, influence food 47 microbiology, and affect agricultural productivity (Frey-Klett et al ., 2011). As such, accurate and domain-48 inclusive profiling of microbial communities is essential for advancing microbiome science across 49 multiple disciplines. 50 While shotgun and whole genome sequencing approaches offer high taxonomic resolution and low 51 taxonomic bias, they remain costly and computationally intensive. For studies focused primarily on 52 community composition, amplicon sequencing provides a faster and more cost-effective alternative. 53 Traditionally, however, amplicon-based profiling has been restricted to bacteria using 16S rRNA primers, 54 or has relied on separate 16S and 18S reactions to study bacterial and eukaryotic components 55 independently. Because these are amplified and sequenced in isolation, their abundance outputs are not 56 directly comparable, limiting cross-domain integration. 57 Recent advances in primer design have addressed this issue by enabling the amplification of bacterial, 58 archaeal, and eukaryotic small subunit rRNA from a single reaction using universal primer sets. Illumina-59 based workflows offer high base accuracy and are compatible with such primers, but remain limited by 60 short read lengths. Moreover, while mature and user-friendly pipelines exist for Illumina data, these often 61 require programming expertise and lack tools for domain-aware normalisation or bias correction. 62 Nanopore sequencing, in contrast, supports real-time, full length amplicon sequencing with much lower 63 capital outlay and greater portability (Lao et al ., 2021; Charalampous et al ., 2019). Implementation 79 

NAP is implemented as an end-to-end command line workflow in Bash and Python for Unix/Linux-based 80 execution, with Docker support to simplify installation and improve reproducibility across systems. 81 Established third party tools are used for core sequence processing steps, whilst custom Bash wrappers 82 and Python modules handle workflow orchestration, taxonomy parsing, consensus calling, abundance 83 processing, decontamination, and output summarisation (Figure 1). This structure was adopted to preserve 84 a simple user interface whilst retaining modularity at code level, allowing individual components to be 85 updated or replaced without altering the overall workflow. 86

SILVA rRNA reference databases are first refined to remove ambiguous, taxonomically unresolved, off-98 target, and redundant entries, and in the default ‘mammalian_microbiome_inclusive’ mode, contaminant 99 and organellar records such as plant mitochondrial rRNA are also removed. Alternative filtering modes 100 can also be defined by the user. The curated reference is then divided into 16S and 18S subsets so that 101 primer mapping and amplicon extraction can be performed within the expected domain-specific sequence 102 space, accounting for the different amplicon structures generated by mixed domain primer sets. Primer 103 sequences are mapped to each retained entry using ungapped local alignment, and the longest predicted 104 amplicon region is extracted where possible; untrimmed entries are retained only where strict trimming 105 would result in unnecessary loss of reference coverage. These primer-aware, domain-specific reference 106 sets are then indexed for BLAST-based classification. This design reduces redundant high-scoring 107 matches, limits ambiguous search space, and decreases processing time without requiring users to 108 manually construct primer-specific databases for each assay. 109 NAP processes demultiplexed amplicon reads (FASTQ) through a centroid-first workflow optimised for 110 mixed domain Nanopore microbiome profiling. Reads are first subjected to adaptive quality control using 111 an input depth-dependent filtering scheme. Samples are assigned to preset read depth bins according to the 112 initial number of input reads, with each bin linked, via the NAP configuration file, to a corresponding read 113 level Phred threshold and base masking stringency. Under the validation conditions used here, these 114 settings were empirically calibrated to favour the highest average read quality whilst retaining ≥100,000 115 reads where possible. Firstly, base qualities are tallied across the dataset, and a masking cutoff is selected 116 such that only a defined high quality proportion of bases remains unmasked; bases below this cutoff are 117 converted to ‘ N’ rather than causing the entire read to be discarded in downstream mean Phred filtering. 118 Following this, the associated Phred is applied. This design allows higher depth datasets to be filtered 119 more stringently while preserving usable sequence in lower depth samples. The exact default bin 120 definitions used here are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Users can modify these thresholds to better suit 121 alternative datasets. Once filtering has been completed, very deep samples are capped by default at 122 225,000 reads to prevent unnecessary computational overhead during downstream clustering and 123 classification. Chimeric reads are then removed with VSEARCH, and the filtered read set (‘RAW’) is 124 clustered with CD-HIT to generate representative centroids (‘CNT’). These centroid sequences are 125 classified against the primer-trimmed SILVA database using BLAST+, with alignment settings tuned 126 separately for centroid-to-reference classification and subsequent RAW-to-centroid reassignment, 127 reflecting the distinct alignment objectives of these stages. Taxonomic consensus is then derived from the 128 retained BLAST hits using a hierarchical frequency-weighted procedure. Briefly, the dominant taxonomic 129 structure across the top matches is identified, and the best supported representative assignment is then 130 selected using percent identity, bit score, and e-value. The annotated centroid set (‘HAC’) is subsequently 131 converted into an internal high confidence database against which all RAW reads are re-aligned, 132

Taxonomic assignments can 135 be collapsed to genus and species level, normalised using total sum scaling (TSS), and filtered to remove 136 low confidence taxa below the reporting threshold. For the bundled 515Y/926R configuration, NAP 137 applies a default 16S/18S correction factor of 0.4 to account for the reduced amplification efficiency of 138 co-amplified 18S templates relative to 16S targets. When matched blank controls are supplied, and 139 decontamination is activated in the configuration, blank-informed decontamination is performed by 140 temporarily denormalising and harmonising sample and blank abundance profiles across taxonomic levels, 141 before evaluating taxa using configurable prevalence- and abundance-based rules. Taxa supported 142 primarily by blank signal are then removed or penalised adaptively before final re-normalisation. NAP 143 does not currently apply taxon-specific SSU copy number correction, and output abundances should 144 therefore be interpreted as relative amplicon abundances rather than direct estimates of cellular 145 abundance. 146 Global run behaviour is controlled through a main configuration file, whilst primer-specific defaults are 147 defined in modular sub-configurations containing the expected amplicon structure, reference resources, 148 alignment thresholds, and any domain-specific abundance adjustments. This enables users to adapt the 149 workflow to alternative rRNA primer sets without modifying the core code. Input can be supplied either as 150 a single explicitly pathed read file with a user-defined sample name, or as a tabular manifest for batch 151 execution. For manifest-based execution, output locations are user-defined, allowing runs to be initiated 152 from any working directory. Final outputs include genus- and species-level abundance tables (.tsv), 153 pipeline logs (.txt), intermediate classification files, and quality summaries intended to support 154 troubleshooting and user confidence, whilst also allowing users to interrogate full taxonomic paths and, if 155 desired, make manual taxonomic consensus assessments. 156 157

Mock community preparation 159 

Two commercially available mock microbiome communities were used to evaluate the pipeline: 160 ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard II (Log Distribution), and ZymoBIOMICS Gut 161 Microbiome Standard (both Cambridge Biosciences); henceforth, these are referred to as log mock (L1, 162 L2, and L3) community and gut mock (M1, M2, and M3) community, respectively. These two standards 163 were selected because they impose complementary validation challenges relevant to mixed domain 164 Nanopore amplicon profiling. Homogenisation was performed according to a custom protocol, with six cycles of 30 seconds 180 at 9,000 rpm using a Bertin Precellys Evolution bead beater, increasing the yield of harder to lyse 181 organisms (Zhang et al ., 2020). DNA was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher 182 Scientific). Amplicon pools were then 189 sequenced using one MinION R10.4.1 flow cell according to the Native Barcoding Protocol (SQK-190 NBD114.24), and then basecalled and demultiplexed using Dorado (v0.9.0; “nanoporetech/dorado,” 2025; 191 super accuracy model v5.0.0). 192

As no end-to-end workflow specifically designed for Nanopore-derived cross-domain 515Y/926R 199 amplicon data was identified, benchmarking was performed against two widely used and broadly 200 applicable taxonomic profiling frameworks: QIIME2 (qiime2-amplicon-2026.1) (Bolyen et al. , 2019) and 201 Kraken2 (v2.1.3) (Wood, Lu and Langmead, 2019; Lu et al. , 2022) with Bracken (v3.0.1) (Lu et al. ,202 2017). In each case, benchmarking was performed using recommended standard analytical pathways and 203 default settings wherever possible, without parameter tuning intended to favour performance on the 204 present dataset. SILVA 138.2 SSURef NR99-based reference resources appropriate to each framework 205 were used for taxonomic classification. The same demultiplexed and primer/adaptor-trimmed sample set 206 was used for all workflow comparisons. 207 For QIIME2, reads were imported as single-end FASTQ files via a manifest and processed through the 208 recommended amplicon workflow stages for quality filtering, dereplication, chimera screening, and 209 taxonomic classification, excluding denoising steps designed for short-read Illumina datasets. Taxonomic 210 assignments were generated using the classify-sklearn naive Bayes approach, and resulting feature tables 211 were collapsed to genus and species level for downstream comparison. For Kraken2/Bracken, each sample 212 was classified using Kraken2, and genus- and species-level abundance estimates were subsequently 213 generated from Kraken2 reports using Bracken. Resulting abundance tables were exported on a per 214 sample basis for downstream comparison, and a minimum abundance threshold of 0.05% was enforced as 215 with NAP. 216 217 

In silico primer bias assessment 218 To aid interpretation of abundance distortions potentially attributable to primer–template mismatch, the 219 default 515Y/926R primer set was evaluated in silico using TestPrime against the SILVA SSU RefNR 220 138.2 database after redundancy pruning. Up to three mismatches were permitted, with no enforced zero 221 mismatch zone at the 3′ end. For taxa represented in the mock communities, we summarised perfect match 222 frequency, mean pair coverage, and the proportion of reference matches containing at least one 3′ 223 mismatch in the forward and reverse primers. These results were used descriptively to contextualise 224 observed under-representation and were not incorporated into taxonomic assignment or abundance 225 correction. 226 227

To enable comprehensive sensitivity profiling, taxa absent across all replicates were 232 assigned zero counts. 233 Detection accuracy was quantified by calculating precision, recall, and F1-scores for each replicate at a 234 fixed detection threshold (τ = 0.001) at both genus and species level, using the expected mock 235 composition at the corresponding taxonomic rank as the reference set. Together, these analyses assessed not only detection but 241 also abundance accuracy and consistency. This was restricted to taxa which were expected, meaning false 242 positives were not included in this part of the analysis. 243 Community-level similarity and sample structure were explored using β-diversity metrics. Pairwise 244 dissimilarities were calculated using both Jaccard distance (based on presence/absence data) and Bray– 245 Curtis dissimilarity (based on relative abundances). These dissimilarity matrices were visualised using 246 principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to identify clustering of replicates around their respective mock 247 community centroids. A replicate was defined as a success if it was closer in β-diversity space to its own 248 mock profile than to that of the alternate mock group. Statistical significance of replicate fidelity was 249 assessed using one-sided binomial tests for each group, with groupwise p-values aggregated via Fisher’s 250 method to test for global consistency. To investigate taxon-level variability, bar plots were generated 251 showing the expected genus abundances overlaid with individual replicate values. For each genus, the 252 coefficient of variation (CV) across replicates was annotated, with values exceeding 0.5 highlighted to 253 indicate high within-group variability. Detection sensitivity was further examined by plotting, at both 254 genus and species level, the proportion of replicates in which each expected taxon was detected against its 255 expected relative abundance. These analyses included all observed taxa, including false positives. 256 Development-stage optimisation analyses used to select the final NAP configuration, referred to as the 257 default here, are summarised in the Supplementary material. 258

At genus level, the clearest advantage of 552 NAP was the marked reduction in unexpected assignments relative to QIIME2 and Kraken2/Bracken.

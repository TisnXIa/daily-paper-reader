ABSTRACT 9In compositional next-generation sequencing (NGS) analyses (including microbiome 10 studies, RNA-seq and metagenomics) the arithmetic mean (AM) of relative proportions is 11 the default operator for summarizing feature abundances. We show that this default 12 produces unstable rankings in real compositional data. Across 102 prevalent genera in the 13 dietswap dataset (n=38 baseline samples), 23 genera (22.5%), including members of 14 

Bacteroides , Eubacterium and Bilophila , yielded opposite group-level conclusions under AM 15 and the geometric mean (GM). 16 This pattern reflects two formal properties of compositional aggregation. First, 17 AM-based rankings change with the within-sample normalization domain, whereas 18 GM-based rankings are invariant under the multiplicative structure of compositional data. 19 Second, the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation absorbs geometric averaging into the 20 data representation, so that arithmetic averaging on CLR-space recovers the GM ranking 21

IMPORTANCE 33 Studies of the gut microbiome routinely identify which bacterial groups are more or less 34 abundant in patients versus healthy controls, in different diets, or before and after a 35 treatment. The same kind of comparison underlies sequencing-based analyses across 36 biology, from gene expression to metagenomics. To do this, researchers must average the 37 abundance of each measured entity across many samples, and the standard choice is the 38 simple arithmetic average. We show that this choice can be misleading for any data where 39 each measurement is expressed relative to a sample total, as is typical of sequencing-based 40 assays, and that in real data it can flip the answer to which group is more enriched. 41 Analyzing a published dietary intervention study, we found that one in five gut bacteria 42 (including Bacteroides and Eubacterium ) gave opposite results depending on which average 43 was used. Switching to the geometric average resolves this inconsistency and makes 44 biomarker discovery more reproducible. This change is immediate to implement (it does 45 not require new software or specialized training) and applies not only to microbiome 46 studies, but to any biological measurement where what is detected, whether a gene 47 transcript, a microbial taxon, or a metabolite, is quantified relative to a sample total: 48 gene-expression analysis, metagenomics, and metabolomics among others. 49

INTRODUCTION 50 Across biology, quantitative profiling technologies share a fundamental constraint: the 51 count or intensity of each feature (a microbial taxon, a gene transcript, a metabolic 52 pathway, or a metabolite), whether produced by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, 53 RNA-seq, shotgun metagenomics, single-cell transcriptomics, or 54 mass-spectrometry-based metabolomics, is meaningful only relative to the sample total, 55 since that total is fixed by sequencing depth or extraction yield rather than by the 56 underlying biological load. Data with this property are called compositional [6, 2, 21]. 57 Within-sample (WS) normalization is therefore necessary to render abundances 58 comparable, and the choice of normalization (to total reads, to a reference feature, to a 59 spike-in standard) influences which features appear enriched and which depleted 60 [22, 3, 5, 11, 12]. 61 Beyond WS normalization, a second choice shapes the analysis: how to summarize 62 feature abundances across the samples of a group, for example when identifying which 63 microbial taxa are most enriched in a diseased cohort, which gene transcripts characterize 64 a treatment condition, or which metabolites distinguish two dietary groups. 65 Across-sample (AS) summarization typically defaults to the AM of relative abundance or 66 ratios. The AM, however, is sensitive to the total number of counts in each sample: 67 high-depth samples carry more weight in the group average, even for features whose 68 relative abundance is the same across all samples, thus introducing instability in the 69 feature ranking. The GM, by contrast, operates in log space, preserves multiplicative 70 relationships, and is invariant to within-sample rescaling [15]: a feature’s GM-based 71 group summary depends only on its relative abundance in each sample, and is unaffected 72 by differences in sequencing depth across samples. 73 The choice of averaging operator at this aggregation step is logically prior to 74 differential-abundance testing: any procedure that relies on AM-based summaries of raw 75 proportions or counts inherits their normalization dependence. Despite this, AM-based 76 summaries remain the default in much of the applied microbiome literature, and the 77 practical consequences of this default for biomarker discovery have not been 78

Throughout, our focus is on feature ranking 95 under compositional constraints, not on differential abundance inference, and we treat 96 zero handling as a separate concern that can be addressed by sparsity-aware methods 97 downstream. 98

CLR transformation absorbs geometric averaging. Applying the CLR transformation, 164 CLR (xi) = log (xi/GM (x)) , to the same dataset (panel E of Figure 2) produces 165 per-sample, scale-invariant abundances centered on the WS-GM. Across-sample 166 arithmetic means of CLR values are F1 = 0.10, F2 = −0.05, F3 = −0.06, ranking the three 167 features in the same order as AS-GM on raw counts or proportions. This is not a 168 coincidence: AS-AM on CLR-transformed values equals log (AS-GM ) up to a 169 group-specific additive constant, so CLR-based and GM-based rankings coincide exactly 170 (formal statement in Text S1). CLR therefore provides the natural arithmetic space for 171 compositional data: arithmetic averaging in CLR space is mathematically equivalent to 172 geometric averaging in the original space, and differences of CLR values correspond to 173 log fold-changes between features (CLR (xi) − CLR (xj) = log (xi/xj)), enabling 174 interpretable cross-sample comparisons. 175

DISCUSSION 275 In gut microbiome and broader compositional NGS analyses, the choice of summary 276 operator used to rank features within a group of samples is typically left implicit and 277 defaults to the arithmetic mean. We have shown that this default has consequences. 278 Theoretically, AM-based rankings change with the within-sample normalization domain 279 (Proposition 1, Text S1), so that the same dataset can yield different feature hierarchies 280 depending on whether one summarizes raw counts, proportions, or ratios to a reference 281 feature. Empirically, this instability translates into directional disagreement on real 282 microbiome data: in the dietswap baseline, 23 of 102 prevalent genera (22.5%) yielded 283 opposite group-level conclusions under AM and GM (Figure 3), and the conflict involves 284 taxa of established biological relevance for diet–microbiome interactions, including 285 

Bacteroides , Eubacterium and Bilophila . The geometric mean, by construction, is invariant to 286 within-sample normalization (Proposition 1), satisfies an exact reciprocal identity under 287 reference change (Proposition 3), and produces feature rankings that coincide exactly with 288 those obtained by arithmetic averaging on CLR-transformed data (Proposition 2; 289 Spearman ρ = 1.000 on all 102 genera in both groups). 290 These observations clarify the relationship between feature ranking and downstream 291 differential abundance (DA) testing. Established DA methods such as ANCOM-BC [10] 292

The problem analyzed here is logically prior: ensuring that the aggregation 295 operator used to summarize feature abundances within a group is itself consistent with 296 the compositional nature of NGS data. AM-based summaries violate this consistency, and 297 any inferential procedure that ingests an AM-based feature ranking inherits its instability. 298 The three-taxa illustration in Figure 5 makes this propagation explicit: under AM, log 2299 fold-changes vary with the chosen reference and the relative ordering of features by effect 300 size is not preserved; under GM, fold-changes shift by a group-constant translation under 301 reference change and their relative ordering is preserved; under CLR, a single 302 reference-free value per feature returns the same ranking as GM. 303 The practical implication for microbiome and genomics research is that biomarker 304 rankings, signatures, and effect-size comparisons should be derived from GM-based 305 summaries on raw counts or proportions, or equivalently from arithmetic averaging on 306 CLR-transformed data. This recommendation requires no additional modeling 307 assumptions and no new computational tools, and is fully compatible with existing DA 308 pipelines, which already adopt log-ratio or geometric-mean reference frames internally. 309 By removing operator-induced instability at the summarization stage, GM- and 310 CLR-based pipelines provide a reproducible foundation for cross-study comparisons and 311 meta-analyses, including the kind of microbial signature analyses we have previously 312 reported in clinical microbiome contexts [16, 17, 18]. 313 The framework developed here applies to the strictly positive subset of compositional 314 data, the regime where AM and GM aggregation differ. NGS datasets typically contain 315 structural and sampling zeros, and zero-handling strategies (pseudocounts, zero-inflated 316 models, non-parametric estimators) remain orthogonal to the operator-choice problem 317 analyzed here. The arithmetic sum on raw counts retains its biological meaning as an 318 estimate of total sequencing signal allocated to a feature, but loses this interpretation once 319 the data are normalized (Section S1.5 of Text S1). Future work integrating the GM/CLR 320 framework with sparsity-aware models would extend its applicability without altering its 321 core compositional logic. 322

Worked-example datasets 324 The illustrative datasets used in Figure 1 and Figure 2 are synthetic compositional 325 matrices adapted from the worked examples in (author?) Figure 1 uses three features (F1–F3) measured in two samples (S1, S2), with 328 integer counts chosen so that AM-based rankings of feature ratios change across 329 normalizations whereas AS-GM rankings remain invariant. Figure 2 extends the 330 construction to five samples with sequencing depths spanning three orders of magnitude 331 (S2 = 223 reads, S4 = 138,976 reads), demonstrating the same properties under extreme 332 depth heterogeneity. Empirical dataset and preprocessing 335 To validate the theoretical framework on real compositional data, we analyzed the 336 publicly available dietswap dataset [24], accessible via the microbiome R package 337 (v. 1.32.0). The dataset comprises 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing data from 222 fecal 338 samples collected from rural African (AFR) and African-American (AAM) individuals 339 undergoing a two-week dietary exchange. Analysis was restricted to the baseline 340 timepoint (n = 38 samples; AFR n = 17, AAM n = 21) to avoid within-subject correlation 341 across timepoints. Taxa with prevalence below 50% in at least one group were excluded, 342 retaining 102 genera for compositional analysis. A unit pseudocount (+1) was added to all 343 retained counts to ensure strict positivity prior to log-based transformations. The choice 344 of pseudocount = 1 is principled rather than arbitrary on count-based NGS data: it equals 345 the smallest count observable on a discrete sequencing platform, and avoids introducing 346 any a priori assumption about the relative abundance of features absent in a given sample, 347 an assumption that in practice cannot be informed from the data itself. Pseudocount 348 values smaller or larger than 1 implicitly carry such priors and are therefore not pursued. 349 To verify that the AM/GM discordance pattern is not an artefact of pseudocount 350

Compositional aggregation and CLR transformation 356 For each feature i across n samples within a group, the across-sample arithmetic and 357 geometric means were computed as AS-AM (xi) = ( 1/ n) ∑k xi,k and 358 AS-GM (xi) = ( ∏k xi,k)1/ n, respectively. Within-sample normalizations applied to the 359 empirical and worked-example datasets included: (i) raw counts; (ii) proportions, 360 obtained by dividing each count by its within-sample total; and (iii) ratios to a chosen 361 reference feature (F1 or F2 in the worked examples; the corresponding genera in the 362 three-taxa empirical illustration). The centered log-ratio transformation was computed 363 sample-wise as CLR (xi,k) = log (xi,k/WS-GM (x·,k)) , where WS-GM (x·,k) is the geometric 364 mean over all features in sample k. Formal statements and proof sketches of the 365 underlying invariance properties are provided in Text S1. 366

The same pipeline was repeated 373 for GM-based versus CLR-based rankings.

Abstract 24 

Residential indoor surfaces are recognized as diverse microbial ecosystems, while their 25 

genome-based organization and functional repertoires remain understudied. We recovered 26 

2304 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from shotgun metagenomic sequencing of 27 

10 houses in New Delhi, India. Genome-resolved analysis revealed a highly structured 28 

microbial community and substantial unexplored diversity, with 60% Species-level 29 

Genome Bins (SGBs) (629/1014) unclassified at the species level. Metabolism reveals a 30 

conserved metabolic core, along with spatial functional enrichment: the living area was 31 

significantly different from the bathroom and kitchen areas. The prevalent MAG species 32 

of the house microbiome, Paracoccus marcusii , Ottowia sp. 018060485, and Kocuria 33 

palustris , showed strain-level diversity with no stratification by house, but a subtle 34 

location-wise grouping. Potential pathogens, along with a wide range of antimicrobial 35 

resistance genes (ARGs), were identified across the MAGs, with 64 ARGs associated with 36 

mobile elements. Phylogenomic analysis of Escherichia coli MAGs indicated a split 37 

between commensal-like fecal lineages and pathotype-associated clusters, like Intestinal 38 

Pathogenic E. coli (InPEC). These results suggest that residential house microbiomes 39 

harbor microbial communities with both diverse metabolic capacity and clinical relevance. 40 

Together, these findings establish a reference for future indoor microbiome research and 41 

provide a foundation for antimicrobial resistance surveillance and the development of bio-42 

informed building-infrastructures. 43

1. Introduction 47 

Indoor environments constitute complex and dynamic microbial ecosystems. The house 48 

microbiome comprises a diverse community of bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses 1.49 

Unlike other environments, residential spaces constitute a unique microbial meeting 50 

ground as microbes from outdoor environments (air, water, and soil) converge with 51 

additional inputs from human skin shedding and hair 1. These microbial communities are 52 

influenced by factors such as building design, human occupancy, pet presence, materials 53 

used, and proximity to green spaces 2,3 . These dynamic ecosystems shape human microbial 54 

exposure, modulate immune development, and facilitate the transmission of pathogens and 55 

antimicrobial resistance determinants 1,4–7 . With the increase in urbanization, contemporary 56 

building designs have isolated residential spaces from outdoor environments, shifting the 57 

balance between environmental microbes and human-associated taxa, plausibly altering 58 

the health-relevant microbial landscape of homes 8–11 .59 

The residential microbiome has already been shown to harbor substantial biological 60 

diversity. In the Indian house microbiome study, more than 1,400 bacterial species, over 61 

100 viral groups, and 669 antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG) subtypes spanning 22 62 

classes have been reported 12 . In addition, ESKAPE pathogens and opportunistic strains 63 

such as Escherichia coli have been detected on household floors across different sites, 64 

underscoring the need to examine these communities in the context of human health 13 .65 

Amplicon and short-read metagenomic approaches suffer in terms of strain resolution and 66 

the association of function with taxa, which are crucial for understanding the survival of 67 

organisms in resource-limited environments 14,15 . Reconstructing metagenome-assembled 68 

genomes (MAGs) bridges this gap through metabolic characterization and by linking 69

In this study, we recovered genomes from shotgun metagenomic data generated from 74 

Indian residential samples to assess the functional profiles and clinical implications of 75 

indoor microbial communities. A total of 90 samples from 9 locations across 10 houses 76 

were analyzed to: (i) expand the genomic representation of indoor residential floor-77 

associated microbes; (ii) elucidate metabolic potential related to carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, 78 

and iron cycling; (iii) assess the presence of potential pathogens, prevalence and genomic 79 

context of antimicrobial resistance genes in the house microbiome; and (iv) explore the 80 

phylogenomic diversity of Escherichia coli . Together, these analyses provide genome-81 

resolved insight into the ecological potential and health relevance of residential 82 

microbiomes. 83

Phylogenomic analysis of the E. coli MAGs reveals affiliation with fecal commensal 280 

and pathotype strains 281 

Of the 10 E. coli MAGs recovered from the house microbiome, 8 met the MIMAG 282 

standards for near-complete bins and were used for subsequent analyses. These MAGs 283 

were distributed across multiple household areas, including the drawing room, foyer, 284 

bedroom, kitchen sink, and shower area ( Supplementary Fig. 10 ). To infer the likely 285 

origin of the recovered Escherichia coli MAGs, we performed a phylogenomic analysis 286 

using a curated reference set of pathogenic, commensal, and environmental strains 11 .287 

Whole-genome phylogenetic analysis of E. coli MAGs recovered from the house 288 

microbiome revealed a dichotomy in the evolutionary origins and pathogenic potential of 289 

the house E. coli MAGs ( Fig. 8 ). The resulting tree placed the eight MAGs within the 290 

broader E. coli phylogeny, rather than as a distinct house-specific lineage. Importantly, 291 

MAGs originating from different locations within the houses appeared to be intermixed 292 

within the tree and mostly belonged to one of the two major phylogroups of E. coli : group 293 

A and group B1. Five MAGs (A18_bin.33, A74_bin.8, A9_bin.17, A37_bin.50, and 294 

A99_bin.11) had close genetic relationships with the reference InPEC strains, while three 295 

(A79_bin.142, A82_bin.3, and A80_bin.3). had similarities with commensal fecal isolates 296 

The closely related commensal strains include ECOR01 and CIP61.11 while the 297 

pathogenic strains include 101-1(InPEC), and 55989(In-PEC) 19 . None of the MAGs 298 

clustered with the environmental strains, negating their environmental origin and 299 

indicating a plausible fecal origin. 300

Methods 530 

Study design and Sample collection 531 

This study analyzed 90 shotgun metagenomic swab samples collected from 10 urban 532 

houses in New Delhi, India, comprising five houses with cemented floors and five with 533 

tiled floors. The houses were distributed across different regions of New Delhi and were 534 

occupied by 2–4 residents each. Samples were collected from nine household locations, 535 

including the foyer, bedroom, drawing room, shower area, toilet area, bathroom sink, 536 

kitchen floor, kitchen slab, and kitchen sink, one day after routine cleaning and before the 537 

next cleaning cycle. The sequencing data used for metagenomic analysis and MAG 538 

recovery were obtained from BioProject PRJNA1053252 and comprised a total of 800.8 539 

Gb of Illumina NovaSeq 6000, 2x150 paired-end data, with per-sample yields ranging from 540 

2.68 to 79.059 Gb. Human reads were removed using Bowtie2, which aligned each sample 541 

to the hg37 human reference genome. The sample with the maximum number of human 542 

reads was A90, in which 57% reads were mapped to the Hg37 reference genome, and the 543 

sample that had the minimum host DNA content was A88, in which only 182 reads were 544 

aligned to Hg37. The sample yield ranged from 1.4 Gbp to 73.12 Gbp in the A17 and A79 545 

samples, respectively. 546

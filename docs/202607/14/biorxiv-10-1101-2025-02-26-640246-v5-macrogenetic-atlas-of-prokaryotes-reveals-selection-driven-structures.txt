Abstract 18 Macrogenetics investigates patterns and predictors of intraspecific genetic variation across diverse 19 taxa, offering a framework for understanding species diversity and addressing evolutionary 20 hypotheses. Here, we present a macrogenetic atlas of prokaryotes (MAP), integrating genomic 21 data from 15,235 prokaryotic species (30 parameters in 12 categories) and population genetic 22 data from 786 species (22 parameters in 7 categories), together with phylogenetic, phenotypic, and 23 ecological information. MAP enables quantitative species characterization, straightforward 24 cross-species comparisons, and easy generation, testing, and refinement of evolutionary 25 hypotheses. For example, our analyses show that long- and short-range genetic linkage capture 26 distinct evolutionary dynamics and independently shape genetic diversity. Further, we propose that 27 as within-species diversity increases, epistatic selection increasingly contributes to shaping genetic 28 diversity, exemplified by convergent selection-driven structures (ecospecies) identified in 29 

Streptococcus mitis and S. oralis . Overall, MAP represents a widely applicable resource 30 (www.genomap.cn) and offers novel insights into the drivers of macroevolution and the life cycle 31 of prokaryotic species. 32 33

Introduction 34 Life is diverse, and its understanding requires macro-scale frameworks that capture species 35 diversity, akin to maps guiding exploration. Since Linnaeus, taxonomy has provided this 36 foundation based on phenotypic classification, and is now being systematically updated by the 37 addition of genomic data, for example in the Tree of Life project 1. The integration of genotype, 38 phenotype, and ecological data remains the major unresolved challenge and is essential for 39 characterizing species and identifying the forces driving diversification and speciation. Within 40 microbiology, classical resources such as Bergey’s Manual , which integrates taxonomy with 41 phenotypic and ecological information 2, have long served this purpose. 42 43 Incorporating within-species genomic variation data is the next critical step. Advances in 44 high-throughput sequencing have produced extensive genomic resources across many taxa 3,4 . With 45 their rapid accumulation, the time is now ripe for systematic integration under a new framework, 46 macrogenetics, which embraces broad taxonomic, spatial and temporal scales across dozens to 47 thousands of species 5,6 . Macrogenetics provides a framework for exploring species diversity and 48 testing long-standing evolutionary hypotheses. For example, community-level species richness has 49 been shown to correlate positively with within-population genetic diversity in fish and mammals 7,8 ,50 supporting the species-genetic diversity correlation (SGDC) concept 9. Other studies have linked 51 genetic diversity to life-history traits such as fecundity and propagule size 10 , and revealed 52 latitudinal gradients in genetic diversity 11 .53 54 Prokaryotes make up more biomass than all other organisms except plants 12 . Furthermore, a single 55 species often contains lineages adapted to multiple niches, differing in host range, pathogenicity 56 and nutrient use, as exemplified by Escherichia coli 13 . This contrasts with eukaryotes, where 57 within-species differentiation and specialization are typically limited. Such pervasive intraspecific 58 diversity in prokaryotes raises fundamental questions: how are ecological niches partitioned, and 59 how do phenotypic and niche diversity shape genomic and population genetic features? These 60 differences also imply that differentiation and speciation in prokaryotes are driven by forces 61 distinct from those shaping eukaryotes 14 , but these remain poorly understood. 62 63 Here we present the Macrogenetic Atlas of Prokaryotes (MAP), an open resource 64 (www.genomap.cn) that integrates large-scale genomic and population genetic data with 65 phylogenetic, phenotypic and ecological information. MAP assigns each species a quantitative 66 “position” based on ranked features across species, enabling quantitative characterization and 67 straightforward cross-species comparisons. It also serves as an empirical resource for generating, 68 testing, and refining evolutionary hypotheses. Overall, MAP provides a framework for exploring 69 and understanding prokaryotic diversity. 70

To ensure robustness, only 81 PGPs with low variability (standard deviation/median < 0.5) across random-sampling replicates 82 were retained, yielding estimates highly consistent with full datasets (correlation coefficient r >83 0.8 for all but one parameter; h/m , r = 0.68) and independent of sample size (Supplementary Figs. 84 2-4). Metadata and key process outputs, including whole-genome 91 alignments and variation profiles, are available as community resources. Quantitative characterization of prokaryotic species 95 First, MAP quantitatively characterizes prokaryotic species by ranking GPs and PGPs relative to 96 all available species ( Fig. 2a,b ). For example, it confirms that the human pathogen Helicobacter 97 

pylori is highly recombinogenic 15 , with recombination-derived diversity (‘recombination 98 coverage’, Rec. c 16 ) in the top 1% and short-range (10 bp) linkage disequilibrium (LD, inversely 99 correlated with recombination) in the bottom 1%. This quantitative characterization also revises 100 prevailing views. For example, the widely distributed E. coli , naturally presumed to have a large 101 effective population size ( Ne) and the correspondingly high diversity 13 , instead shows moderately 102 high but not extreme N e (>71% species; estimated from dN/dS , a negative proxy for N e and 103 selection efficacy in many microbial studies 17 ) and intermediate genetic diversity (>39% species). 104 105 

Defining range of prokaryotic diversity 106 Second, MAP delineates the range of genomic features across prokaryotes ( Fig. 2c ). Overall patterns of prokaryotic genomes 125 Third, MAP reveals overall patterns of prokaryotic genomes ( Fig. 2c ). Archaea and bacteria show 126 broadly similar GP distributions, except that archaeal genes are generally shorter. Most parameters 127 follow roughly normal or right-skewed distributions. In contrast, GC content and codon bias index 128 (CBI) are distinctly bimodal, with GC likely reflecting ancient adaptive legacies 24 , and CBI 129 strongly influenced by GC 25 . Bacterial genome size approximates a normal distribution, whereas 130 core- and pan-gene counts are bimodal. The distribution of core-genes likely reflects different 131 outcomes of an evolutionary tradeoff but with the constraint that a minimum number of genes is 132 needed for cellular function. 133 134 

Easy testing and refining evolutionary hypotheses 135 Fourth, MAP systematically quantified correlations among parameters ( r for quantitative traits, η136 for categorical traits) and their relationships with phylogeny (Pagel’s λ), phenotype, and ecology 137 (Fig. 3a ). Although these correlations do not necessarily imply causal relationships, the resulting 138 standardized correlation matrices provide an empirical resource for testing and refining 139 evolutionary hypotheses. 140 141 

Determinants of genetic diversity 142 Our understanding of the determinants of genetic diversity remains limited. Ne, mutation, and 143 recombination have been proposed as major contributors 26 . While partly tested in eukaryotes, no 144 equivalent dataset existed for prokaryotes because it is challenging to directly measure these 145 factors. Two MAP parameters most closely related to these proposed contributors are dN/dS (r-adj 146 = -0.76, inversely reflecting N e) and the recombination-to-mutation ratio ( r/m , r-adj = 0.32), a 147 proxy for recombination 27 . Both correlated with synonymous diversity πs in the expected direction, 148 providing preliminary empirical support for their roles in shaping prokaryotic genetic diversity 149 (Fig. 3b ). 150 151 MAP also highlighted ecological associations with genetic diversity. Plant-root-associated species 152 exhibited the highest πs, consistent with nutrient-rich conditions sustaining large populations, 153 whereas fermented-food species showed the lowest, reflecting artificial selection and bottlenecks. 154 Diversity also increased with temperature ( r-adj = 0.24), consistent with previously reported 155 latitudinal diversity gradients in both bacteria 28,29 and eukaryotes 11 (Fig. 3b ). 156 157 

Underexplored correlations 158 MAP revealed previously overlooked patterns ( Fig. 3c ). Beyond confirming the established 159 positive relationship between prokaryotic genome size and selection efficacy 17 , evidenced by the 160 negative correlation between genome size and dN/dS (r-adj = -0.22, a negative proxy for selection 161 efficacy), MAP further revealed an even stronger negative correlation between gene length and 162 

dN/dS (r-adj = -0.31). This pattern suggest that, analogous to genome size, stronger selection may 163 favor longer genes. Gene length also correlated positively with coding proportion ( r-adj = 0.48), 164 possibly because regulatory non-coding regions remain relatively constant regardless of gene size. 165

Overview of prokaryotic genetic diversity 170 Finally, we illustrate how MAP can refine existing theoretical frameworks and generate new 171 hypotheses. We focused on the relationship between LD and genetic diversity, summarized in a 172 two-dimensional triangular plot, since short-range (10 bp) LD is intrinsically constrained to be 173 higher than long-range (10 kb) LD ( Fig. 4a ). First, nearly the whole triangle is occupied, implying 174 short- and long-range LD vary largely independently. Second, πs showed a strong negative 175 correlation with short-range LD ( r-adj = -0.58) but no significant correlation with long-range LD. 176 Instead, for species with similar short-range LD, there is a pronounced positive correlation 177 between long-range LD and πs (Fig. 4a ), contrasting with the theoretical expectation that stronger 178 genetic linkage should be associated with lower genetic diversity 26 . Together, these observations 179 suggest that short- and long-range LD capture distinct aspects of evolutionary dynamics. 180 181 These qualitative patterns can be interpreted within a conceptual framework defined by the joint 182 effects of two variables: “species age”, reflected in genetic diversity, and barriers to gene flow 183 (with non-recombining clonal species representing a complete barrier), reflected in long-range LD 184 (Fig. 4b ). In this framework, as species accumulate age (indicated by increased πs) in the absence 185 of barriers, long-range LD decays rapidly, whereas short-range LD equilibrates more gradually, 186 ultimately toward panmixia with high diversity and little LD at any distance ( Fig. 4b , top arrows). 187 By contrast, barriers to recombination slow the decay of long-range LD, raising its equilibrium 188 level ( Fig. 4b , middle and bottom arrows). Because LD decays more slowly in the presence of 189 gene flow barriers, species positioned along a given vertical line in Fig. 4a tend to be “older” for 190 higher long-range LD values ( Fig. 4b ), thereby explaining the positive association with πs along 191 each vertical line. 192 193 Although simplified, for example because species-wide bottlenecks can reduce diversity and 194 elevate LD, thereby making species appear “younger”, this two-dimensional framework provides a 195 useful lens for interpreting species-specific LD and genetic diversity configurations. 196 197 The framework is consistent with several well-characterized species. A species with low barriers 198 to genetic exchange is Vibrio parahaemolyticus (Fig. 4a,c ), which exhibits low long-range LD but 199 moderate diversity and correspondingly moderate short-range LD. Previous analyses suggested 200 that its diversity remains moderate because population size expanded only recently, and if 201 maintained over longer timescales, diversity would increase considerably, driving the species 202 toward panmixia 32 .203 204 A species with well-recognized barriers to genetic exchange is E. coli (Fig. 4a,c ), where 205 recombination between phylogroups is constrained by homology-dependent mechanisms 33 ,206 resulting in higher long-range LD than V. parahaemolyticus . Another example is H. pylori , a 207 species notorious for its exceptionally high rates of recombination during mixed infection 15 , which 208 have led to very low short-range LD. Role of epistatic selection in shaping population structure 228 One possible explanation for the paradoxical absence of true panmictic species is that species with 229 low barriers to exchange systematically evolve stronger barriers as species age and accumulate 230 genetic diversity. V. parahaemolyticus illustrates this process: in addition to weak geographic 231 barriers generating oceanic gene pools 36 , further barriers arise from epistatic selection. Specifically, 232 two major ecospecies are highly differentiated at ~1% of the core genome, reflecting strong 233 selection against gene flow in these regions 37,38 . If functional diversification expands over time, 234 epistatic selection could progressively create barriers to exchange across larger genomic regions. V. 235 

parahaemolyticus would then not evolve into a panmictic species but instead come to resemble the 236 structured, high-diversity species that we have observed. 237 238 We therefore propose that epistatic selection is one mechanism that could contribute to the absence 239 of panmictic species, although it is unlikely to be the only one. Specifically, we hypothesize that 240 panmictic bacteria are absent because, in species maintaining large Ne, inevitable functional 241 diversification driven by epistatic selection progressively generates long-range LD. This predicts 242 stronger signatures of selection in the genetic regions responsible for population structure in “older” 243 species with higher genetic diversity. 244 245 To evaluate whether this prediction is consistent with the data, we performed principal component 246 analysis (PCA) for each species with >100 genomes and identified SNPs most strongly associated 247 with the first principal component (top 1% by loading values; PC1-related SNPs), representing the 248 genetic basis of major population structure 39 . As a proxy for selection, we quantified the 249 enrichment of non-synonymous sites among these SNPs relative to the genomic background, using 250 the N/S (non-synonymous to synonymous site) ratio. 251 252

Identification of convergent ecospecies 260 Although the proposed framework remains to be rigorously tested, it provides a practical strategy 261 for identifying selection-driven population structures, such as ecospecies, which are characterized 262 by fixed differences in a small fraction of the genome, but free genetic exchange elsewhere 37,38,40 .263 Guided by this framework, we searched for signatures of ecospecies in highly diverse species, 264 asking whether any had progressed further along the road to functional differentiation than V. 265 

parahaemolyticus .266 267 To this end, we examined species with higher diversity and many genomes (>100 genomes). Two 268 such species are Streptococcus mitis and S. oralis . The PCA loading distribution for both 269 resembled that seen for previous ecospecies 37,38,40 , showing strong differentiation in specific 270 genomic regions together with high gene flow elsewhere ( Fig. 5a,b ). Furthermore, there is strong 271 enrichment of genes of particular categories with extreme PC1 loadings. For both species, COG 272 categories D (cell cycle control, cell division, chromosome partitioning) and M (cell 273 wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis), and KEGG category ko03036 (chromosome and associated 274 proteins) were involved, suggesting functional divergence in cell division and biogenesis. The 275 convergence of the differentiation signal is further supported by a correlation of 0.41 between PC1 276 gene loadings in the two species, together with substantial overlap among genes with extreme PC1 277 loadings in the D, M and ko03036 categories ( Fig. 5c ). 278 279 To characterize these putative ecospecies, we constructed phylogenetic trees based on SNPs with 280 extreme loadings. We found two putative ecospecies for S. mitis and three for S. oralis , which also 281 correspond to distinct clusters in strain PCA loadings ( Fig. 5a,b ). To determine whether they had 282 evolved independently, we constructed a joint tree for the shared high loading genes, which 283 implies independent origin of ecospecies in the two species ( Fig. 5c ). Finally, we constructed 284 phylogenetic trees based on remaining SNPs and all SNPs to examine the pattern of differentiation 285 in the rest of the genome. Distinct ecospecies clusters are visible, implying modest genome-wide 286 differentiation, which contrasts with the absence of differentiation in most of the genome seen in V. 287 

parahaemolyticus and H. pylori 37,40 . This result is consistent with the Streptococcus ecospecies 288 being older and further along the path of functional differentiation and helps to explain the higher 289 long-range LD seen in these two species. Together, these findings demonstrate how MAP can 290 generate evolutionary hypotheses and facilitate the discovery of selection-driven structures, 291 exemplified here by convergent ecospecies.. 292 293 

Discussion 294 Exploring and understanding the diversity of life requires a framework akin to a geographical map. 295 For prokaryotes, classical resources such as Bergey’s Manual have long served as the initial “map”. 296

MAP extends this foundation with a genome-based atlas, capturing rich genomic and population 297 genetic features across prokaryotic species. It is accessible online, enabling straightforward 298 exploration and use. 299 300 MAP serves both focused and broad research needs. For researchers interested in particular 301 microbes, it provides concise summaries of genomic, population genetic, phenotypic, and 302 ecological features. Beyond single species, it facilitates straightforward comparisons across all 303 available prokaryotes and serves as a resource for macrogenomic and population genetic studies, 304 including both raw and curated non-redundant datasets. The current MAP is primarily based on 305 genomes from isolated strains, and future versions will incorporate metagenome-assembled 306 genomes to expand species coverage and genomic diversity. 307 308 Our analysis of MAP has generated a number of interesting observations that deserve further study. 309 For example, while bacterial genome size approximates a normal distribution, core- and pan-gene 310 counts are unexpectedly bimodal. Gut-dominant Bacteroides , such as B. uniformis and B. fragilis, 311 have exceptionally high genome fluidity. Moreover, we confirm that selection favors bigger 312 genomes 17 , and also find a negative correlation between gene length and dN/dS , suggesting that 313 selection also favors long genes. 314 315 Our analyses and resources are available based on two taxonomic classifications, namely NCBI 316 and Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) 41,42 . The problem of bacterial species definitions 317 remains unsolved and large databases inevitably contain errors 43,44 . NCBI is based on user 318 inputted classifications, while GTDB uses an automatic algorithm, based on a universal average 319 nucleotide identity cutoff of 0.95. While GTDB has advantages of consistency and quality control 320 over NCBI, it often splits taxonomically diverse named species into multiple putative species 321 (Supplementary Fig. 6). In many cases, these species do not correspond to coherent evolutionary 322 units. In particular, highly diverse species such as P. fluorescens have very low short-range LD, 323 indicating that gene flow is effective in distributing diversity within the species, and accordingly 324 they are among the best examples of biological species, despite being split into >10 species by 325 GTDB. Therefore, GTDB is not especially well-suited for macrogenomic analysis, especially for 326 highly diverse species, and we presented our main results based on NCBI classification. We 327 nevertheless provide an alternative version of parameter calculation and correlation analysis 328 results based on GTDB (Supplementary Tables 7-9), and show a strong correlation between 329 parameter relationships derived from two taxonomies ( r = 0.96, Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating 330 that the great majority of our results are robust to species definitions. 331 332 The correlation matrix integrating genomic, population genetic, phenotypic, and ecological 333 parameters provides a resource-level overview that facilitates hypothesis generation and testing, 334 thereby helping to refine existing theoretical frameworks. These correlations, however, do not 335 necessarily imply causal relationships and may include false positives arising from multiple 336 testing or sensitivity to outliers; they should therefore be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, 337 they serve as a valuable starting point for hypothesis-driven and statistically rigorous follow-up 338 analyses. 339 340

As an illustration, motivated by the observed lack of correlation between short- and long-range 341 genetic linkage, we propose and provide evidence that these two linkage scales capture distinct 342 aspects of evolutionary dynamics and independently shape genetic diversity. Classical population 343 genetic theory predicts a negative correlation between genetic diversity and genetic linkage level 26 .344 We indeed find that short-range LD is strongly inversely correlated with genetic diversity. 345 However, there is a pronounced positive correlation in MAP between long-range LD and πs for 346 species exhibiting similar levels of short-range LD ( Fig. 4a ). We propose that this pattern can be 347 interpreted by considering the joint effects of “species age” and barriers to gene flow. Because 348 long-range LD principally reflects barriers to gene flow, it decays more slowly across all genetic 349 distances ( Fig. 4b ). Consequently, among species with similar short-range LD values, those with 350 higher long-range LD tend to be older and more diverse. Although simplified, this conceptual 351 framework is consistent with several well-characterized species and provides a useful basis for 352 generating testable evolutionary hypotheses. 353 354 There is one zone in the triangle that is surprisingly empty, corresponding to panmictic species 355 with low LD at all distances ( Fig. 4a ). Many bacteria occupy niches favorable to global dispersal 356 and the maintenance of vast, stable populations over evolutionary timescales and this should 357 generate panmictic, i.e., freely mixed species 32 . Our resolution of this paradox is that as 358 within-species diversity increases, functional diversification driven by epistatic selection 359 progressively generates barriers to gene flow. Consistent with this, we identified stronger 360 signatures of selection structuring diversity in more genetically diverse species ( Fig. 4e ). Thus, 361 while there may be species that start on the journey to panmixia, with large population sizes, weak 362 geographic barriers and free recombination (as exemplified by V. parahaemolyticus ), they may 363 progressively acquire barriers to gene flow due to epistatic selection as they age, and end up with 364 intermediate long-range LD. Together, these observations are consistent with epistatic selection as 365 one mechanism that could contribute to this process, although it is unlikely to be the only one. 366 Other processes, including geographic isolation and intrinsic barriers to genetic exchange 33,34 , are 367 also likely to play important roles. 368 369 An important implication of our findings is that we can gain insight into the ecological pressures 370 acting on genetically diverse prokaryotic species, simply by characterizing how their diversity is 371 structured. One example of structure generated by selection is bacterial ecospecies 37,38,40 . We have 372 previously described the first two bacterial ecospecies in V. parahaemolyticus and H. pylori. In V. 373 

parahaemolyticus the structure reflects diversification at motility-related genes 37,38 , while in H. 374 

pylori differentiation has occurred in genes related to immune escape and nutrient uptake 40 . Both 375 species have moderate diversity and ecospecies structure is restricted to a small fraction of the 376 genome, representing an early stage of diversification. 377 378 Guided by this framework, MAP provides a practical strategy for identifying additional candidate 379 ecospecies. Here we have identified two additional species with ecospecies-like structure, namely 380 

S. mitis and S. oralis . These two sister taxa both inhabit the mouths of humans and other animals 381 and there is a substantial convergence in the genetic basis of the ecospecies structure, with the 382 same cell division and biogenesis associated genome categories and genes involved. These suggest 383 similar ecological driving forces and/or adaptive potentials in both species, which might reflect the 384

Methods 400 

Genome datasets 401 

Deduplication and quality control 402 We retrieved all assembled genomes (n=317,542) included in the Genome Taxonomy Database 403 (GTDB; https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org, release 207) 41,42 . We performed genome deduplication 404 based on BioSample accessions, retaining only the genome with the highest N50 value for each 405 BioSample. Only high-quality genomes (n=268,463) with estimated completeness ≥90% and 406 contamination ≤5%, as assessed by CheckM v1.1.3 45 , were retained for subsequent analyses. 407 408 The high-quality genomes were assigned to species based on both National Center for 409 Biotechnology Information (NCBI) taxonomy and GTDB classifications. For each species, the 410 earliest sequenced complete genome, or alternatively the genome with the highest N50 value (if no 411 complete genome was available), was designated as the reference genome. All genomes of a 412 species were aligned to the corresponding reference genome using MUMmer v3.23 46 to generate 413 whole-genome alignments, as previously described 36 , and only genomes with ≥60% alignment 414 coverage against the reference were retained for subsequent analyses. 415 416 After deduplication and quality control, a total of 266,627 high-quality genomes were retained for 417 downstream analyses, and their genome quality metrics and taxonomic classifications are provided 418 in Supplementary Table 1. The whole-genome alignments for each species are publicly available 419 from the Zenodo database (https://zenodo.org/records/17430471). 420 421 

Representative datasets and clonal groups 422 While robust estimation of population genetic parameters requires randomly sampled 423 representative datasets, biased repeat sampling ( e.g ., strains from disease outbreaks) is prevalent in 424 public genomic datasets, especially for pathogenic species. To reduce the effects of repeat 425 sampling, we generated representative genome datasets as previously described 47 .

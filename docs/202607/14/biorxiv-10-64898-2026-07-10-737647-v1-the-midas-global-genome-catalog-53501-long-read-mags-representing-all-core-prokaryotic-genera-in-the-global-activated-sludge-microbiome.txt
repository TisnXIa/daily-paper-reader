## Abstract 19 

Wastewater treatment relies on complex microbial communities, yet existing genome-resolved references 20 for this essential engineered ecosystem remain dominated by short-read assemblies, limiting genome 21 contiguity and linkage between taxonomic and metabolic function. We applied long-read sequencing to 22 activated sludge from 83 globally distributed plants, reconstructing 53,501 metagenome-assembled 23 genomes to establish the Microbial Database of Activated Sludge (MiDAS) global genome catalog. The 24 catalog encompasses high-quality genomes for 12,047 prokaryotic species, 82% of which are not 25 represented in GTDB release 226, and provides a median of 32 high-quality genomes for each of the 250 26 core prokaryotic genera previously defined in our MiDAS global 16S rRNA gene survey. This enables 27 analyses of predicted functional traits and their ecological context, for example, we identified two sparsely 28 represented Nitrospiraceae genera with conserved nitrite-oxidation genes that are abundant in higher-29 temperature wastewater treatment plants. In summary, the MiDAS genome catalog provides a framework 30 for linking taxonomy, metabolism and ecological roles in wastewater treatment systems globally. 31

## Introduction 32 

Diverse microbial communities drive activated sludge (AS) processes in wastewater treatment plants 33 (WWTPs), and are responsible for nutrient removal (e.g., carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus), protection of 34 downstream habitats, and, increasingly, recovery of resources and energy 1. Culture-independent molecular 35 methods, particularly 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and shotgun metagenomics, have been applied 36 to these essential engineered ecosystems for decades and substantially advanced our understanding of 37 WWTP taxa and community dynamics 2–6 . A recent large-scale metagenomic study has begun to provide 38 genome-resolved references for global WWTP microbiomes 7, but this resource is based on short-read 39 assemblies, limiting genome contiguity and the linkage between taxonomic identity and metabolic 40 function 8.41 42 The Microbial Database of Activated Sludge (MiDAS) project was developed to summarize knowledge 43 about the physiology and ecology of functionally important microorganisms in activated sludge plants, 44 anaerobic digesters, and related wastewater treatment systems 4,9 . This includes a 16S rRNA gene-based 45 global survey of 740 WWTPs across 31 countries 10 . From this survey, we identified 965 ecologically 46 important genera based on their relative abundance and ubiquity, with a subset of 250 core genera predicted 47 to be central to AS functionality 10 . With the advent of high-throughput genome-resolved metagenomics 11– 

48 15 , it is now possible to extend MiDAS with a genome catalog, which we recently initiated using Danish 49 WWTPs 16 . Long-read sequencing is particularly attractive for this purpose because it yields more 50 contiguous metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), reduces the risk of contamination and mis-binning, 51 and improves metabolic reconstructions 8. Here, we extend our long-read MAG sequencing of AS 52 ecosystems to 83 WWTPs across 27 countries to construct a comprehensive genome catalog of the global 53 WWTP microbiome. This resource provides genome-resolved insights into the metabolic potential of core 54 taxa, facilitates linking community composition to environmental and operational conditions, and reveals 55 previously unrecognized nitrifying lineages that occasionally dominate nitrifier communities. 56 

## Results 57 

Recovery of 53,501 MAGs from MiDAS global survey samples 58 

To construct the MiDAS global genome catalog, we generated metagenomic datasets from 83 of 740 59 samples previously collected for the MiDAS global amplicon survey 10 . This subset is broadly representative 60 of the survey encompassing 83 WWTPs across 75 cities, 27 countries, and five continents ( Fig. 1a ). Of 61 these, 70 samples were from AS plants, 47 of which used advanced process configurations ( Fig. 1a and 62 

Supplementary Table S1) . Each sample was sequenced using an entire PromethION flow cell (R10.4.1), 63 yielding 11.3 Tbp of nanopore long reads >1 kbp. Reads had a median length of 6.1 kbp (IQR: 5.2-7.3 kbp), 64 median read identity of 98.4% (Q18.0), and a median sequencing depth of 138 Gbp per sample (IQR: 127-65 155 Gbp) ( Fig. S1 ). 66 67 Each of the 83 datasets was assembled individually, producing 433 Gbp of contigs with a median read 68 recruitment of 88.3% (IQR: 86.2-89.6%; Supplementary Table S2 ). Contigs from each sample were then 69 binned, producing a total of 53,501 MAGs. These included 22,277 high-quality (HQ) MAGs with a median 70 estimated completeness of 96.8% (IQR: 94.0-99.0%) and contamination of 0.9% (IQR: 0.3-2.0%; Fig. 1b,c ). 71

Genome dereplication clustered the 22,277 MiDAS HQ MAGs into 12,047 species representatives (11,995 93 bacterial and 52 archaeal). Relative to GTDB (r226), these species span 80 known phyla, 193 classes, 504 94 orders, 1,056 families, and 2,560 genera. Most species are novel (82%; 9,912), yet the species accumulation 95 curve is far from saturation ( Fig. 2b ), consistent with estimates that the great majority of prokaryotic species 96 remain unsequenced 34 . The distinct trajectory observed when MQ MAGs were included suggests that these 97 genomes recover additional low-abundance diversity not captured among HQ genomes. Notably, 4,437 98 MQ-only species were recurrently detected across at least five samples and at least two non-source WWTPs 99 while remaining below 1% maximum abundance. 100 101 To assess how much phylogenetic diversity MiDAS adds beyond rank-based novelty estimates, we 102 integrated the catalog into a custom GTDB-derived HQ bacterial reference tree. Adding MiDAS HQ species 103 increases total branch lengths by 26.0% and the number of HQ species representatives by 29.7% ( Fig. S4 104 

and Supplementary Note ). This expansion reflected both improved representation of known taxa and the 105 recovery of previously unrepresented lineages. Among known taxa, the catalog provides the first HQ 106 species representatives for 3 phyla, 18 classes, 76 orders, 266 families, and 1,256 genera and at least doubles 107 the number of HQ species diversity in a further 750 genera ( Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S6 ). 108 Additionally, MiDAS adds HQ representatives for novel lineages at higher taxonomic ranks, including two 109 classes, 21 orders, 104 families, and 1,040 genera that had no genomic representation in GTDB ( Fig. S5 110

The MiDAS global genome catalog represents the global activated sludge microbiome 114 

To quantify how well the MiDAS catalog represents global AS microbial communities, we first mapped 115 reads from the 83 metagenomes used to construct the catalog back to MiDAS species-representative 116 genomes and to the full non-dereplicated MiDAS genome catalog ( Fig. 3b and Fig. S6 ). The MiDAS HQ 117 species representatives captured a median of 57.6% of microbial reads (IQR: 52.9-61.2%), and inclusion 118 of MQ species representatives increased median recruitment to 67.6% (IQR: 64.9-70.4%). The full non-119 dereplicated MiDAS catalog (n=53,501) further increased median read recruitment to 77.6% (IQR: 74.5-120 81.3%), where the increase suggests strain-level genomic diversity that may vary across WWTPs and 121 indicates that the catalog captures most genomes detectable in the AS metagenomes generated in this study. 122 123 We then compared this representation with GTDB reference and sample-specific datasets. The GTDB HQ 124 set provided limited coverage of most AS metagenomes (median: 13.0%, IQR: 8.0-19.7%). European 125 samples showed relatively higher recruitment (median: 21.3%), which reflects the >1,000 HQ MAGs from 126 Danish WWTPs 20 included in GTDB (r226). By contrast, the MiDAS HQ catalog improved reads 127 recruitment across regions, including underrepresented regions such as Oceania and South America, 128 aligning with extensive sharing of species across AS systems 6,10 . HQ MAGs from individual samples 129 explained only about one third of their own microbial communities (median: 37.8%, IQR: 28.9-44.3%) 130 (Fig. S6 ). 131 132 Read-recruitment estimates closely matched coverage inferred using a computationally efficient heuristic 133 

k-mer-based approach implemented in sylph 35 (Supplementary Table S8 ). Therefore, we applied sylph to 134 estimate coverage for 295 publicly available AS metagenomes from 165 WWTPs across 17 countries and 135 six continents 16,36 (Fig 3c ). The full non-dereplicated MiDAS catalog captured a median of 72.8% (IQR: 136 62.1-78.7%) of the microbial communities in these externally sourced, globally distributed datasets, further 137 supporting its broad representation of global AS microbiomes. 138 

All activated sludge core taxa are represented in the MiDAS global genome catalog 139 

Previously, we estimated that 250 core genera and 715 conditionally rare or abundant taxa (CRAT) genera 140 are necessary for, or detrimental to, WWTP operation based on the MiDAS global amplicon survey 10 . All 141 core genera are captured in the MiDAS genome catalog based on mapping of 16S rRNA genes using the 142 genus-level identity threshold of ≥94.5% 37 . 248 of the 250 core genera (99.2%) are represented by at least 143 one HQ MAG, with a median representation of 32 HQ genomes (IQR: 18-57) ( Fig. 3d ). ANI-based species 144 clustering further resolved these into a median of 14 species per core genus (IQR: 7-23) ( Supplementary 145 

Table S9 ). In total, 11,359 HQ MAGs (4,875 species) were assigned to core genera, accounting for more 146 than half of all HQ MAGs in the MiDAS catalog. Across the 83 WWTP samples, these core genera 147 contributed a median of 48.1% (IQR: 36.2-53.1%) of total community abundance, consistent with our 148 previous amplicon-based estimate of 57-68% relative abundance across 589 AS samples 10 . Beyond the core 149 genera, we recovered MAGs for 564 (78.9%) CRAT genera, including HQ MAGs for 509 (71.2%) genera 150 with a median of 4 HQ MAGs each (IQR: 2-10). Taken together, the MiDAS catalog provides near-151 complete and genome-resolved coverage for core genera and CRAT lineages that structure global AS 152 communities, thereby establishing a reference framework for future trait-based and process-level studies. 153

Naming core AS species using HQ genome sequences as type material 154 

Given the inferred ecological importance (prevalence and abundance) of AS core taxa to the performance 155 of WWTPs, we decided to name all MAG-defined species (and associated higher ranks) currently lacking 156 Latin names that belong to the 250 16S rRNA gene-defined core genera. This was done according to the 157 SeqCode that uses genome sequences as type material 38 . For each genus, we selected a representative 158 genome (MIMAG HQ, ≤10 contigs) by prioritizing circular MAGs over fragmented assemblies. In addition, 159 geographic representation was incorporated during name assignment. Genus names were derived from the 160 cities closest to the WWTPs from which type genomes were recovered, while species epithets were assigned 161 as generic, WWTP-related or geographic names to minimize redundancy ( Method and Supplementary 162 

Table S10 ). This provided names for 1,474 species, 140 genera, 41 families, 15 orders and 4 classes, which 163 would support scientific communication about these important AS taxa. 164 

Conservation of inferred functional traits in genomically well-represented core genera 165 

In AS ecosystems, genus is often considered an ecologically meaningful unit because closely related species 166 within a genus frequently share overlapping functional roles 10,39 . The large number of independent MAGs 167 recovered for most core genera enabled us to directly test this assumption and quantify how consistently 168 genome-encoded functional traits are conserved within genera. To obtain robust, genome-based trait 169 estimates, we used GTDB-defined genus-level groups rather than 16S rRNA gene-based genus definitions .170 This reclassification yielded 277 core genera with at least 10 HQ MAGs ( Fig. 4 ), reflecting the different 171 approaches to define taxa by SILVA (16S rRNA gene sequence identity thresholds 37 ) and GTDB (relative 172 evolutionary divergence normalization 40 ). We classified each genome-encoded trait as either conserved 173 present (when detected in ≥80% of MAGs within a genus), conserved absent (<20% of MAGs in a genus) 174 or mixed (20-80% of MAGs in a genus). 175 176 Across the selected 6,094 genus-trait combinations examined, 24.3% were conserved present, 67.3% were 177 conserved absent, whereas only 8.4% were mixed ( Fig. 4 and Fig. S7a ), indicating substantial functional 178 consistency in trait presence or absence within core genera. However, this consistency was pathway-179 dependent ( Fig. S7b ). Mixed states were rare for traits such as nitrite oxidation, the reductive tricarboxylic 180 acid cycle and pyruvate oxidation, but exceeded 20% for glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway and 181 assimilatory sulfate reduction. Thus, genus-level groupings provide a useful but pathway-dependent 182 framework for functional interpretation. 183 184 The strongest conservation was observed for carbon metabolism. Complete pyruvate oxidation and the 185 tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle were detected in nearly all core genera (272 and 259 genera) and were 186 classified as conserved present in 267 (96%) and 211 (76%) genera, respectively. By contrast, autotrophic 187 carbon fixation capacity was rare and restricted to a few specialized lineages. The Calvin cycle was 188 classified as conserved present in five genera, including Sulfuritalea , whereas the reductive TCA (rTCA) 189 cycle was conserved in two genera, including Nitrospira _A and Nitrospira _D. Complete oxidative 190 phosphorylation potential was encoded in 103 core genera, of which 75 (27%) were classified as conserved 191 present, likely reflecting the temporal redox conditions occurring in AS that support metabolic niche 192 differentiation (see below). 193 194

Intracellular storage compounds are often used by bacteria experiencing fluctuating substrate availability 41 .195 Accordingly, glycogen and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) were broadly represented across the core 196 community, with 230 genera encoding biosynthetic potential for at least one of the two polymers and 192 197 genera (69%) showing conserved present storage potential. Glycogen biosynthesis was detected in 191 198 genera and classified as conserved present in 139 genera, whereas PHA biosynthesis was detected in 113 199 genera and conserved in 90 genera. However, complete storage/mobilization potential was far more 200 common for PHA than for glycogen, with only 8 genera encoding both glycogen biosynthesis and 201 degradation potential, compared with 62 genera for PHA ( Fig. 4) . This observation suggests that PHA-202 utilizing lineages more commonly retain pathways for both intracellular carbon storage and mobilization, 203 potentially coupling them to short-term fluctuations in carbon availability and redox conditions 42 , although 204 incomplete annotation of glycogen degradation genes may also contribute to this difference. 205 206 As expected, nitrification potential was confined to a narrow and highly specialized guild within the core 207 community 10 . Ammonia oxidation potential occurred only in the canonical ammonia-oxidizing bacteria 208 (AOB) genus Nitrosomonas (100% conservation) and in the comammox genus Nitrospira _D (77% 209 conservation), according to the GTDB taxonomy. Conserved nitrite-oxidation potential was largely 210 restricted to Nitrospira _D (100% conservation), Nitrospira _A (96%), and the canonical NOB genus 211 

Nitrotoga (100%). By contrast, denitrification traits were broadly distributed across the core community. 212 Only seven genera (2.5%) were classified as conserved present for complete denitrification pathway, 213 however, 215 genera (78%) showed conserved potential for at least one reduction step (nitrate, nitrite, nitric 214 oxide, or nitrous oxide reduction). This pattern is consistent with denitrification being modular and 215 distributed across multiple taxa, rather than concentrated in a small set of complete denitrifiers 43 . Notably, 216 69 genera (25%) encoded conserved nitrous oxide reduction potential ( nosZ ), including two genera that 217 lacked a complete upstream denitrification pathway, forming a widespread guild of putative N 2O reducers 218 that may act as community-level N 2O sinks ( Fig. S8 ). By predicting which core lineages encode complete 219 versus partial nitrogen-transformation pathways, the catalog provides a taxonomically anchored framework 220 for interpreting nitrogen cycling and pinpointing putative N 2O-consuming guilds in wastewater treatment 221 systems. 222 223 Although sulfur cycling is not generally considered a major process in AS 10,36 , the sulfur oxidation (Sox) 224 module was classified as conserved present in 27 genera (10%). In addition, oxidative dissimilatory sulfate 225 reduction (Dsr) capacity, represented by phylogenetically confirmed reverse-acting dsrAB genes, was 226 conserved in 11 genera (4%). This pattern contrasts with the restricted nitrifying guild and suggests that 227 sulfur-oxidation capacity is more broadly embedded within the core community. Together, these results 228 show that conserved-present traits are widespread in carbon metabolism (99% of genera), denitrification 229 (78%), carbon storage (69%) and, to a lesser extent, sulfur oxidation (10%), whereas carbon fixation (2.5%) 230 and nitrification (2%) are highly restricted traits. 231

## Discussion 311 

A key question in genome-resolved microbial ecology is how functional potential is organized across 312 taxonomic scales in complex communities. Across core genera in the global AS microbiome, our results 313 reveal a scale-dependent organization in which some traits reflect lineage-level constraint, others are 314 partitioned among closely related species, and modular pathways are assembled across multiple taxa. This 315 provides a framework for interpreting AS functions as traits shaped by different degrees of evolutionary 316 conservation and ecological partitioning, rather than as uniform properties of genus-level taxa. Carbon 317 metabolism formed an expected broadly distributed chemoorganoheterotrophic scaffold, suggesting that 318 presence or absence of these basic modules alone provides limited resolution for distinguishing ecological 319

By contrast, process-relevant traits showed stronger differences in their taxonomic 320 organization, indicating that functional interpretation requires attention to the biological scale at which each 321 pathway is conserved or distributed. However, these trait predictions remain limited by database-dependent 322 annotation biases, which may lead to under-detection of divergent or poorly characterized genes. 323 324 Consistent with the established understanding of nitrifiers, nitrification potential was restricted to 325 specialized taxa. However, long-read genome analysis uncovered diversity within this constrained 326 functional guild. Comammox bacteria, whose identity cannot be reliably resolved from 16S rRNA gene 327 data alone, were represented by 13 HQ MAGs (10 species) including 4 novel species, supporting their 328 broader presence in full-scale WWTPs 60 . Moreover, the identification of Nitrosoma gen. nov. and 329 

Nitrocyclum gen. nov. expands the known diversity of putative NOB in WWTPs. For Nitrocyclum , the 330 MiDAS catalog extends prior observations from individual non-AS MAGs by resolving distinct novel 331 species-level members in AS and linking them to conserved nxrA across multiple WWTPs. For Nitrosoma 332 

gen. nov., the MiDAS genomes newly link this previously functionally unresolved lineage to nitrite 333 oxidation potential. Notably, this genus consistently retains multiple distinct phylogenetic clades of the 334 

nxrA gene, suggesting stable retention of functionally divergent Nxr variants within the same lineage. Such 335 genomic configurations may enable nitrite oxidation across a broader range of redox or substrate conditions. 336 However, the catalytic properties, regulation or environmental responsiveness of these populations carrying 337 distinct Nxr enzymes will require further experimental assessment. 338 339 Denitrification potential represents a contrasting mode of functional organization. Although denitrification 340 is well recognized as a modular pathway in different ecosystems 43,61 , our analysis places this modularity 341 into a global taxonomic context by showing how individual pathway steps are distributed across recurrent 342 core genera. Thus, genus-level profiles can inform denitrification potential only when linked to step-specific 343 genome annotations. Within this modular process, the widespread canonical NosZ -encoding guild 344 identified here suggests substantial potential for N 2O reduction and may represent a target for N 2O emission 345 mitigation in WWTPs 62 . In addition, species encoding the recently described L-NosZ variant 63 were 346 detected in 28 of 83 WWTPs, but their combined relative abundance remained low where present (median 347 0.004%, IQR 0.002-0.013%; maximum 0.3%). This suggests that L-NosZ-encoding populations are 348 unlikely to dominate community-level N 2O reduction in the system, although low relative abundance does 349 not necessarily preclude ecological importance 64 .350 351 Species-level analyses of four experimentally verified PAO genera revealed another layer of functional 352 organization. Rather than following a single conserved genomic configuration, closely related PAO species 353 differed in both storage strategies and nitrate-reduction potential 55 . The expanded genomic representation 354 provided by the MiDAS catalog resolves this variation across major PAO lineages, including Nap-based 355 complete denitrifiers in Azonexus , coexisting Nap- and Nar-based lineages in Ca. Accumulibacter, and Nar-356 dominated, truncated denitrifiers in Ca. Phosphoribacter and Ca. Lutibacillus. This species-level mosaic is 357 consistent with PAO niche partitioning across microscale chemical gradients within flocs and the 358 alternating anaerobic, anoxic and aerobic zones of EBPR systems, where oxygen and nitrate availability 359 vary over space and time 65 . Together, this species-level resolution complements genus-level trait patterns 360 observed across the core community ( Supplementary Note ) and suggests that EBPR performance and 361 robustness may depend not only on total PAO genus abundance but also on the relative contributions of 362 distinct species adapted to different microscale environments. ## Methods 445 

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and long-read sequencing 446 

The samples included in this study were primarily collected in 2018 for the MiDAS 4 study 10 , except for 447 one AS sample collected in 2023 ( Supplementary Table S1 ). These samples were contributed by the 448 MiDAS Global Consortium, which is distributed across various regions worldwide. The sequencing and 449 analysis of all 83 samples were approved by the relevant authorities, either through applications under the 450 Nagoya Protocol or by authorization from local WWTP managers. Detailed sampling procedures have been 451 described previously 10 . To ensure DNA quality, samples stored at -80 °C were used for fresh DNA 452 extraction using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), following the manufacturer’s 453 instructions. Bead-beating was performed using vortexing at 80% of maximum speed to ensure efficient 454 lysis while minimizing excessive DNA shearing. DNA was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo 455 Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). 456 457 To enable efficient long-read sequencing, 2.5 μg of DNA per sample underwent one round of size selection 458 using the long fragment buffer to enrich fragments >3 kbp before library preparation. The concentration 459 and quality of size-selected DNA were re-evaluated using the Nanodrop ND1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 460 MA, USA) and Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Libraries were prepared for 461 each sample using the Ligation Sequencing DNA V14 (SQK-LSK114) protocol, then loaded onto R10.4.1 462 flow cells (FLO-PRO114M) and sequenced using a PromethION P24 device, with one sample per flow cell. 463 The kit 14 chemistry was run at a 4 kHz sampling rate for phase 1 samples and later at 5 kHz for phase 2 464 samples ( Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S2 ). Basecalling was performed with Guppy (v6.3.9-v6.5.7) 465 integrated into MinKNOW, using the super-accurate model with a Phred score of >10. The basecalled 466 FASTQ files were further filtered to remove reads shorter than 1 kbp using seqkit (v2.6.1) 76 to generate 467 trimmed long reads. Statistical summary (such as mean, median read length, read quality, and N50) for each 468 long-read dataset was calculated by NanoStat 77 (v1.6.0). GNU Parallel (20210822) 78 was used to execute 469 tasks in parallel. 470 

MAG recovery, quality assessment, taxonomic assignment, and species clustering 471 

The genome recovery pipeline mmlong2 (v0.9.2; https://github.com/Serka-M/mmlong2) 15 with the 472 parameter ‘ -sem wastewater ’ was used to recover MAGs from each of the 83 samples individually. Briefly, 473 clean reads were first assembled into contigs using MetaFlye (v2.9.2) 30 , followed by one round of polishing 474 with Medaka (v1.8.0; https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka). Eukaryotic sequences identified by Tiara 475 (v1.0.3) 79 were removed before performing four rounds of iterative genome binning with three binning tools: 476 MetaBAT2 (v2.15) 80 , SemiBin (v1.5) 81 , and GraphMB (v0.1.5) 82 . Contigs with lengths < 3 kbp were 477 discarded. DAS_Tool (v1.1.3) 83 was then used to select the best representative bins from the binning 478 iterations for each metagenome. GUNC (v1.0.5) 84 was used to detect the chimerism and contamination in 479 recovered MAGs. Genome quality assessment and coverage estimation within each sample were conducted 480 using CheckM2 (v1.0.2) 85 and CoverM (v0.6.1) 86 . Only MAGs that met at least the minimum MQ criteria 481 were retained in the MiDAS catalog. HQ MAGs were evaluated according to the MIMAG guidelines 87 ,482 with rRNA genes identified using Bakta (v1.8.1) 88 and Barrnap (v0.9; 483 https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap), while tRNA genes were detected with tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.11) 89 .484

Additionally, 16S rRNA genes were annotated using SILVA (v138) 90 and the MiDAS database (v5.0) with 485 VSEARCH (v2.22.1) 91 .486 487 To ensure the latest classification, after the default run of mmlong2, we re-run the classification steps using 488 GTDB-Tk (v2.4.1) 92 with GTDB 26 (r226) and the MiDAS database (v5.3) 93 . We also re-run the 489 classification based on the MiDAS database (v4.8) to have a consistent group with the previous study 10 ,490 where core taxa and CRAT were identified. In this study, two independent methodologies, CheckM (v1.2.2; 491 lineage-specific workflow) 94 and CheckM2, were employed to estimate genome completeness and 492 contamination, ensuring robust quality assessment and minimizing potential biases associated with specific 493 lineages. Based on these results, 22,277 out of 53,501 MAGs were classified as HQ according to the 494 stringent MIMAG standards, while the remaining were designated as MQ. The 53,501 recovered MAGs 495 form the full non-dereplicated MiDAS global genome catalog (MiDAS-HQ+MQ), with a subset of 22,277 496 HQ MAGs constituting the MiDAS-HQ catalog. To identify distinct species within the MiDAS-HQ+MQ 497 and MiDAS-HQ catalogs, dRep (v3.5.0) 95 was used to cluster the two genome sets at 95% average 498 nucleotide identity (ANI) with the parameter of ‘dereplicate’ and ‘ --S_algorithm skani ’. Additionally, 499 MAGs within each species cluster were extracted from dRep output results. Notably, HQ species 500 representatives in GTDB (r226) were selected using the same criteria. 501 

Phylogeny tree construction 502 

The phylogeny of 11,995 bacterial HQ species representatives within the MiDAS catalog was inferred using 503 GTDB-Tk (v2.4.1) with the de_novo_wf workflow, based on 120 bacterial marker genes. This process 504 utilized dependencies including Prodigal (v2.6.3) 96 , pplacer (v1.1.alpha19) 97 , FastTree 2 (v2.1.11) 98 , skani 505 (v0.2.1) 99 , and HMMER (v3.4) 100 . The resulting tree was then imported into iTOL (v6) 101 for further 506 refinement and visualization. 507 

Microbial community coverage estimation 508 

We randomly selected 1% of reads from each metagenome using seqkit (v2.6.1), with a median size of 1.4 509 Gbp (IQR: 1.3-1.5 Gbp), to estimate the percentage of the microbial community that was successfully 510 recovered and to reduce the computational cost of mapping all metagenomes. The subsampled reads were 511 then mapped to the different MiDAS genome sets in Fig. 3b (Dataset from this study) and Fig. S6 using 512 minimap2 (v2.26-r1175) 102 and filtered with the following criteria: a minimum alignment length of 1 kbp, 513 a minimum identity of 90%, and a minimum read coverage of 50%. For datasets that include only species 514 representatives, we also used a k-mer-based method, sylph (v0.8.0) 35 , to calculate the known percentage 515 based on the whole metagenomes. This approach also showed high consistency with the minimap2-based 516 mapping method (Supplementary Table S8 ). The coverage estimation of GlobDB 103 , a more 517 comprehensive species-dereplicated microbial genome resource that integrates additional genome 518 catalogues beyond GTDB, was also included for the comparison. For publicly available, external 295 519 datasets, sylph was used to calculate the recovery fractions based on different genome sets. 520

Linking MAGs to globally important taxa, genome annotation and functional trait conservation 521 

analysis 522 

To assess the coverage of globally important taxa (core and CRAT taxa) within the MiDAS-HQ catalog, 523 16S rRNA genes extracted from the HQ MAGs were aligned to core and CRAT genera (94.5% identity 524 cutoff) identified in the MiDAS 4 study 10,37 based on the MiDAS (v4.8) database. Core taxa were first 525 identified separately within each process type (C, CN, CNDN and CNDNP) and subsequently combined to 526 obtain the global core set in the MiDAS 4 stduy 10 . Additionally, core genera were defined as those with a 527 relative abundance of at least 0.1% in each WWTP and were further classified into strict core (>80% of 528 WWTPs), general core (>50%) and loose core (>20%) based on their detection frequencies across WWTPs, 529 as described in the MiDAS 4 study. All HQ MAGs were re-annotated using DRAM (v1.4.6) 104 with the 530 KEGG database (downloaded in January 2024) 105 . The KEGG-based annotations were then imported into 531 EnrichM (v0.5.0) using the ‘classify’ subcommand (https://github.com/geronimp/enrichM) to identify 532 complete modules for each MAG with the ‘ --custom_modules ’ parameter. The analyzed modules and the 533 criteria for defining complete modules (100% of genes present) followed those established in previous 534 studies 16,106 , the version of the KEGG database used here, or custom modules to construct the functional 535 traits of core genera ( Supplementary Table S12 ). 536 537 For trait conservation analysis, HQ MAGs assigned to GTDB-defined core genera represented by at least 538 ten genomes were used. For each genus, we calculated the fraction of HQ MAGs encoding each pathway 539 as a measure of within-genus trait conservation. Genus-trait combinations were assigned to three 540 conservation categories: conserved present when the trait was detected in ≥80% of MAGs, mixed when 541 detected in 20-80% of MAGs, and conserved absent when detected in <20% of MAGs. Genera with 542 insufficient genome representation were excluded from the genus-level conservation analysis to reduce the 543 influence of sparse sampling. Then, we extended the trait conservation analysis to higher taxonomic ranks, 544 including family, order, class and phylum, using the same set of HQ MAGs assigned to the 277 core genera. 545 Trait conservation was recalculated independently at each rank following the same category grouping. The 546 fraction of mixed taxon-trait combinations was then compared across ranks to evaluate how functional 547 heterogeneity changed with taxonomic breadth. A follow-up species-level analysis was restricted to traits 548 classified as mixed at the genus level with two cutoffs of at least three and five HQ MAGs per species were 549 evaluated. The resulting species-level mixed fractions were compared with genus-level mixed 550 classifications to assess whether functional heterogeneity within genera could be resolved at the species 551 level. 552 553 For nitrifying bacteria, nxrA genes were detected with GraftM 107 using pNXR and cNXR GraftM packages 554 and filtered based on gene phylogeny and gene length of >3000 nucleotides (HMM search lengths: pNXR: 555 3,411 nucleotides, cNXR: 4,092 nucleotides). Gene-synteny of nxr -encoding scaffolds was assessed using 556 the DRAM annotation output in R v.4.5.0 using packages gggenes (v.0.6.0) 108 and tidyverse (v.2.0.0) 109 .557 The rTCA-cycle was assessed using DRAM annotations (ko_id: K15230, K15231, K00169, K28726, 558 K28727, K00170, K28728, K00172, K28729, K00171, K01902, K01903, K00031 and kegg_hits: “2-559 oxoglutarate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase”). The dsrA and dsrB gene pairs from each HQ MAG were 560 concatenated and aligned with reference dissimilatory sulfite reductase (Dsr) sequences. Phylogenetic 561 placements were inferred using IQ-TREE 110 to determine whether the sequences belonged to the reductive 562 or oxidative Dsr groups. 563

Identification and phylogenetic classification of L-nosZ genes 564 

L-nosZ genes in the MiDAS global genome catalog were identified following the workflow described in 565 the original discovery study 63 . Protein sequences derived from DRAM annotations were screened using 566 hmmsearch (HMMER v3.2.1 111 ) against a hidden Markov model (HMM) constructed in the above study. 567 HMM hits were filtered using the following criteria: an e-value threshold of <1×10 -5 , a length range of 400-568 900 amino acids and the presence of conserved NosZ motifs (CX 2FCX 3HXEM, DXHH or GXHH, PHG, 569 GPLH and EPH) as suggested. To further verify the clade assignment, the above candidate sequences were 570 combined with the reference NosZ sequences for phylogenetic inference, i.e., sequence alignment using 571 MAFFT (v7.525) 112 , tree construction using FastTree (2.2.0) 98 . NosZ clades were assigned based on 572 phylogenetic placement relative to the reference sequences, using the most recent common ancestor of each 573 reference clade. Only three HQ genomes representing two species, including one species from the core 574 genus Desulfobacillus , were identified. 575 

Phylogenetic diversity evaluation and novel lineage detection 576 

Phylogenetic diversity was estimated using total branch length, while the additional branch length 577 (phylogenetic gain) quantified the phylogenetic contribution of the MiDAS-HQ catalog. To compute these 578 metrics for each taxonomic lineage, the toolbox, GenomeTreeTk (v0.1.8; https://github.com/donovan-h-579 parks/GenomeTreeTk) 11 was applied to a phylogenetic tree constructed from a merged HQ species-580 representative dataset comprising MiDAS and GTDB (r226), generated using GTDB-Tk (v2.4.1). 581 Additionally, a novel species was defined when a species could not be classified at the species level based 582 on GTDB (r226). To evaluate lineage novelty at higher taxonomic levels, the pipeline, MAG phylogeny 583 (https://github.com/aaronmussig/mag-phylogeny) 15 was used to assess the relative evolutionary divergence 584 (RED) value based on the entire-genome set of species representatives from GTDB, inferred by PhyloRank 585 (v0.1.12; https://github.com/donovan-h-parks/PhyloRank). Manual review and curation were performed on 586 phylogenies of highly divergent MiDAS-HQ MAGs. 587 

Naming of novel core taxa 588 

HQ MAGs with high contiguity (≤10 contigs) that remained unclassified in GTDB (r226) among AS core 589 taxa were selected for name proposals under the SeqCode registry (https://registry.seqco.de/), following the 590 previous work 17 . Latinized genus names were derived from the cities where the corresponding WWTPs are 591 located. Genus suffixes were assigned according to genomic features: -bacter for representative genomes 592 encoding shape-determining proteins, -coccus for genomes lacking shape-determining proteins, -plasma 593 for genomes lacking both shape-determining proteins and peptidoglycan biosynthesis, and -monas for 594 genomes encoding flagella. Species epithets were assigned using three categories, i.e., generic, WWTP-595 related, and geographic (country or broader region), to minimize name redundancy and provide geographic 596 representation among proposed names. For lineages whose parent taxa were represented by placeholder 597 names in GTDB, Latinized names were derived from the corresponding genus names. Etymologies for all 598 genus and species names are provided in Supplementary Table S10 , and the names will be manually 599 registered under SeqCode after publication. 600

Longitudinal analysis of vaccine-associated alterations in the faecal microbiota of layer 1

chickens using a vaccination schedule representative of commercial practice 2Anum Ali Ahmad 1* , Kris Hogan 1 , and Laura Glendinning 131. The Roslin Institute and Royal (Dick) School of Veterinary Studies, University of 4Edinburgh, Easter Bush Campus, Midlothian, EH25 9RG, UK 5*Corresponding author: Anum Ali Ahmad (aahmad3@ed.ac.uk) 6

Abstract 7The gut microbiota is crucial for immune development and overall health in chickens. In 8commercial production, birds routinely receive multiple vaccines during early life. While 9individual vaccines are known to affect microbial composition, the impact of complex, multi-10 vaccine programs, as used in the poultry industry, is not well understood. This longitudinal 11 study examined the impact of multiple live and inactivated vaccines, given at commercially 12 relevant times from an early age, on gut microbial diversity and composition in layer 13 chickens. We characterised microbiota profiles using 16S rRNA gene sequencing at pre- and 14 post-vaccination timepoints across different vaccine groups. Overall, microbial diversity 15 remained stable across most vaccines, indicating strong resilience of the gut microbiota to 16 repeated immunological interventions. Differential abundance analyses identified changes in 17 selected bacterial taxa following vaccination, with responses varying among vaccine groups. 18 Notably, these changes were not sustained, as the gut microbial community returned to a 19 stable state after the vaccination schedule. These findings underscore the robustness of the 20 chicken gut ecosystem and lay a foundation for future research into microbiome-vaccine 21 interactions and their implications for poultry health, immunity, and production efficiency. 22 

Background 23 The poultry industry is the leading source of animal protein for human consumption. In 2025, 24 global poultry meat production exceeded 151 million metric tons, and egg production reached 25 99 million metric tons (FAO 2025). This rapid expansion has led to the development of high-26 density poultry farms, which increases the risk of disease outbreaks. Vaccination is crucial in 27 poultry health management, as it protects against a range of diseases that threaten bird 28 welfare and productivity. 29 The immune system and gut microbiota develop an interdependent relationship from birth, 30 establishing long-term immune homeostasis. In vertebrates, about 70-80% of immune cells 31 reside in the gut, interacting with the microbiome to maintain gut integrity, support immune 32 maturation, and influence overall health (Wiertsema et al. 2021). Similarly, increasing 33 evidence in chickens indicates that vaccines can modulate microbial composition through 34 immune activation, while the microbiota can affect vaccine responsiveness by shaping 35 mucosal and systemic immunity (Beck, Zhao, and Erf 2024, Liu PY et al. 2024). However, in 36 modern poultry houses, young chicks lack transmission of maternal microbiota and primarily 37 acquire microbiota from the environment, which potentially influences their responses to 38 vaccines (Shterzer et al. 2023). Understanding the impact of vaccines on gut microbiota is 39 essential for improving chicken health and vaccine efficacy. 40 Most research has focused on the impact of single vaccines on gut microbial communities of 41 chickens, usually under experimental or controlled settings (Das et al. 2021, Khan et al. 42

This work provides an important insight into 61 microbiome resilience, host-microbe-vaccine interactions, and broader implications for 62 poultry health and performance. 63 

Methodology 64 

Study design and sample collection 65 Faecal samples were collected from 11 Hy-Line GP chickens maintained for breeding 66 purposes at the National Avian Research Facility, The Roslin Institute. The chickens were 67 undergoing routine vaccinations as they transitioned into breeding and egg-production flocks, 68 consistent with standard commercial vaccine practices. The birds were maintained under 69 PUN039 (Breeding and Maintenance of Hy-Line Brown), housed in accordance with the 70 Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 (ASPA) Code of Practice and subject to Animal 71 Welfare and Ethical Review Body (AWERB) subcommittee approval. 72 The chicks were housed in four pens, and three birds per pen were randomly selected for 73 longitudinal sampling, with the same birds followed throughout the study. Vaccines were 74 administered orally through drinking water, so their first exposure to the immune system 75 would have been through the gut. The birds were vaccinated in accordance with standard 76 commercial practices, using the timing and vaccine combinations commonly used for layer 77 breeders. Vaccines administered included Nobilis ND Clone 30, Nobilis Gumboro D78, and 78 Poulvac IB Primer for infectious bronchitis/Newcastle disease/gumboro (IB/ND/IBD) at 79 week 4 (W4); Poulvac AE for avian encephalomyelitis/fowl pox (AE/FP) at week 7 (W7); 80 Nobilis ND Clone 30, Nobilis IB 4-91, and Nobilis® IB Ma5 for infectious 81 bronchitis/Newcastle disease (IB/ND) at week 9 (W9); and Thymovac for chick anaemia 82 (CIA) at week 12 (W12), all administered according to the manufacturer's recommended 83 dosage (Figure 1). 84

DNA extraction and sequencing 103 Total DNA from pooled faecal samples (n=56) was isolated using QIAamp PowerFecal Pro 104 DNA Kit (QIAGEN®) following the manufacturer's instructions. As the chicken faecal 105 samples contain uric acid, which affects DNA quality, an additional DNA purification step 106 was performed using AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter) at a modified 1:1 volume ratio, 107 followed by the remaining steps as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA quality was 108 assessed by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis, while DNA quantification was performed 109 using a Nanodrop™ 1000 Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, UK) and Qubit® 4.0 110 (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Purified DNA samples were sent to 111 Novogene, UK, for sequencing of the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene using Universal 112 primers 515F (5 ′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3 ′) and 806R (5 ′-113 GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3 ′), generating 250-bp raw reads. 114

The reads were then 117 demultiplexed and denoised using the DADA2 plugin implemented in QIIME2 (release 118 2025.7.0; Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology), generating representative sequences 119 and a feature table. Taxonomic classification was conducted using the SILVA 138.2 120 reference database (Quast et al., 2013). As no pre-trained classifier was available for this 121

Statistical analysis 140 Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Alpha diversity indices 141 (Shannon and InvSimpson) were analysed using generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs) 142 implemented in the glmmTMB package. Vaccine, timepoint, and their interaction were 143 included as fixed effects, and pen was included as a random effect to account for repeated 144 measures. Depending on data distribution, a Gaussian family was applied to normally 145 distributed data, whereas a Gamma distribution with a log link was used for non-normally 146 distributed data. Post hoc pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were conducted 147 using the emmeans package, with Tukey-adjusted p-values to account for multiple 148 comparisons. 149 Beta diversity was assessed using unweighted UniFrac distance matrices. Differences in 150 microbial community composition across vaccine groups and timepoints were evaluated 151 using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), and visualised using 152 principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). 153 For differential abundance analysis, GLMMs were fitted for individual taxa to evaluate the 154 effects of vaccine, timepoint, and their interaction, with pen included as a random effect. 155 Only taxa that appear in more than 10% of samples and were detected in more than one 156 vaccine group were retained for analysis. Relative abundance data were log-transformed 157 following the addition of a pseudocount (1×10    ) to zero values to stabilise variance 158 (Nishijima et al. 2025). Post hoc comparisons were performed using emmeans (Lenth V. 159 Russell and Piaskowski Julia 2025), with Tukey-adjusted pairwise p-values. Only taxa 160 showing non-significant differences in DM4 vs DM1 and DM4 vs D03, but significant 161 differences between DM1 and D03, were considered potentially vaccine-influenced taxa. 162 Microbiota stability was further assessed by comparing the final vaccination timepoint (D03) 163 with follow-up timepoints (weeks 13 and 14) using the same modelling framework. P-value ≤164 0.05 was declared significant. 165

Gut microbial diversity exhibited vaccine-dependent temporal shifts 167 A total of 342,0118 raw paired-end reads were obtained from 56 samples, with an average of 168 61,703 ± 15,575 reads per sample. After filtering, denoising, merging, and chimera removal, 169 an average of 92.1% of reads were retained, indicating high sequencing quality. The filtered 170 reads were used to generate 2191 unique ASVs. After removing unassigned features and 171 known environmental contaminants, 1866 ASVs were retained for downstream analyses. 172 To explore how the administration of different vaccines influences gut microbial diversity 173 over time in a commercial setting, we compared within-sample (alpha) and between-sample 174 (beta) diversity across vaccine groups and timepoints. The highest alpha diversity was 175 observed in the CIA vaccine group at the DM4 (Shannon: 3.92 ± 0.96; InvSimpson = 27.7 ± 176 22.3) timepoint, and the lowest diversity was recorded in the IB/ND/IBD vaccine group at 177 DM1 (Shannon: 1.59 ± 0.22; InvSimpson = 3.4 ± 0.7) timepoint (Figure 2 A-B). Both 178 indices showed significant effects of vaccines group (Shannon: χ² = 35.75, p < 0.001; 179 InvSimpson: χ² = 21.76, p < 0.001), sampling timepoints (Shannon: χ² = 12.49, p = 0.002; 180 InvSimpson: χ² = 13.19, p = 0.001), and vaccine × timepoints interactions (Shannon: χ² = 181 13.26, p = 0.039; InvSimpson: χ² = 17.76, p = 0.007), indicating temporal vaccine-dependent 182 changes in microbial diversity. 183 Pairwise comparisons indicated that microbial diversity remained stable during the pre-184 vaccination period (DM4 vs. DM1) for all vaccine groups except CIA, which showed a 185 significant decrease in both Shannon (z = 3.278, p = 0.003) and InvSimpson diversity (z = 186 4.260, p < 0.001). Diversity between the pre-vaccination (DM4) and post-vaccination 187 timepoint (D03) was also stable across all groups, except AE/FP, where both Shannon (z = -188 2.360, p = 0.040) and InvSimpson indices (z = -2.745, p = 0.020) showed a significant 189 increase. Significant decreases in Shannon and InvSimpson diversity were observed between 190 DM1 and D03 timepoints in IB/ND/IBD (EMM, z = -2.64, p = 0.02) and CIA (EMM, z = -191 2.39, p = 0.04) groups, respectively, whereas AE/FP and IB/ND groups showed no 192 significant changes. 193 During the follow-up period after the final CIA vaccination, both Shannon (EMM: D03 vs 194 F1, z = 0.76, p = 0.72; D03 vs F2, z = 1.76, p = 0.18) and InvSimpson (EMM: D03 vs F1, z = 195 1.01, p = 0.56; D03 vs F2, z = 1.93, p = 0.13) diversity indices showed a non-significant 196 gradual decline. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between the two 197 follow-up timepoints (F1 vs F2; Shannon: z = 0.57, p = 0.57; InvSimpson: z = 0.92, p = 198 0.63), supporting the stability of microbial diversity during the post-vaccination period. 199

Discussion 291 Vaccines are recognised as an important factor influencing the gut microbiota of chickens 292 (Redweik et al. 2020). In commercial production systems, birds are routinely exposed to 293 multiple vaccines to protect them against a broad range of pathogens. In this longitudinal 294 study, we examined gut microbial dynamics in response to a series of live and inactivated 295 vaccines administered at different timepoints from early life through sexual maturity, 296 mimicking a commercial vaccine regimen for layer production. To our knowledge, this is the 297 first longitudinal study to investigate microbiome responses to complex, multi-vaccine 298 programs mimicking commercial conditions. 299 In this study, microbial diversity remained relatively stable across most vaccine groups, 300 indicating a notable resilience of the layer chicken gut microbiota despite multiple 301 immunological interventions. Previous studies have reported variable effects of vaccination 302 on gut microbiota. Some studies observed an increase in diversity post-vaccination, whereas 303 others reported minimal influence, highlighting the complexity of host-microbe-vaccine 304 interactions (Beck, Zhao, and Erf 2024). In contrast to the overall stability, we observed a 305

This finding 306 aligns with a recent report showing that IBV vaccination was associated with reduced 307 bacterial diversity (Borey et al. 2021). The CIA vaccine in this study is a live attenuated 308 formulation. Live vaccines have been reported to induce greater changes in gut microbial 309 composition than inactivated vaccines, potentially through transient colonisation, local 310 immune activation, or niche competition (Loddo et al. 2025). However, live attenuated 311 

Salmonella Typhimurium vaccination in layer chickens exerted minimal effect on alpha 312 diversity, likely reflecting differences in vaccine type, target pathogen, or host response 313 (Khan et al. 2024). 314 Interestingly, we also observed fluctuations in microbial diversity during the pre-vaccination 315 period in some vaccine groups. These shifts likely reflect natural microbiome succession 316 associated with birds' age and environmental influences, such as diet, stress, and housing, 317 consistent with previous findings (Wickramasuriya et al. 2022). Importantly, because of pre-318 vaccination temporal variation, we cannot exclude the possibility that part of the post-319 vaccination variation reflects ongoing natural fluctuations rather than vaccine effects alone. 320 However, our statistical framework accounted for both sampling timepoints and vaccine × 321 timepoints interactions, allowing us to distinguish vaccine-associated deviations from shared 322 temporal trends. Such baseline dynamics highlight the importance of including pre-323 vaccination sampling in microbiome studies. Without accounting for these natural 324 successional changes, observed post-vaccination differences may reflect a combination of 325 vaccination effects and underlying temporal variation, making it difficult to attribute changes 326 solely to vaccination. Importantly, after the final vaccine dose, microbial diversity gradually 327 recovered by weeks 13-14, with no significant further change between follow-up timepoints, 328 indicating that the microbiome largely stabilised post-vaccination. 329 At the taxonomic level, phyla Bacillota and Fusobacteriota were dominant across all vaccine 330 groups and sampling timepoints, consistent with the typical faecal microbiota composition of 331 layer chickens reported previously (Feng et al. 2025). We didn’t find any differentially 332 abundant phyla, suggesting that vaccination did not induce major shifts at higher taxonomic 333 levels. At the genus level, we observed different dominant genera during different weeks. In 334 contrast, genus-level profiles revealed marked temporal shifts in dominant taxa. These 335 changes appeared more closely associated with host age than with vaccination, as earlier 336 studies similarly reported high Lactobacillus abundance during early life, followed by 337 replacement with Romboutsia around week 8 and increasing Turicibacter in later stages of 338 growth (Song et al. 2024). These age-responsive genera are generally linked to gut 339 development, improved nutrient utilisation, and enhanced immune maturation (Song et al. 340 2024, Liu Q et al. 2025). 341 Differential abundance analysis revealed vaccine-associated microbial changes in different 342 vaccine groups. Both of these genera belong to the phylum 355 Bacillota and are associated with carbohydrate fermentation and anti-inflammatory activity 356 by synthesising beneficial metabolites such as SCFA (Fusco et al. 2023, Li et al. 2023). In 357 the CIA group, we only observe the decrease in the abundance of Ruminococcus , which is a 358 key fibre degrader and SCFA producer, particularly butyrate (Zhou et al. 2021). In the CIA 359 group, no substantial taxonomic changes were observed following vaccination, with the 360 exception of a decrease in Ruminococcus . Members of this genus are associated with 361 carbohydrate fermentation and SCFA production, particularly butyrate (Zhou et al., 2021). 362 Given that Ruminococcus was the only differentially abundant taxon detected, the biological 363 significance of this change and its relationship to vaccination remain unclear. 364 A limitation of this study is the use of faecal samples rather than caecal content samples for 365 gut microbiota analysis. While faecal microbiota profiling provides valuable insights into 366 intestinal communities, it may not fully capture mucosa-associated microbial populations and 367 can be influenced by transient environmental or sampling variability. Moreover, faecal 368 samples are not fully representative of the caecal microbiota, where the highest microbial 369 density and diversity in chickens is typically observed (Rychlik 2026). However, obtaining 370 caecal contents from live birds is not feasible in a longitudinal experimental design; therefore, 371 faecal sampling was used to enable repeated monitoring of microbiome dynamics over time. 372 Consequently, some vaccine-associated microbial changes may be attenuated or not fully 373 reflected in faecal microbial profiles. 374 Together, these findings indicate that different vaccines were associated with distinct 375 taxonomic responses rather than a uniform microbiome signature. Although several affected 376 genera have previously been linked to fibre fermentation and short-chain fatty acid 377 production, the observed changes were generally limited to a small number of taxa and varied 378 among vaccine groups. Consequently, the functional implications of these shifts remain 379 uncertain and cannot be directly inferred from 16S rRNA gene sequencing data alone. 380 

Conclusion 381 This longitudinal study investigated the impact of various vaccines administered to layer 382 chickens at different ages within a commercial setting on the gut microbial community. Our 383 findings revealed that microbial diversity remained relatively stable across most vaccine 384 groups. Differential abundance analyses identified changes in selected bacterial taxa 385 following vaccination, with responses varying among vaccine groups. However, these shifts 386 were short-lived, and the microbial community returned to stability following the vaccination 387 period. This work offers a foundational framework for future studies on microbiome-vaccine 388 interactions and their implications for poultry health, immune development, and productivity. 389

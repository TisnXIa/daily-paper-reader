Abstract 7

The minimum flow decomposition problem abstracts a set of key tasks in bioinformatics, 8

including metagenome and transcriptome assembly. These tasks, collectively known as multi-9

assembly, aim to reconstruct multiple genomic sequences from reads obtained from mixed sam-10 

ples. The reads are first organized into a directed graph (e.g., overlap graph, splice graph), 11 

where each edge has an integer weight representing the number of supporting reads. By viewing 12 

the graph as a flow network, the underlying sequences and their abundances can be extracted 13 

through decomposition into a minimum number of weighted paths. Although this problem is 14 

NP-hard, prior work has proposed an efficient heuristic that transforms the graph by identi-15 

fying nontrivial equations in the flow values. However, for graphs with complex structures, 16 

many equations cannot be fully resolved by existing mechanisms, leading to suboptimal de-17 

compositions. In this study, we revisit the theoretical framework of the flow decomposition 18 

problem and extend the equation-resolving mechanisms to jointly model all equations in the 19 

graph, enabling safe merge operations that iteratively simplify the graph. Experimental results 20 

demonstrate that our new algorithm substantially improves decomposition quality over existing 21 

heuristics, achieving near-optimal solutions for complex graphs, while running several orders 22 

of magnitude faster than the ILP formulation. Source code of our algorithm is available at 23 

https://github.com/Shao-Group/catfish-LP .24

## 1 Introduction 25 

Minimum flow decomposition (MFD) is a fundamental problem in graph theory that asks to de-26 

compose a directed acyclic graph with a unique source s, a unique sink t, and a valid flow on 27 

its edges into a minimum set of weighted s-t paths whose combined contributions exactly explain 28 

the given flow. MFD abstracts a broad class of computational biology tasks collectively known 29 

as multi-assembly. In the general setting, sequencing reads are obtained from an unknown col-30 

lection of sequences with unknown counts and are aggregated into an assembly graph; the goal 31 

is then to reconstruct those sequences and estimate their relative abundances. A prominent ex-32 

ample is reference-based transcriptome assembly [24, 11, 23, 22, 16, 18, 15, 19, 29, 25]. For each 33 

gene, mapped RNA-seq reads are combined into a splice graph whose edge weights represent the 34 

number of reads supporting each splice junction. Decomposing this graph into s-t paths recovers 35 

the expressed RNA isoforms from the gene. Similarly, the decomposed paths can represent contigs 36 

or strain haplotypes from a microbial community in metagenomic assembly [21]; or viral strains 37 

inferred from infected hosts in the study of virus quasispecies [1]. 38 

Despite being a clean and idealized theoretical model for the above multi-assembly tasks, MFD 39 

is computationally challenging. It is known to be strongly NP-hard [26] and hard to approximate 40 

within some fixed constant factor [12]. To compute optimal solutions, Kloster et al. [14] proposed a 41 

fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) algorithm whose running time grows exponentially in the number 42 

of paths in the solution. More recently, a series of studies introduced integer linear programming 43 

(ILP) formulations for MFD [5, 9], which can handle larger instance sizes than the FPT approach 44 

and are versatile enough to incorporate extensions such as inexact flows [27, 4], safety and subpath 45 

constraints [7, 13, 28, 3], as well as graphs with cycles [6]. However, despite improvements in 46 

modeling and steady progress in commercial solvers such as Gurobi [10], the intrinsic NP-hardness 47 

of ILP limits their scalability on large practical datasets. 48 

A complementary line of research focuses on heuristics that aim to produce practically accept-49 

able decompositions efficiently. Among these, the greedy-width algorithm [26], which iteratively 50 

extracts the path with maximum possible flow, is widely used and often outperforms methods with 51 

stronger theoretical guarantees [12], even though it can be exponentially worse than optimal in 52 

the worst case [2]. This disconnection between theoretical guarantees and empirical performance 53

in [20] have since become a standard benchmark, and even the most advanced ILP formulations 59 

still struggle to solve all of them within a reasonable amount of time. 60 

While efficient and generally superior to greedy-width, catfish remains vulnerable to suboptimal 61 

solutions due to the presence of superficial equations—linear relations between the edge flows that 62 

are not supported by any minimum decomposition. Distinguishing good equations from superficial 63 

ones often requires global structural information that local flow values alone cannot capture. Our 64 

work addresses this limitation through two key insights. First, many structural dependencies among 65 

edge flows can be tested using an efficient linear programming (LP) formulation that checks whether 66 

the subflows saturating each edge are compatible with a candidate set of linear equations. Note 67 

that, unlike ILP, LP can be solved in polynomial time, so this filtering step incurs only modest 68 

computational overhead. Second, while the primary goal is to determine whether the LP system is 69 

feasible, the feasible solution returned by the solver often reveals additional opportunities to further 70 

simplify a complex graph, making it possible to tackle equations that were previously unresolvable. 71 

Building on these observations, we propose catfish-LP (Algorithm 2), an LP system that models 72 

the saturating subflows required for optimal decomposition. This single LP formulation is highly 73 

versatile, enabling more effective filtering of superficial equations, safer edge-merging operations, 74 

and a principled LP-guided greedy extraction strategy. Through extensive experiments, we show 75 

that catfish-LP produces significantly higher-quality decompositions than previous heuristic meth-76 

ods, while remaining orders of magnitude faster than state-of-the-art ILP formulations. 77

## 3 Limitations of catfish 132 

The while-loop in catfish reduces the optimality gap by iteratively resolving equations on edge 133 

flows. While efficient and empirically effective on simpler graphs, catfish exhibits three fundamental 134 

limitations that restrict its performance on more complex instances. 135 

3.1 Superficial equations hurt decomposition 136 

The equation-resolving mechanisms in catfish provide an effective way to exploit good equations, 137 

but they rely critically on the assumption that the equations being resolved correspond to nontrivial 138 

null vectors of an optimal decomposition. The difficulty is that this correspondence is only one-139 

directional. Although every nontrivial null vector induces a linear equation on edge flow values, 140 

the converse does not hold: a flow graph may satisfy linear equations that do not correspond to 141 

any null vector of a minimum decomposition. We call an equation superficial if it does not arise 142

3.2 Fully resolving an equation is difficult 155 

While the equation-resolving mechanisms of catfish come with strong theoretical guarantees, namely, 156 

that optimal decompositions are preserved, they also limit the algorithm’s effectiveness in practice. 157 

In particular, in complex graphs, closed subgraphs on which reverse operations can act are often 158 

rare, making it difficult or impossible to fully resolve many equations. 159 

When an equation cannot be fully resolved, catfish discards all information provided by that 160 

equation. In the extreme case where no equation is fully resolvable, the algorithm falls back entirely 161 

to greedy decomposition. This behavior trades safety for effectiveness: although partial resolution 162 

may be safe in some cases, catfish lacks a systematic mechanism for determining which aspects 163

3.3 Premature fallback to greedy decomposition 167 

A related limitation is that catfish may revert to greedy decomposition even when the graph still 168 

contains equations with substantial structural information. Empirically, greedy decomposition often 169 

performs poorly on complex instances, producing solutions with sizes close to the theoretical upper 170 

bound ∆ and leaving a large optimality gap. 171 

In such situations, a more effective strategy would be to perform an informed greedy decomposition— 172 

one that remains consistent with the constraints implied by the unresolved equations—rather than 173 

abandoning equation-based guidance entirely. The absence of such a mechanism further limits the 174 

performance of catfish on challenging graphs. 175 

These observations motivate the need for a more robust framework that can validate equations, 176 

exploit partial information, and guide decomposition beyond purely combinatorial rules. 177 

## 4 Method 178 

Rather than introducing ad hoc heuristic fixes for each limitation of catfish in isolation, we address 179 

them in a unified manner by augmenting its equation-resolving framework with a lightweight yet 180 

expressive linear programming (LP) formulation, named catfish-LP. The key idea is to use LP not 181 

as an exact solver for MFD, but as a structural oracle that validates equations, extracts consistent 182 

subflow information, and guides safe graph transformations. 183

1. Recall that a feasible LP solution provides, for each edge e ∈ E, a valid subflow xe(·) that 268 

saturates e. If, in this subflow, an edge e′ satisfies xe(e′) = f (e), then there exists a valid 269 

decomposition (consistent with all remaining equations) in which every unit of flow through 270 

e also uses e′. Moreover, if e and e′ are adjacent, they can be conveniently merged while 271 

preserving the decomposition. However, this merge may be only supported by some feasible 272 

decompositions discovered by the LP and is not guaranteed to be correct for an optimal one. 273 

To err on the safe side, we again use LP to determine whether e and e′ must be merged in 274 

all decompositions consistent with the current equation set. This can be done by adding a 275 

temporary constraint xe(e′) < f (e) − ϵ and test for feasibility. If the LP becomes infeasible, 276 

the merge is deemed safe, and we contract e and e′ to simplify the graph. Such merges can 277 

have meaningful downstream effects, for example, enabling the resolution of equations that 278 

are otherwise unresolvable. 279 

2. If no further safe simplifications are possible, Algorithm 1 exits the while-loop and proceeds 280 

to greedy decomposition. Empirically, we observe that greedy decomposition almost always 281 

yields inferior solutions when unresolvable equations remain. In such cases, the feasible LP 282 

solution itself may lead to a better outcome. We examine the LP solution to identify any 283 

saturating subflow xe(·) that is actually a simple path through e. Among all such paths, we 284 

extract the one carrying the maximum flow f (e), remove it from the graph, and add it to the 285 

final decomposition. Unlike the previous heuristic, this operation is not guaranteed to be safe 286 

as the extracted path may not appear in all compatible decompositions. Instead, it serves as 287 

an “informed greedy” step: selecting the heaviest path that is consistent with all remaining 288 

equations, rather than the heaviest path in the graph overall. 289

addresses the three limitations of catfish described in Section 3. The method orchestrates three 291 

components, all derived from the same underlying LP formulation. First, the LP model is used 292 

to distinguish good equations from superficial ones by enforcing necessary conditions for compati-293 

bility with a minimum decomposition. Second, saturating subflows are extracted from feasible LP 294 

solutions, which encode partial yet reliable information implied by good equations and enable safe 295 

graph simplifications even when full resolution is not achievable. Finally, the LP solutions guide 296

## 6 Summary and discussion 393 

This work introduced catfish-LP, a new algorithm for the minimum flow decomposition problem that 394 

augments the state-of-the-art heuristic framework, catfish, with a lightweight yet highly informative 395 

use of linear programming. By leveraging LP both as a filter for identifying structurally consistent 396 

equations and as a guide for graph simplification via saturating subflows, catfish-LP addresses 397 

a central challenge in the theoretically motivated equation-resolving-based strategy: determining 398 

which equations are good for an unknown optimal decomposition and how to exploit them when 399 

direct resolution fails. Unlike ILP-based approaches, which offer exact solutions but scale poorly, our 400 

LP-based design remains computationally efficient while capturing much of the structural insight 401 

needed for high-quality decompositions. 402

Our experiments further show that catfish-LP provides a compelling balance between accuracy 411 

and scalability. Although slower than the purely combinatorial catfish algorithm due to the nec-412 

essary LP invocations, it remains orders of magnitude faster than ILP and scales effectively to 413 

large and entangled graphs where ILP routinely hits time limits. Moreover, the LP formulation 414 

introduced here provide a flexible foundation that can be adapted to other MFD variants with 415 

meaningful bioinformatics applications. 416 

Overall, catfish-LP demonstrates that incorporating LP into the combinatorial structure of MFD 417 

yields both practical benefits and conceptual insights. Practically, it produces smaller and more 418 

accurate decompositions within realistic computational budgets. Conceptually, it highlights how 419 

LP-feasible subflows can encode structural information about the decomposition space, suggesting 420 

new directions for hybrid optimization-combinatorial approaches for MFD and related flow-based 421 

problems. Future directions include exploring strengthened LP formulations, integrating domain-422 

specific constraints, and developing probabilistic interpretations of LP-guided decompositions for 423 

transcriptome assembly and beyond. 424

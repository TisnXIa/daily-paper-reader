Abstract 17 

18 

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a multisystem disease characterized by the accumulation of mucus in the 19 

airways that promotes pathogen colonization, leading to respiratory exacerbation s, lung failure, and 20 

death. Culture -independent approaches have revealed that the CF airway harbor s a complex microbiota, 21 

including opportunistic pathogens and bacteria that colonize the oropharynx . Here, we reanalyzed 5,260 22 

16S rRNA gene microbiota datasets to infer ecological associations between members of the CF 23 

microbiota. We determined that pathogens are more likely to proliferate and dominate when present ,24 

while oropharyngeal bacteria are more likely to form persistent communities. Further, we found higher 25 

diversity and increasing numbers of inferred interactions were positively associated with lung function. 26 

In contrast, pathogens were negatively associated both with each other and with oropharyngeal 27 

bacteria , suggesting that they may disrupt the microbiota . To validate these predictions, we cultured 28 

1,597 bacterial isolates from 96 people with CF and performed 12,542 coculture assays against eight 29 

representative CF pathogen ic and oropharyngeal bacteria. 23% of these interactions resulted in growth 30 

inhibition . While Pseudomonas isolates were , on average, the most inhibitor y, we observed variable 31 

activity among isolates. We then confirmed that Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates, even those from the 32 

same donor and timepoint, exhibited significant differences in their metabolome and bioactivity profiles 33 

that correlated with acquisition of mutations . Together, our results suggest that pathogens may disrupt 34 

the CF microbiota and bloom in part through differential metabolite production . Furthermore, these 35 

data highlight that characterizing multiple isolates is necessary to capture the full landscape of 36 

chemically mediated interactions within microbial communities. 37

Importance 38 

39 

The cystic fibrosis (CF) airway harbor s a complex microbiota, including oropharyngeal bacteria 40 

and opportunistic pathogens that establish chronic infections and cause lung failure. We confirmed that 41 

microbiota diversity is correlated with health and that a pathogen -dominated microbiota is associated 42 

with reduced lung function. We then inferred microbial interactions, which suggested that pathogens are 43 

able to disrupt the microbiota. To validate these predictions, we cultured bacterial isolates from people 44 

with CF and performed thousands of coculture assays, finding that approximately one -quarter of 45 

interactions resulted in growth inhibition. Pseudomonas broadly inhibit ed other members of the CF 46 

airway microbiota. However, we observed marked variability in bioactivity and metabolite profiles of 47 

Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates , even from the same donor at the same time . Our results suggest that 48 

pathogens disrupt the CF microbiota, possibly through bioactive metabolite production , and that 49 

characterizing multiple isolates is necessary to capture the complete picture of interactions in these 50 

communities. 51

Introduction 52 

53 

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disease in humans that results from recessive mutations in the 54 

gene encoding the CF transmembrane conductance regulator. As a consequence of these mutations, 55 

chloride ion transport is impaired in the respiratory epithelium leading to the accumulation of a thick 56 

and dehydrated mucus layer in the lower airways (1, 2) , which serves as the substrate for the microbial 57 

growth (3) .58 

59 

The most well -known bacterial pathogen that colonizes the airways of people with CF ( pwCF ) is 60 

Pseudomonas aeruginosa . A long -standing assumption in the field was that P. aeruginosa is the sole 61 

dominant pathogen in the CF airway . However, a recent meta -analysis revealed that less than half of 62 

sputum samples from 507 adults with CF (42.7%) are dominated by this pathogen (relative abundance 63 

>50%) (4) . In addition to being colonized by other pathogens, such as Burkholderia cenocepacia and 64 

Staphylococcus aureus (5) , recent advances in metagenomics and culturomics have confirmed that the 65 

airways in pwCF are colonized by a heterogeneous microbiota . This community can include other 66 

opportunistic pathogens (e.g., Achromobacter, Burkholderia , Haemophilus, Pseudomonas, and 67 

Staphylococcus ) and commensal oropharyngeal bacteria (e.g., Neisseria , Prevotella , Rothia ,68 

Streptococcus , and Veillonella ) (6, 7) . Together, these ten highlighted genera were previously reported to 69 

represent >85% of reads identified by 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of CF sputum (8, 9) .70 

71 

The microbiota of the CF airway is complex and the contribution of microbial interactions toward 72 

disease remain s poorly understood (10, 11) . Recent microbiome -wide association studies suggest that 73 

bacterial interactions are strong predictors of declining lung function in pw CF (12) . For instance, 74 

coculture between CF pathogens can enhance virulence factor production or antibiotic resistance by one 75

the growth of specific microbes. However, i t has also been suggested that elimination of specific 81

are normally suppressed by their competi tors (9, 18) . Thus, i dentifying the antagonistic interactions that 83 

structure the CF airway microbiota are therefore key to predicting disease trajectories and therapeutic 84 

outcomes. 85 

86 

Previous studies that investigated antagonism between CF bacteria have focused primarily on P. 87 

aeruginosa co cultured with S. aureus (19 –21) and Streptococcus spp. (22) . To date, there have been few 88 

large -scale interaction screens to identify antagonism between other members of the CF airway 89 

microbiota. For instance, Menetrey et al. recently performed 170 coculture interaction assays between 90 

CF and environmental isolates of P. aeruginosa , S. aureus, Achromobacter xylosoxidans , and 91 

Stenotrophomonas maltophilia , and documented 30 instances of growth inhibition, suggesting 92 

antagonistic interactions occur between among the CF airway microbio ta (23) . While their work was 93 

more exhaustive than prior studies, many members of the CF airway microbiota have not been tested in 94 

coculture with other microbes . Thus, there is a gap in our knowledge regarding antagonism that may 95 

occur between members of the CF airway microbiota, including interactions that could be exploited for 96 

human benefit. 97

One major mechanism by which bacteria mediate antagonism is through the production of 99 

bioactive secondary metabolites (24, 25) . Our previous genomic surveys indicated that biosynthetic gene 100 

clusters for secondary metabolite are abundant in aerodigestive tract bacteria that reside within the 101 

airways of pwCF (26) . Further, secondary metabolite production has been reported from CF pathogens, 102 

and these compounds function in a broad range of biological activities, including as antibiotics, 103 

siderophores, and virulence factors . For instance, P. aeruginosa produces a suite of secondary 104 

metabolites, including redox -active phenazines , such as pyocyanin, which contribute to host tissue 105 

damage and virulence (27 –29) , electron transport (30, 31) , and microbial interactions (32 –34) . In 106 

addition, t his pathogen also produces the iron -binding siderophores pyochelin and pyoverdine (35) , and 107 

antibiotics , including the copper -containing fluopsin C (36) , and 2-heptyl -4-hydroxyquinoline N-oxide (19, 108 

37) . However, g iven that P. aeruginosa populations diversify during chronic infection (38) and that 109 

secondary metabolite production often varies between strains (39 –41) , antagonistic phenotypes are 110 

unlikely to be uniform even within a single pwCF . This variability suggests that surveys that incorporate 111 

multiple isolates from the same pwCF and across multiple pwCF are needed to understand how bioactive 112 

metabolites mediate interactions broadly between members of the CF microbiota. 113 

114 

To address these gaps in the ecology of the CF airway , we combined a large -scale reanalysis of 115 

microbiota data with culture -based interaction screens . Using 5,260 existing 16S rRNA gene microbiota 116 

datasets , we found that pathogens and oropharyngeal bacteria exhibit differenti al colonization dynamics 117 

in the airways of pwCF. Further, we determined that lung function correlated positively with microbiota 118 

diversity and with the number of inferred interactions between bacterial genera . Among these potential 119 

associations , we ascertained that pathogenic genera exhibited mixed associations with other pathogens, 120 

but were negatively correlated with oral bacterial genera, all of which were positively correlated with 121 

each other . To test competitive interactions directly, w e cultured 1,597 bacterial isolates from 96 pw CF122

enabled us to consolidate all of th e data into a single analysis , at the cost of losing lower level taxonomic 143

Discussion 387 

388 

In this study, we investigated the impact of microbial interactions on microbiota dynamics and 389 

disease in the airways of pwCF. We initially analyzed preexisting 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing 390 

datasets, which led us to determine that members of the oropharyngeal microbiota and pathogens 391 

exhibit different colonization dynamics within the airways of pwCF ( Fig. 1 ). We then inferred interactions 392 

that occur between these two different groups of bacteria and found that increasing numbers of 393 

predicted interactions were associated with lung function ( Fig. 2 ). To validate the negative associations 394 

that we inferred , we performed culture from CF airway specimens to build an isolate library, which we 395 

then used for in vitro coculture inhibition assays with ecologically relevant bacterial strains. We 396 

confirmed that antagonism was widespread among CF isolates ( Fig. 3 and 4). We then noticed that 397 

different isolates of Pseudomonas possessed variable bioactivity in coculture ( Fig. 4 and 5). Finally, w e398 

showed that different P. aeruginosa isolates, even those from the same person at the same time point, 399 

produced different metabolites and exhibit ed variable bioactivity in vitro (Fig. 6 and 7).400 

401 

Previous studies have reported a well -defined ecological succession of bacterial populations that 402 

occur s within the airways of pwCF as they age (50) . In infancy the airways are first colonized primarily by 403 

bacteria from the oropharyn x and upper respiratory tract (e.g., Streptococcus spp. and Haemophilus 404 

influenzae ). Then in childhood and adolescence the abundance of S. aureus rise s. Finally , P. aeruginosa 405 

or othe r, rarer pathogens proliferate in adulthood and as disease advances (6) . In our reanalysis of 16S 406 

rRNA gene amplicon sequencing studies, we detect ed age -associated shifts in the composition of the 407 

microbiota , albeit only weakly ( Fig. S2 ). However, our data were consistent with a broader ecological 408 

pattern described in prior work (6, 51) , as oropharyngeal bacteria tend ed to form relatively resident 409 

communities, whereas pathogens tend ed to bloom in abundance and dominate the microbiota when 410

supporting the idea that pathogen proliferation is linked to disease progression. Alternatively, this 412 

pattern is also consistent with the possibility that the inflammatory and nutrient -rich environment of the 413 

CF airway selects for pathogens that are adapted to grow under these conditions (52, 53) . Regardless, 414 

the association between pathogen blooms and loss of lung function suggests that disruption of the 415 

microbiota, whether by environmental changes, host immunity, or direct microbial interactions, 416 

contributes to disease progression in pwCF. 417

Reanalysis of 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing studies suggest ed the potential for 419 

widespread antagonistic and cooperative interactions among members of the CF airway microbiota (Fig. 420 

2). Notably, we found that increasing numbers of interacting genera were associated with improved lung 421 

function in pwCF (Fig. 2A ). This observation appears to contrast with Rivett et al. (12) , who recently 422 

reported that increasing numbers of interactions among a subset of CF -associated taxa were associated 423 

with worsening disease in the CF airway . However, in our analysis, many of the inferred interactions 424 

among oropharyngeal genera were positive, and these bacteria are typically associated with a more 425 

diverse microbiota and with lung health (7, 17) . Rivett et al. instead focused on taxa whose pairwise 426 

associations were linked to reduced lung function, including pathogens. These studies suggest that 427 

positive associations among oropharyngeal bacteria are characteristic of resident communities in 428 

healthier pwCF, whereas worsening disease is associated with antagonistic interactions involving 429

together, these results support a model in which pathogen blooms in the CF airway result, at least in 433

Here, we note there were limitations in our study. First, due to prior sample preparation 467 

protocols that did not limit exposure to oxygen , we were unable to culture strict anaerobes from the 468 

donated sputa samples. We predicted multiple interactions that involve strictly anaerobic bacteria ( Fig. 469 

2B ) and it is known that anaerobes play important roles in interactions with CF pathogens (14, 15) . In 470 

addition, we did not isolate any Burkholderia spp., as no sputa was donated from individuals at the 471 

Oklahoma City Cystic Fibrosis Clinic that were culture -positive for these pathogens . Second , we have 472 

focused exclusively on the bacteria that colonize the airways of pwCF, while ignoring Aspergillus ,473 

Scedosporium , and other fungi that often inhabit this same environment . These fungi are known interact 474 

with CF bacteria (18) and to produce bioactive metabolites (71 –76) . It is also likely that fungi will exhibit 475 

isolate -to -isolate variability in their bioactive metabolite production. Finally, our cross -streak assays are 476 

not well -suited to test for positive interactions (e.g., growth promotion) between microbes. While these 477 

limitations mean that our cultures are not representative of the overall CF microbiota, to our knowledge 478 

they do represent the largest study of interactions between CF microbes to date , and we recapitulate the 479 

associations we inferred from amplicon sequencing data . Future studies that account for each of these 480 

limitations will improve our understanding of the microbial interactions that occur within the CF airway .481

In conclusion, we report that P. aeruginosa and other CF pathogens potentially disrupt the 483 

structure of microbiota , bloom in abundance , and cause worsening disease in part by producing 484 

bioactive metabolites , an underappreciated phenomenon in the CF airway. We speculate that molecules 485 

that target secondary metabolite production by these pathogens could be developed as an anti -virulence 486 

strategy to keep pathogen proliferation in check and prevent worsening lung disease in pwCF. 487 

Furthermore, we suspect that additional efforts to screen the CF airway microbiota for bioactive 488 

metabolites may yield additional leads that could be developed as new antibiotics. 489

Microbiota analyses . We identified 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing studies from pwCF by searching 492 

the SRA for "cystic fibrosis" AND "16S" , followed by manual curation of the resulting studies and cross -493 

validation with the corresponding publications to limit our scope to relevant specimens ( i.e. , airway, 494 

bronchoalveolar lavage , bronchoscope, oropharynx, protected brush, sputum) (12, 77 –113) . In total, we 495 

analyzed 5,260 sequencing runs (see Table S 1 in the supplemental material for the full list of runs 496 

analyzed and the donor demographics). For all analyses, we treated each individual sequencing run as an 497 

independent sample. 498

We downloaded reads from the SRA using fasterq -dump 2.10.3 from the SRA toolkit 500 

(https://github.com/ncbi/sra -tools ). We then used fastp 0.23.2 (114) for quality control and adapter 501 

trimming of the raw reads. To remove potential contamination from human -derived reads, we used 502 

bowtie2 2.4.1 and discarded reads that mapped to a reference human genome sequence (GenBank 503 

accession: GCA_009914755.4). We then used mothur 1.48.1 (115) to process 16S rRNA gene amplicon 504

whether microbiota composition differed by host or technical variables, we performed PERMANOVA 510 

with the vegan package using 9,999 permutations. We tested these variables with both unadjusted and 511 

partial models conditioned on the technical covariates (BioProject accession, 16S rRNA gene region 512 

sequenced, and sample type). We adjusted all PERMANOVA P-values for multiple comparisons using the 513

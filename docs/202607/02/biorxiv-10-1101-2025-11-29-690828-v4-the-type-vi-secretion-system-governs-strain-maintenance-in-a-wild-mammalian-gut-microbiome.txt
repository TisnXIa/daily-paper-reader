Abstract 28 

Bacteria inhabiting the mammalian gut coexist in dense communities where contact-dependent 29 antagonism mechanisms are widespread. The type VI secretion system (T6SS) is an 30 interbacterial toxin delivery pathway prevalent among gut Bacteroidales, yet its function in 31 naturally evolved microbiomes remains poorly defined. Here, we examine the role of the T6SS 32 in Bacteroides within a physiologically relevant gut community derived from wild mice (the 33 WildR microbiome). Using newly developed genetic tools and a strategy for functional 34 replacement of strains within the WildR community, we demonstrate that the WildR isolate B. 35 

acidifaciens employs a T6SS to antagonize co-resident Bacteroidales. We also show that loss of 36 T6SS function compromises the long-term maintenance of B. acidifaciens in the community but 37 not its initial colonization, establishing the system as a determinant of strain persistence. The 38 T6SS we identified resides on an integrative and conjugative element (ICE). ICE-seq, a targeted 39 sequencing approach, reveals that the T6SS-ICE is distributed among select Bacteroidales and 40 

Muribaculaceae species in the WildR microbiome, between which it appears to be recently 41 exchanged. We also show that transfer of the T6SS-ICE to WildR isolate Phocaeicola vulgatus 42 

confers transient colonization benefits in mice, but is linked to eventual population decline. Our 43 findings demonstrate that the T6SS can stabilize the presence of specific strains within a 44 complex, co-evolved gut microbiome, yet its value is context dependent and constrained by the 45 ecological and physiological landscape of the host community. 46 47

Introduction 48 

Pathways for contact-dependent antagonism between bacteria are prevalent among 49 species residing in the densely colonized mammalian gut (Coyne et al. 2016, Verster et al. 2017, 50 Whitney et al. 2017, Garcia-Bayona and Comstock 2018). Prominent among these is the 51 bacterial type VI secretion system (T6SS), a mechanism employed by Gram-negative species to 52 deliver toxic effector proteins directly to contacting Gram-negative cells (Hood et al. 2010, 53 Schwarz et al. 2010, Coulthurst 2019). The T6SS is widely distributed among gut resident 54 species belonging to the Bacteroidota phylum. Over half of Bacteroides spp. isolated from 55 mammalian gut samples encode T6SS genes, and Bacteroidales T6SS effector genes can be 56 found in >60% of individual human metagenomic datasets (Verster et al. 2017, Garcia-Bayona et 57 al. 2021). In vitro experiments suggest the T6SS of Bacteroides spp. can target a broad cross 58 section of gut Bacteroidales, while it does not appear to act on Proteobacteria, the second most 59 abundant group of Gram-negative organisms in the gut (Chatzidaki-Livanis et al. 2016). 60 A number of model murine gut microbiome studies have sought to provide insight to the 61 role of the Bacteriodales T6SS in the gut. Studies employing gnotobiotic animals co-colonized 62 by pairs of strains demonstrate that T6SS-mediated killing can occur in this environment, and 63 show that targeting in laboratory co-cultures is not necessarily diagnostic of an interaction 64 between strains in the murine intestine(Chatzidaki-Livanis et al. 2016, Wexler et al. 2016, Ross 65 et al. 2019, Robitaille et al. 2023, Sheahan et al. 2024). Gnotobiotic experiments in which more 66 complex mixtures of bacteria (up to 12 species) are co-inoculated and colonization of 67 conventional mice with pairs of Bacteroides strains demonstrate T6SS-mediated antagonism can 68 occur in the context of a more diverse assemblage (Hecht et al. 2016, Wexler et al. 2016, Hill et 69 al. 2024). However, these experiments all suffer from a number of limitations. For instance, the 70

Gnotobiotic 72 experiments additionally suffer from the lack of community complexity, while experiments 73 employing conventional animals require antibiotic pre-treatment or large inoculums to enable 74 strain engraftment (Hecht et al. 2016, Hill et al. 2024). As a result of these limitations, the 75 physiological and ecological role of the T6SS in mammalian gut microbial communities remains 76 largely unknown. 77 The limited insights available into the function of the T6SS in native gut microbiomes 78 primarily derive from metagenomic analyses of T6SS gene distribution patterns. T6SS gene 79 clusters in Bacteroidales segregate into three Genetic Architectures (GA) 1-3 based on synteny 80 and evolutionary relatedness; however, each contain conserved structural genes and variable 81 effector and cognate immunity genes (Coyne et al. 2016). GA1 and GA2 loci are encoded on 82 mobile elements, and several studies show they can be horizontally exchanged among 83 Bacteroidales strains co-resident in a microbiome, suggesting a selective benefit to obtaining 84 T6SS effector and immunity genes shared by neighboring species (Coyne et al. 2014, Garcia-85 Bayona et al. 2021, Sheahan et al. 2024). Further evidence supporting selection for compatibility 86 among the T6SS effector repertoire in co-resident species lies in the finding that effector gene 87 diversity in individual microbiomes is low, in contrast to the high degree of effector diversity 88 observed across metagenomes (Verster et al. 2017). Studies have also revealed that gut 89 Bacteroidales species can encode clusters of T6SS immunity genes unlinked to cognate effectors 90 (Ross et al. 2019). One type of these acquired interbacterial defense (AID) gene clusters, 91 designated rAID for recombinase-associated, are widely distributed across Bacteroidales and 92 have features consistent with active acquisition of additional immunity genes. However, other 95 evidence suggests that under certain conditions the cost of producing a T6SS can outweigh its 96 benefit. Studies of the prevalence of T6SS genes among B. fragilis strains reveal they are more 97 common in the microbiomes of infants than in adults (Verster et al. 2017, Robitaille et al. 2023). 98 Furthermore, longitudinal sampling of individual human gut microbiomes has revealed instances 99 in which the B. fragilis T6SS acquires inactivating mutations over time(Robitaille et al. 2023). A 100 comprehensive understanding of the physiological role of the T6SS in gut Bacteroidales has thus 101 yet to emerge. 102 Experimental testing of the hypotheses regarding T6SS function generated from 103 metagenomic and theoretical studies requires a tractable experimental model substantially more 104 representative of natural gut communities than those that have been employed to date. Here, we 105 study the function of a Bacteroidales T6SS in a natural gut community derived from wild mice 106 and reintroduced into gnotobiotic animals (Rosshart et al. 2017). We show that this community 107 retains a similar composition and level of diversity as the wild mouse community from which it 108 derives, and we demonstrate we can functionally replace members of the community by adding 109 an excess of a mutant strain of interest at the time of community introduction. Using this 110 approach, we demonstrate that the T6SS of B. acidifaciens is important for maintaining this 111 species in the community. We also demonstrate T6SS-mediated targeting between Bacteroides 112 species from the same gut microbiome, and provide evidence that not all Bacteroidales in the 113 community benefit from horizontal acquisition of the T6SS. Together, our findings show that the 114 T6SS can be an important fitness determinant in a native gut community of naturally co-occuring 115 organisms, and that its contribution to competitiveness in the gut varies across species. 116

The WildR microbiome is stable and similar to wild mice microbiomes despite propagation 119 

The WildR microbial community consists of a complex assemblage of organisms 120 obtained from the pooled ileocecal contents of three wild mice and subsequently introduced to 121 germ-free mice for laboratory propagation (Rosshart et al. 2017). This community diverges 122 substantially from that found in lab-reared mice, with a significantly greater proportion of strains 123 from the Bacteroidota and Pseudomonodata phyla, and a lower proportion of Bacillota and 124 Verrucomicrobia. Additionally, the most abundant OTUs in the WildR population are 125 consistently missing from lab mouse microbiomes. Importantly, by the F4 generation of 126 laboratory propagation in mice, much of the diversity of the WildR was retained, and its 127 composition was indistinguishable from that found in the originally gavaged dams (Rosshart et 128 al. 2017). 129 To establish whether mice colonized by the WildR community could serve as an 130 appropriate model to study T6SS-mediated interactions in the gut, we first sought to determine 131 whether community composition diverged in the subsequent generations of propagation since its 132 initial study. We obtained ileocecal contents of the F7 WildR generation, introduced these to 133 gnotobiotic mice via oral gavage, then harvested and cryopreserved cecal contents after 14 days 134 of in vivo propagation of the community (Supplemental Figure 1A). To assess the phylogenetic 135 composition of the WildR microbiome after this propagation, we performed short-read 136 metagenomic sequencing on DNA extracted from two stored aliquots and two fecal pellets from 137 mice used to propagate the community, generating a total of 63 Gb of sequence data. Reads were 138 then mapped to a mouse microbiome-specific reference database using Kraken 2 (Wood et al. 139

Distribution of a mobile T6SS in the WildR community 350 Given the fitness benefit of the T6SS we observed for B. acidifaciens during stable 351 colonization of the mouse gut, and previous observations that T6SS-containing ICE can be 352 present in multiple species in a single microbiome (Coyne et al. 2014, Zhang et al. 2024), we 353 sought to better understand the distribution of the system in the WildR community. To avoid 354 both the biases inherent in screening for the element in cultured isolates and the challenges of 355 assigning repetitive sequences from metagenomic datasets to MAGs, we developed a targeted 356 sequencing-based approach for identifying ICE-carrying strains in DNA extracted directly from 357 WildR-colonized mouse fecal samples. Our method, which we named ICE-seq, is based on 358 techniques used for identifying transposon insertion locations (Gallagher 2019). In short, we 359 designed primers to specifically amplify and sequence outward from the 5’ and 3’ ends of the 360 ICE to capture the genomic context of the insertion (e.g., ICE junctions; Supplemental Figure 361 4A). Discussion 450 

In the stable mammalian gut microbiome, where individual strains can persist throughout 451 the lifetime of an individual, the role of interbacterial antagonism pathways is not well 452 understood. Modeling-based studies suggest that antagonistic relationships between community 453 members can be stabilizing to the community as a whole, but how these pathways are employed 454 in an established community and the degree to which active targeting occurs between community 455 members in this setting is unexplored (Coyte et al. 2015, Coyte and Rakoff-Nahoum 2019). 456 Here, we show that an active T6SS is required for persistence of the mouse commensal B. 457 

acidifaciens in a natural murine gut community. To our knowledge, our results represent the first 458 evidence of T6SS-mediated targeting between naturally co-resident gut species. The species 459 targeted by the B. acidifaciens T6SS in vivo could not be confidently ascertained by the approach 460 we employed, likely because this strain represents a small fraction of the community and its 461

It is made The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 26, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.11.29.690828 doi: bioRxiv preprint

21 antagonistic activity did not detectably affect other populations. However, in vitro experiments 462 demonstrate that other Bacteroidales species from the same community are effectively targeted 463 by the T6SS of this organism. We postulate that B. acidifaciens relies on T6SS-mediated 464 antagonism to prevent displacement by competing Bacteroides. This model for the role of the 465 T6SS in gut Bacteroidales is consistent with metagenomic analyses showing that in adult human 466 gut microbiomes, B. fragilis is most likely to carry the T6SS if the overall prevalence of 467 

Bacteroides species is high (Verster et al. 2017, Robitaille et al. 2023). The lytic nature of T6SS-468 mediated killing could also contribute to the adaptive benefit of the system for Bacteroidales 469 species by releasing nutrients from targeted organisms (Borgeaud et al. 2015, Stubbusch et al. 470 2025). 471 Under our experimental conditions, the T6SS of B. acidifaciens appears to be dispensable 472 for initial colonization of the murine gut. This result was unexpected, as the T6SS is more 473 prevalent in human infant gut microbiomes than in adults, suggesting that T6SS-mediated 474 competition between Bacteroides is important during microbiome establishment (Verster et al. 475 2017, Robitaille et al. 2023). The colonization dynamics associated with WildR introduction to 476 adult animals differ from the natural process of gut microbiome development. Modifications to 477 our carrying capacity-driven approach to strain engraftment will be needed to establish a model 478 that more faithfully replicates the early stages of natural gut microbiome establishment. 479 Additionally, our experiments did not address the role of the T6SS in resilience following 480 disturbance, nor in preventing strain invasion, both of which are questions that should be 481 amenable to future studies employing the WildR model. 482 A feature shared by the widely distributed GA1 and GA2 T6SSs is their placement within 483 an ICE (Coyne et al. 2016). Previous studies show these can be exchanged between species 484

However, the factors governing the extent to which the ICE-encoded T6SSs spread 487 among co-resident Bacteroides species are not well understood. We show using ICE-seq that an 488 ICE-encoded GA1 T6SS is present in a minority of Bacteroides species within the gut of a 489 natural mouse community. The ICE present in two species, B. acidifaciens and B. caecimuris, 490 

shares >99% sequence identity across the element, indicated it was recently exchanged between 491 them. Interestingly, we find that T6SS activity contributes to B. acidifaciens competitiveness 492 towards B. caecimuris during co-colonization of gnotobiotic mice, suggesting these species may 493 occupy a similar niche, and thus be in direct competition in their native habitat. Accordingly, 494 ICE acquisition by one of these species could be expected to exert strong selective pressure for 495 acquisition and retention of the element in the second species, allowing it to resist intoxication by 496 its competitor. 497 In contrast, we find that P. vulgatus has not acquired the T6SS-encoding ICE in the 498 WildR gut community. We show that the T6SS is active in this species, and it can promote initial 499 gut colonization by P. vulgatus . However, during stable gut colonization, the fitness cost of 500 harboring the element appears to outweigh its benefit. One potential explanation for these results 501 is that P. vulgatus relies on mechanisms other than T6SS-mediated antagonism to maintain its 502 niche in the gut. An alternative possibility is that the fitness cost incurred by T6SS expression in 503 

P. vulgatus is higher than in species that maintain the element, such as B. caecimuris and B. 504 

acidifaciens . In support of this, we observed that T6SS activity provided up to a 100-fold 505 competitive advantage over two days to P. vulgatus during growth in competition with WildR 506 Bacteroidales isolates, whereas in B. acidifaciens, the degree of T6SS-dependent 507

This observation is in-line with a 510 proposal by Comstock and colleagues that dysregulation is a factor limiting GA1 and GA2 511 mobility within communities (Garcia-Bayona et al. 2021). Whether other factors, such as the 512 functions encoded by non-T6SS genes on the ICE, contribute to the differences in the fitness 513 costs and benefits conferred by ICE acquisition across species remains to be determined. 514 Our finding that the T6SS of a gut Bacteroides species is important for stable 515 colonization of its native habitat expands the diversity of demonstrated benefits to T6SSs in 516 natural environments. In both the midgut of honeybees and the light organ of bobtail squid, the 517 T6SS appears to be primarily important for intraspecies competition (Speare et al. 2018, Motta et 518 al. 2024). Strains of the squid symbiont Vibrio fischeri encoding a T6SS exclude T6SS-deficient 519 competitors from the light organ crypts they colonize (Speare et al. 2018). Similarly, T6SS-520 encoding honeybee gut resident Snodgrassella alvi excludes T6SS-deficient strains from bee gut 521 colonization (Motta et al. 2024). In contrast, the T6SS of Pseudomonas species appears to be 522 important for colonization of soil and the plant roots, and one study found that T6SS activity of 523 

P. protegens affected overall community composition in the tomato rhizosphere (Duran et al. 524 2021, Vazquez-Arias et al. 2025). Additional studies across more habitats and species will serve 525 to build a complete picture of the ecological and evolutionary factors that have led to the 526 dissemination of the T6SS across such a wide diversity of species. 527 A key challenge facing the field of microbiome research is how to investigate gut 528 microbe functions of interest in a physiologically relevant context that recapitulates the 529 complexity of native gut communities.

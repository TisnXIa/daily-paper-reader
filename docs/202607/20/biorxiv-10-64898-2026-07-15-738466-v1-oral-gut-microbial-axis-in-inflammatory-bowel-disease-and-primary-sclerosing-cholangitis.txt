ABSTRACT 33 

The oral cavity is increasingly recognized as a reservoir of microbes that can 34 

translocate to and influence the gut microbiome. This oral–gut microbial axis may 35 

contribute to the pathogenesis of chronic gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary disorders, 36 

including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and its comorbidity, primary sclerosing 37 

cholangitis (PSC). To investigate the oral–gut microbial axis in IBD and PSC, we 38 

enrolled 191 participants spanning Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), CD 39 

with PSC (CD-PSC), UC with PSC (UC-PSC), and healthy controls. We generated and 40 

analyzed the whole-metagenome shotgun sequencing data from saliva, tongue swabs, 41 

and fecal samples. Across oral and gut niches, we identified multiple microbial species 42 

differentially enriched in participants with UC compared with healthy controls, including 43 

Fusobacterium nucleatum and Gemella sanguinis in saliva, Catonella massiliensis and 44 

Tannerella serpentiformis on the tongue, and G. sanguinis and Sellimonas intestinalis in 45 

feces. Paired oral-gut analyses revealed 15 potential oral-origin species enriched in 46 

fecal samples; notably, G. sanguinis and Veillonella rogosae were consistently enriched 47 

in participants with UC. These findings were further supported by an external validation 48 

dataset comprising 1,716 gut metagenomes from five independent IBD cohorts, 49 

highlighting the reproducibility of oral microbial signatures. In exploratory analyses, 50 

smoking history was associated with shifts in the oral microbiome toward a UC-like state 51 

in healthy controls, suggesting a possible environmental modifier of the oral-gut 52 

microbial axis. Collectively, our study identifies distinct oral microbial signatures and 53 

their potential translocation to the gut in IBD and PSC, underscoring the role of the oral– 54 

gut microbial axis in disease progression. 55

INTRODUCTION 63 

The human microbiota is not only represented by the microbes from the gastrointestinal 64 

tract, but also those resident in other body sites, including the skin, oral cavity, lung, and 65 

urinary tract 1. Those microbial communities are not entirely isolated from one another; 66 

they interact and influence each other through various pathways. Among these, the 67 

oral-gut microbial axis has gained increasing attention. 68 

The oral cavity, as a primary gateway to the gastrointestinal tract 2, harbors a 69 

distinct microbial community shaped by various factors such as pH, oxygen levels, 70 

nutrient availability, and the host immune response 3. Accumulating evidence indicates 71 

that resident oral microbes can enter the gut through swallowing, aspiration, or 72 

translocation 4. This translocation can occur even in healthy individuals 5. Under certain 73 

conditions, the translocation of oral microbes may contribute to or exacerbate 74 

gastrointestinal diseases 6.75 

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of the gut microbiome in the 76 

pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) 7–11 and its comorbidity with primary 77 

sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) 12 . IBD, primarily Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis 78 

(UC), cause inflammation and damage to the digestive tract, resulting in diarrhea, 79 

abdominal pain, and weight loss. PSC is a chronic and progressive cholestatic disease 80 

characterized by peribiliary inflammation and fibrosis 13–16 . An estimated 60-80% of 81 

individuals with PSC are also diagnosed with IBD 17 . However, the involvement of oral 82 

microbiome in the pathogenesis of IBD and PSC, particularly via oral–gut translocation, 83 

remains poorly understood. 84 

Cigarette smoking is an important environmental factor that can alter both oral 85 

and gut microbial communities and has implications for disease development 18 .86 

Interestingly, previous studies have suggested that smoking has opposing effects in UC 87 

and CD 19,20 . In general, smoking is associated with a reduced risk of developing UC, 88 

while former smokers may have an increased risk 21 and in some cases quitting smoking 89 

may even worsen UC symptoms 22 . By contrast, smoking is widely recognized to worsen 90 

the course of CD, leading to more severe disease and a higher rate of complications 23,24 .91 

The factors underlying these opposing effects remain largely unclear. One possible 92 

mechanism is that smoking may influence the oral microbiome through the induction of 93

In the present study, we leveraged a well-characterized cohort ORCA (Oral 100 

Microbiome Assessment in PSC) to investigate the distinct oral microbial signature 101 

related to IBD and PSC, the impact of smoking on the oral microbiome, and its potential 102 

contribution to the pathogenesis of IBD and PSC ( Fig. 1a ). We performed whole-103 

metagenome shotgun (WMS) sequencing of microbiome samples from the oral cavity 104 

(saliva and tongue swab) and gut (feces) to examine the connections between the oral 105 

and gut microbiomes, providing new insights into their potential roles in the 106 

pathogenesis of IBD and PSC. 107

RESULTS 109 

Study cohort 110 

In the ORCA cohort, we enrolled 191 participants, including individuals with CD alone 111 

(n=42), UC alone (n=65), CD with PSC (CD-PSC, n=6), UC with PSC (UC-PSC, n=16), 112 

and healthy controls (n=62). The healthy control group included current smokers (n=22), 113 

former smokers (n=20) and never smokers (n=20). We collected saliva, tongue swabs, 114 

and fecal samples from those individuals and performed WMS sequencing ( Fig. 1a, 115 

Methods ). In total, we have WMS sequencing data from 201 saliva samples (from 187 116 

individuals), 195 tongue samples (from 181 individuals), and 109 stool samples (from 117 

108 individuals) ( Fig. 1b) . For 103 individuals, we have the WMS data from all three 118 

sample types. 119

healthy controls with different smoking statuses: current smokers, former smokers, and 215 

never smokers. We found that smoking status was significantly associated with the 216 

overall microbial structure of tongue samples, but not saliva or stool samples ( Fig. 5a-c ). 217

Smoking history shapes oral microbiome similarity to participants with UC 234 

In the ORCA cohort, unfortunately, no current smokers were enrolled among 235 

participants with UC, and only one current smoker with CD was enrolled. The healthy 236 

control group did include individuals with varying smoking statuses. To test how 237 

smoking is associated with the oral and gut microbiome in IBD, we stratified the healthy 238 

controls into three different subgroups based on their smoking status (never smoker, 239 

former smoker, and current smoker, denoted as HC ns , HC fs , and HC cs) and then 240 

calculated the microbiome distance between participants with UC/CD and the three 241 

subgroups of healthy controls. Interestingly, we found that the oral microbiome distance 242 

between UC and HC cs is significantly higher than that between UC and HC ns or HC fs ,243 

regardless of the sample types: saliva or tongue swab ( Fig. 6a-b ). Yet, the trend is 244 

opposite for stool samples. The stool microbiome distance between UC and HC cs is 245 

significantly lower than that between UC and HC ns or HC fs (Fig. 6c ), indicating sample 246 

types can be very critical to identify the positive or negative effects of smoking on UC. 247 

However, we did not observe similar pattern in patients with CD ( Fig. S8 ). 248

DISCUSSION 265 

This study comprehensively measures associations between oral and gut microbiome in 266 

a well-characterized cohort of IBD and PSC. Our results provide critical insights into the 267 

interplay between the oral and gut microbiomes and their associated metabolic profiles 268 

in IBD and PSC. Through systematic analysis of saliva, tongue swabs, and fecal 269 

samples, we identified significant alterations in microbial composition, diversity, and 270 

functional capacity, along with evidence of oral microbial translocation in UC and the 271 

potential modulatory role of smoking. These results shed light on novel microbial and 272 

metabolic mechanisms that may contribute to disease pathogenesis and progression of 273 

UC. 274 

Over the past decades, the role of the human gut microbiome in IBD has been 275 

widely explored 7,9,36,37 . In contrast, the potential contribution of microbes from other 276 

body sites, such as the oral cavity, has received comparatively less attention. Previous 277 

studies have demonstrated that IBD patients are associated with a significantly higher 278 

prevalence of periodontitis 38,39 , suggesting the potential role of the oral microbiome in 279

IBD. Among the different phenotypic groups, we found in this study that UC patients 280 

were associated with more oral species, such as F. nucleatum , P. nanceiensis , T. 281 

serpentiformis , and G. sanguinis . As a periodontal pathogen, F. nucleatum has been 282 

shown to exacerbate the severity and microbial dysbiosis in DSS-induced colitis mice 40 ,283 

and to promote gut inflammation and epithelial barrier dysfunction, thereby aggravating 284 

UC 41 . T. serpentiformis , commonly found in oral biofilms, has been linked to 285 

periodontitis 42 . Additionally, G. sanguinis , previously implicated in infective 286 

endocarditis 43,44 , was enriched in both oral and gut microbiome of UC patients. 287 

Meanwhile, we identified more species that were absent in UC patients in the gut 288 

samples, for example, A. shahii , B. intestinihominis , and C. catus . These species have 289 

also previously been reported to be depleted in UC patients 45 or associated with 290 

beneficial effects in DSS-induced colitis 46,47 .291 

Although physical and chemical barriers like bile and stomach acid typically 292 

separate the oral and gut microbiomes, the disruption of the oral-intestinal barrier can 293 

enable microbial translocation between the two sites and contribute to diseases. 294 

Leveraging the paired oral (saliva and tongue) and fecal samples, we found UC patients 295 

shared more species between oral and fecal samples, suggesting increased oral-gut 296 

microbial translocation or barrier disruption. Importantly, the 15 identified oral-origin 297 

species were indeed significantly more abundant in UC patients. These findings align 298 

with a previous study of 2,932 paired oral (collected from several oral cavity sites) and 299 

fecal samples with 16S rRNA gene sequencing from 223 healthy individuals in the 300 

Human Microbiome Project, which identified several oral-origin genera, including 301 

Actinomyces , Aggregatibacter , Corynebacterium , Parvimonas , Streptococcus , and 302 

Veillonella in human feces 48 . Notably, most of the 15 species we identified belonged to 303 

these genera. Additionally, G. sanguinis was identified as an oral-origin bacteria, 304 

consistent with our observation of its enrichment in both oral and gut samples from UC 305 

patients. 306 

In our cohort, although current smokers were not included among the UC 307 

patients, the control group included individuals with varying smoking statuses. By 308 

comparing UC patients with controls stratified by smoking status, we found that the oral 309 

microbiomes of current and former smokers of healthy controls differed more from those 310

predominantly detected in UC but not in CD, and that smoking-associated differences in 327 

oral microbiome composition were also primarily evident in UC rather than CD. This 328 

subtype-specific pattern is notable given that previous epidemiological studies have 329 

shown that smoking is differentially associated with IBD subtypes, being protective in 330 

UC but a risk factor for CD 20 . Although our findings do not directly address causality, 331 

they suggest that smoking may be associated with modulation of oral–gut microbial 332 

interactions in a disease-specific manner, potentially reflecting distinct host– 333 

environment–microbiome relationships between UC and CD. 334

in both salivary and fecal microbiomes, with enrichment of oral-associated genera such 339

suggesting oral–gut microbial overlap. Iwasawa et al. (24 PSC, 16 UC, and 24 healthy 341

351 

The translocation of oral bacteria to the gut has several important implications in 352 

addition to understanding the persistent dysbiosis characteristic of UC, CD and PSC. 353 

There remains strong interest in manipulating the intestinal bacteria through fecal 354 

transplant or other means 51–54 . These efforts may be less effective than hoped for as 355 

the oral microbiome may serve as a reservoir to perpetuate the dysbiosis evident in 356 

these diseases. The recurrent recolonization from the mouth to the gut may contribute 357 

to frustrating these efforts to renovate the gut microbiome more substantially or for a 358 

longer duration. 359 

In conclusion, our study contributes to the understanding of the oral microbiome 360 

in IBD and highlights the potential role of smoking in modulating disease risk in UC. 361 

These insights have important implications for understanding the mechanisms 362 

underlying oral-gut translocation, which may potentially be targeted in preventive or 363 

therapeutic strategies for IBD. 364

Microbiome taxonomic and functional potential profiling 395 

We performed quality control on the raw metagenomics sequencing data by initially 396 

discarding low-quality reads. Subsequently, we removed reads belonging to the human 397 

genome by mapping the data to the human reference genome with KneadData 398 

(https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/kneaddata/). Microbial taxonomic profiling was 399 

performed using MetaPhlAn4 27 , which uses a library of clade-specific markers to 400 

provide panmicrobial (bacterial, archaeal, viral, and eukaryotic) profiling. We then 401 

performed functional profiling for metagenomes by applying HUMAnN3 55 , which maps 402 

DNA reads to a customized database of functionally annotated pan-genomes. 403

Identification of oral species in fecal samples 405 

An oral-origin species in a fecal sample is defined as one that meets the following four 406 

criteria 48 : 1) The species’ average relative abundance across all oral cavity samples 407 

exceeds ‖ᡓ; 2) The species’ average relative abundance across all fecal samples does 408 

not exceed ‖ᡓ but large than 0; 3) The species’ prevalence across all oral cavity 409 

samples exceeds ‖ᡨ; and 4) The species’ prevalence across all fecal samples does not 410 

exceed ‖ᡨ  but is larger than 0. ‖ᡓ and ‖ᡨ were set as 1  × 10 −4 and 5%, respectively. 411

Microbiome profiling of external validation cohorts 413 

A total of 1,716 publicly available gut metagenomic sequencing data were collected 414 

across five datasets 10,31–35 , including HMP (n=888), PRJEB15371 (n=55), 415 

PRJNA1086048 (n=601), PRJNA400072 (n=132), and PRJNA429990 (n=40). The 416 

quality control and host contamination removal were performed by KneadData. Species-417

package. The alpha diversity was measured by the Shannon index. The beta diversity 426 

for community composition was measured by the robust Aitchison distance in the PCoA. 427 

The differences in microbiome composition by disease status were tested with the 428 

PERMANOVA using the ‘adonis’ function in the ‘vegan’ R package. All PERMANOVA 429 

tests were performed with the 9,999 permutations based on the robust Aitchison 430 

distance. 431

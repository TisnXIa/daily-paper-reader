Abstract 18 

Microbial communities occur in all habitats , yet how individual grow th on available nutrients scales to 19 

communit y assembly remains poorly understood . Th is gap stems largely from the unknown effect s of 20 

species interactions . These interactions arise because individual population s both consume and 21 

transform primary substrates into metabolites exploitable by others, and because parasitic and predatory 22 

mechanisms can release cell ular building blocks that enable nutrient reuse . Here, w e present a23 

mathematical framework that predicts community growth and compositional succession from 24 

monoculture growth kinetic s, resource availability , and species interaction parameters . To parametrize 25 

species interactions, we use a simulat ed -annealing optimization algorithm to search parameter space for 26 

sets that minimize the difference between modeled community growth and experimental time series 27 

from soil microcosms inoculated with defined communit ies of 20 or 21 soil isolates, with or without an 28 

opportunistic bacteriovorous member . The o ptimized interaction parameter sets were then used to 29 

predict growth dynamics in an independent 21 -member community and in species drop -out 30 

communities . We find that community development is biphasic: an initial phase dominated by 31 

competition for primary resources driven by inherent strain growth kinetics, followed by a phase 32 

governed by cross -feeding and biomass formation on released byproducts. Paired metatranscriptom ic 33 

analysis corroborated predicted shifts in individual growth states and revealed metabolic repurposing 34 

associated with the sudden renewed availability of metabolites and cell ular building blocks . Model 35 

simulations that excluded species interactions reproduced only one -fifth of the observed community 36 

biomass , highlighting the importance of cross -feeding for soil community growth . Overall , models that 37 

integrate monoculture growth kinetics with inferred species interactions can predict the dynamics of 38 

medium -complexity communit ies from starting inocula even when environmental nutrient composition is 39 

largely unknown. 40

INTRODUCTION 45 

Growth is a core property of bacterial populations , determined by species -inherent metabolic capacities ,46 

biotic interactions, available resource s, and the physic ochemical properties of their habitat . The k inetics 47 

of single -strain cultures growing on finite resources are well studied : Monod’s original mathematical 48 

formulat ion of growth kinetics (1), refined many times since (2-4), is now widely accepted . By contrast, 49 

mi cro bes in natural environments or host -associat ed communities rarely exist as pure cultures ; they 50 

occur within diverse multispecies assemblages (5, 6 ). Because microbial communities are crucial for 51 

health and ecosystem function ing , a central question is how to extend single -species growth formalism s52 

to describe growth of multiple species under defined substrate and habitat conditions (here after 53 

“community growth ”).54 

The main challenge is that multi species mixtures generate dynamic interactions that can enhance or 55 

inhibit individual development , and these interactions are not captured by the original Monod 56 

framework . Although several approaches have been proposed - including consumer -resource models (7-57 

13 ), or generalized Lotka -Volterra formulations (14 -19 ), none consistently link emerging species 58 

interactions to Monod -type , substrate -dependent growth kinetics for individual species. A useful 59 

community -growth framework therefore needs two components : (1) a descri ption of substrate 60 

conversion to biomass that retains the classical dependenc e of growth rate and substrate availability , and 61 

(2) a way to represent how emerg ent interactions modify each species’ substrate -dependent growth 62 

kinetics .63 

We and others previously proposed a chemical -reaction -network formalism that is analogous to Monod 64 

growth kinetics , in which growth of a bacterial population on a single substrate arises from two coupled 65 

processes: direct consumption of the primary (added) substrate and later uptake of released metabolic 66 

byproducts (20, 21 ). In mono culture s, measured biomass yield and growth rates reflect the combined 67 

effect of primary -substrate use and byproduct recycling, because the two contributions are difficult to 68 

separate experimentally . In multispecies communities, however, byproducts released by one species can 69 

be taken up by another , and vice -versa ; this cross -feeding can be captured mathematically as species -70 

specific waste production that serves as additional substrate for growth (20, 21 ). We previously showed 71 

that modeling two -species competiti on on a single resource is substantially improve d by including such 72 

cross -feeding , compared with alternative community -growth models (21 ). Thus, accounting for 73 

metabolite exchange is essential for describ ing community dynamics – a conclusion supported by many 74 

experimental studies showing widespread metabolite release during growth (22 -24 ), and that 75 

demonstrate the important role of byproduct -mediated cross -feeding in shap ing community composition 76 

and diversity (25 -29 ), stability (25, 30 ) and spatial structure (31, 32 ).77

The major objective of the current work was to extend the cross -feeding growth model for cocultures 78 

(21 ) to community level including emerging species interactions (Methods ). We hypothesized that two 79 

main components exist that underly species interactions. The first could be called substrate -mediated 80 

interactions (SMINT) , such as being the result of cross -feeding on metabolites leaked during 81 

transformation of the primary available substrates in the system. SMINT can have a positive but also a 82 

negative component if the released metabolites are growth inhibitory to the other species. The second 83 

component we call parasitic interactions (PARINT) , being the result of predation or damage inflicted by 84 

one species on the other, as a result of which cell contents leaks out and can be again used as growth 85 

substrates or biomass building blocks by others .86 

In absence of available interaction parameter sets, we use a simulated -annealing optimization (SAO) 87 

algorithm that iterative ly searches parameter values to minimize the differences of model -predicted and 88 

experimental observations of community growth (Fig. 1). Communities consisted of 20 or 21 soil isolates 89 

inoculated into sterile soil microcosms as described previously (33, 34 ). Optimized SMINT - and PARINT -90 

parameter sets were then used to predict growth and species succession in an independently started soil -91 

inoculated 21 -member community . Paired metatranscriptomic analysis was used underpin growth -92 

related gene expression and explain metabolic differences among community members appearing as a 93 

result of species interactions . Finally, we used the model to predict outcomes for species drop -out 94 

communities and compared interaction patterns in soil -grown versus mixed liquid culture conditions for 95 

the same community .96

parameter values describing biomass formation from byproduct utilization are estimated by an SAO -109

Substrate -mediated interactions are major enablers for community growth 146 

Next, we sought to dissect the contributions of observed community growth in soil microcosms 147 

attributable to either inherent individual strain kinetic differences or to emerging species interactions. 148 

We first focused on interactions arising from metabolite cross -feeding from primary substrate conversion 149 

(SMINT) . For this we used a 20 -member defined representative soil community (33, 34 ) (SynCom -20; no 150 

inclusion of known predators such as Lysobacter ; table S 1). The SynCom -20 grown in soil microcosms 151 

rapidly expanded in total size in the first 3 days, reaching its maximum total biomass after day 7 followed 152 

by a slower decline (Fig. 3A, biomass converted from measured cell numbers and average dry weights per 153 

strain, see Methods ; table S1 ). The community became dominated by 5 –6 strains, which showed 154 

different succession patterns. Starting from even initial strain proportions, the relative abundances 155 

of Pseudomonas sp. strains 1 and 2 and Rahnella increased after one day, followed by a steep increase 156 

in Flavobacterium and a more gradual increase in Caulobacter , Variovorax , and other minor species (Fig. 157 

3A). 158 

SMINT -parameters were estimated by the SAO -algorithm (Methods ) trained on 5 out of 8 combined 159 

replicate datasets from two independent soil -grown SynCom -20 experiments , with 3 random picked 160 

replicates left out for subsequent validation . SAO -estimations were repeated 10 times from growth 161 

kinetic values picked randomly from the log -normal distributions (Fig. 3B) . Community growth 162 

simulations with optimized SMINT -parameter s reproduced the observed community growth quite well, 163 

both in terms of individual population size s, trajectories and final total community biomass (Fig. 3B -D;164 

mean theta cosine trajectory value of 0.4445, Table 1) . Observed growth trajectories of some strains 165 

showed an initial temporary decrease in population size (e.g., Cohnella, Rhodococcus , Burkholderia ; Fig. 166 

3D) , which may be due to a decrease of cell viability with respect to the measured inoculum size by flow 167 

cytometry. Because growth simulations are initiated with the measured inoculum size, an actual loss of 168 

inoculum viability will lead to an overestimation of simulated population growth . The 10 independent 169 

SAO -simulations start ed from freshly initiated subsampled kinetics showed good parameter convergence 170 

and little variation among replicate s (Fig. 3 D). In contrast, drawing random kinetic starting values for 171 

SMINT -parameters from the respective species ’ growth rate distributions without further SAO -runs 172 

resulted in individual population growth strongly deviating from the experimental observations , despite 173 

good convergence of community trajectories (fig. S1, Table 1) . This suggests that the SAO -algorithm is not 174 

overfitting SMINT parameters, but capturing relevant biological meaning. 175

So far , the simulation models with optimized SMINT - and PARINT -parameter values explained and 252 

reproduced the observed growth of the SynCom -20 and -21 experimental datasets (Table 1). However, as 253 

these models search parameter space to match experimental results it may come as no surprise that 254 

their ‘fitting’ is relatively good. To gain more confidence in the biological significance of the optimized 255 

species interaction parameters , we next used the optimized SMINT - and PARINT -parameter sets to 256 

predict growth dynamics of an independently conducted SynCom -21 experiment starting with a different 257 

inoculum. For this additional SynCom -21 experiment (SynCom -21 MT , Table 1 ), we also generated paired 258 

time series metatranscriptomic and metagenomic data , which was used to infer in situ growth and to 259 

inform potential cross -feeding behavior from gene expression changes of uptake systems and metabolic 260

growth state classifier, which uses proportional abundances in a set of single -copy marker genes to 262 

predict probabilities of growth ranging from 0 to 1 , and classifies strains to be either in a growing or a 263 

non -growing state (35 ). CoPTR uses log 2-ratios of highest -to -lowest (Peak -to -Trough) metagenomic 264 

sequencing coverage as a proxy for in situ growth rates (36 ).265

Statistical analysis 877 

To assess the quali ty of the total biomass prediction s between different models and simulations , we use 878 

Cliff’s delta, a nonparametric effect -size measure. Cliff’s delta quantifies the degree of difference 879 

between two groups without assuming any underlying distribution and does not require a minimum 880 

sample size. The statistic ranges from −1 to 1, where 0 indicates complete overlap between the two 881 

groups, and values close r to ±1 indicat ing that one group consistently yields larger values than the other. 882 

A s tatistical significance can be attributed to Cliff’s delta by permutation testing (n = 100 ,000 883 

permutations) . Group labels are randomly permuted after pooling the two observed groups, Cliff’s delta 884 

is recomputed for each permutation, and the P value is reported as the proportion of permuted statistics 885 

that are at least as extreme as the observed value. 886

on the Bray –Curtis distance matrix. Assuming we have  𝑁  replicates of a community with  𝑆  species, the 888

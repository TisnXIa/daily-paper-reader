# Abstract 18 

Microbes in the animal gut compete to colonize spatially restricted host niches. However, 19 

whether competition for space drives inter -individual variation among hosts, and which factors 20 

determine the outcome of such competition, remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated 21 

competitive interactions of the  western  honeybee gut symbiont  Frischella perrara  with other 22 

members of the bee gut microbiota. Shotgun metagenomics analysis of individual bees revealed 23 

that  F. perrara  is negatively correlated with a specific species of the genus  Gilliamella . Co -24 

colonization of microbiota -depleted  bees  with these two bacteria resulted in their competitive 25 

exclusion. The outcome of this competition depended on the relative number of bacteria each 26 

bee received and  benefited  Gilliamella  when one of the two type VI secretion systems of  F. 27 

perrara  was mutated.  Using fluorescently tagged strains, high -resolution microscopy, and gut 28 

region –specific quantification, we show that both bacteria localize to the same host  niche  in the 29 

ileum of mono -colonized bees, indicating competition  in a spatially restricted host niche .30 

Moreover, both microbes protected against infection, promoting bee health.  This competition 31 

provides a n explanatory  mechanism underlying variation in the occurrence of  F. perrara  across 32 

honeybee colonies and highlights the importance of gut spatial structure and microbial 33 

competition in shaping microbiome composition and inter -individual variability. 34

# Introduction 39 

Gut -associated bacterial communities  play a n important  role in animal  development, physiology, 40 

behaviour and overall fitness. Despite this importance, gut microbiota composition varies 41 

substantially between individuals of the same species. Such inter -individual variation has been 42 

linked to alterations in host health outcomes  (1, 2) . Elucidating the drivers of inter -individual 43 

variation in gut microbiota composition will advance our understanding of gut microbiota –host 44 

interactions and support the development of preventative and interventional approaches to 45 

modulate microbiota composition. 46 

Besides host factors or diet consumption, bacterial competition is considered as a key 47 

factor in determining differences in community composition  (3) . It is well established that 48 

bacteria in the animal gut compete for shared  nutrient  resources, leading to competitive 49 

exclusion  (4, 5) . However, bacteria also compete for space,  as  colonization of a  restricted niche 50 

within the  gut  can  confer  fitness advantages  (6) . Type VI secretion systems (T6SS) are microbial 51 

weapons that play an important role in spatial competition  (7 –10) . These spear -like extracellular 52 

structures enable bacteria to inject toxins into neighboring cells in a contact -dependent manner 53 

(11, 12) . Their widespread distribution among  gut  bacteria suggests that T6SS -mediated 54 

interference is a major driver of competition within this  environment  (13 –16) . Moreover, theory 55 

predicts that early numerical differences in the abundance of bacteria can determine the 56 

outcome of competition via interference, even if the inferior competitor has a higher intrinsic 57 

growth rate  (17) . Consequently,  differences in the initial cell numbers of bacterial inoculation 58 

during community assembly can lead to substantial differences in community composition 59 

among individual hosts  (17 –19) . Yet, little is known about competition for space  in the animal 60 

gut, how  inoculum size  and T6SS modulate this competition, and if it can  contribute to inter -61 

individual variation among hosts . Addressing these questions is challenging given the complexity 62 

of animal gut ecosystems, which contain many spatial niches and exhibit high microbial diversity 63 

and variability. 64 

The  western  honey bee  Apis mellifera  harbors a relatively simple and host -specific gut 65 

microbiota that is spatially structured along the hindgut  (20, 21) . While  the posterior  hind gut 66 

compartment, the rectum, is dominated by bacteria from the genera  Lactobacillus ,67 

Bombilactobacillus , and  Bifidobacterium , the anterior compartment, the ileum, primarily 68 

harbors  species  of the genera  Snodgrassella , Gilliamella , and  Frischella  (22, 23) . These 69 

proteobacteria form multispecies biofilms attached to the cuticular surface of the gut epithelium 70 

(24, 25)  and are equipped with T6SSs  (26) , suggesting that they compete for  host surface 71 

colonization  via interference competition.  Frischella perrara , the only species of the genus 72 

Frischella  present in  A. mellifera  (27) , predominantly colonizes the anterior section of the ileum, 73 

where it  causes a localized host immune response and the formation of  a characteristic brown -74 

to -black deposit,  referred to as the  “scab”  (25, 28) . The prevalence of this bacterium and the 75 

occurrence of scab phenotypes vary greatly among honeybee colonies, with scabs detected in 76 

15% to 80% of adult worker bees within a colony  (25, 29) . However, t he ecological mechanisms 77 

underlying this striking variation in  microbiota composition across conventional bees  remain 78 

unknown . In  a previous study, we examined  how the colonization of  F. perrara  mutants  for 79 

putative colonization factors was impaired by competition with  a synthetic community (SynCom) 80 

composed of other honeybee gut symbionts  (30) . While the wild -type strain consistently 81 

colonized gnotobiotic bees, deletion of  hcp2 , a core component of one of the two type VI 82 

secretion systems (T6SS -2), caused a strong colonization defect specifically in the presence of 83 

the SynCom.  Based on these findings, we hypothesize that  F. perrara  competes with other gut 84

# Discussion 231 

Bacteria compete for space  and nutrients , yet how such competition contributes to inter -232 

individual variation in gut communities remains poorly explored. Our data shows that  F. perrara 233 

and  Gilliamella  sp. ESL309 are negatively correlated in conventional bees, inhibit each other’s 234 

colonization when co -inoculated into  microbiota deprived  bees,  protect bees against infection 235 

and share  a similar  physical niche in the anterior ileum. The outcome of this competition 236 

depends on inoculation size and on the presence of a functional type VI secretion system (T6SS) 237 

in  F. perrara , suggesting that their reciprocal exclusion is driven by interference competition. This 238 

mechanism provides a plausible explanation for why  F. perrara  is found only in a subset of bees 239 

within each colony and emphasizes the importance of competition  between symbionts that 240 

occupy the same spatial niche  in gut microbial communities .241 

Previous studies have shown that closely related bacterial strains and species coexist in 242 

the honey bee gut through the partitioning of metabolic and spatial niches. Such niche 243 

differentiation is thought to have been a key driver of diversification in several dominant bee gut 244 

symbiont lineages, including  Gilliamella  (24, 34, 35) . In contrast, we show that two distantly 245 

related species have converged on a largely overlapping ecological niche.  These microbes  engage 246 

in strong competitive interactions that can lead to reciprocal exclusion, providing a potential 247 

mechanism underlying variation in gut microbiota composition  in  Apis mellifera  population s, and 248 

also evolutionary changes in composition across  other  bee species. 249 

Our metagenomic analyses reveal ed  that the negative association between  F. perrara 250 

and  Gilliamella  is species -specific: only two of the identified  Gilliamella  species were negatively 251 

correlated with  F. perrara . Somewhat counterintuitively, most other  Gilliamella  species, as well 252 

as several  Snodgrassella  species, showed positive correlations with  F. perrara . Given such 253 

samples contained the full hindgut (ileum and rectum), t hese positive associations may be driven 254 

by indirect factors,  such as variation in the contribution of ileum versus rectum DNA to the 255 

sequencing data, rather than by direct facilitative interactions or enhanced colonization capacity. 256 

Moreover,  since  Gilliamella  species localize to distinct regions of the ileum and exhibit different 257 

metabolic capabilities  (24) , some species  may have  reduced  niche overlap with  F. perrara . A258 

similar argument applies to  S. alvi,  which colonizes the host epithelium throughout the ileum 259 

rather than being restricted to  the anterior part  (24, 25, 36)  and relies primarily on organic acids 260 

rather than sugars as an energy source  (37) . Consistent with their distinct spatial distributions 261 

and resource utilization patterns,  S. alvi  colonization was unaffected by the presence of  F. perrara 262 

in our experiments ( Supplementary Figures S1 & S3 ), suggesting limited niche overlap between 263 

the two species. In contrast, our experiments confirmed a negative interaction between  F. 264 

perrara  and one of the two  Gilliamella  species that was negatively associated with  F. perrara  in 265 

our ecological survey of conventional bees. A similar antagonistic interaction was recently 266 

reported for a different strain of the same  Gilliamella  species  (38) , indicating that this 267 

relationship can be recapitulated experimentally and may constitute a consistent feature of their 268 

association. 269 

A striking observation was that in gnotobiotic bees,  F. perrara  and  Gilliamella  sp. ESL309 270 

often completely excluded each other from the bee gut , despite the fact that there was no 271 

competition with other bacteria . These results suggest  that competition can contribute to inter -272 

individual variation in gut microbiota composition. According to ecological theory, such variation 273

independent of intrinsic growth rates —for example, by arriving first in the gut or being present 275 

at higher initial abundance  (18) . Our results align with this as  F. perrara  excluded  Gilliamella  sp. 276 

ESL309 when more abundant at inoculation  (10:1 ratio)  but was outcompeted when less 277 

abundant (1:10 ratio)  (Figure 2) . Moreover, theory predicts  that  the benefits of interference scale 278 

with population size  (17) , which is supported by the observation that the T6SS -2 of  F. perrara 279 

becomes more relevant as the  F. perrara  cell numbers  increase  relative  to those of  Gilliamella 280 

(Figure 2  and  Supplementary Figure S7 : 1:10  ratio compared to  1:1). In the hive, newly emerged 281 

adult bees acquire bacteria from contacts with multiple conspecifics and given  F. perrara  and 282 

Gilliamella  sp.  abundance varies between individuals  (Figure 1 Supplement 4 , Frischella  44_1 283 

and  Gilliamella  52_2 ), bees are likely exposed to different inoculation sizes, shaping symbiont 284 

colonization. 285 

Honey bees colonized with  F. perrara , Gilliamella  sp . ESL309 or both bacteria 286 

simultaneously, survived equally well  (Figure  4A  & C). Moreover  both  bacteria  conferred  similar 287 

levels of  protection  against pathogen infection  with  Serratia  (Figure  4B  & D) (38) . These results 288 

suggest that variation in the abundance or presence of these two bacterial taxa in the gut has 289 

no consequences for host survival following infection with this pathogen. A recent study 290 

suggested that  Frischella  provides protection against  Serratia  through colibactin -induced phage 291 

induction  (38) . Gilliamella  does not encode this gene cluster, indicating that any protective 292 

effects it may confer are likely mediated by different mechanisms. Moreover, defense against 293 

pathogens is only  one of the  ax es  in which these bacteria may  differentially  influence their host. 294 

For example, bacteria of the genus  Gilliamella  have been shown to modulate host learning and 295 

memory  (39) . Whether  F. perrara  exerts similar effects on the host remains unknown.  Thus, 296 

although no differences in pathogen protection were observed, variation in the abundance of 297 

these symbionts may nonetheless affect other host traits. 298 

A previous study focusing on  Snodgrassella  showed that the T6SS contributes to 299 

intraspecific interactions among  Snodgrassella  strains but does not appear to mediate 300 

interspecific interactions with other members of the bee gut microbiota  (40) . In contrast, our 301 

findings indicate that the  F. perrara  T6SS is involved in antagonistic interactions across species 302 

boundaries  targeting a member of a different genus. However, whether  F. perrara  directly 303 

delivers T6SS effectors into  Gilliamella  cells remains to be determined. Notably, the T6SS -304 

deficient mutant of  F. perrara  fails to induce the characteristic scab phenotype on the bee gut 305 

epithelium  (30) . Similarly, the study on  Snodgrassella  showed that the T6SS can modulate the 306 

expression of host immune effectors  (40) . Therefore, although our results are consistent with a 307 

T6SS -dependent antagonistic interaction, it remains unclear whether this effect is mediated by 308 

direct intoxication of  Gilliamella  cells or indirectly through T6SS -induced changes in the host 309 

environment.  Independent ly  of the underlying mechanism,  our findings suggest that the 310 

reciprocal exclusion of the two species is  driven,  at least  partially , by competition for  spatial 311 

niches . Microscopy  analyses  show ed  that both species  are restricted to  the  epithelial 312 

invaginations  in the  anterior  ileum , even in the absence of bacterial co mpetition  elsewhere in 313 

the ileum . Moreover , the competiti ve interaction  between  F. perrara  and  Gilliamella  sp. ESL 0309 314 

was mediated via T6SS,  a contact -dependent antagonistic mechanism, supporting the idea that 315 

competition for physical space contributes to their mutual exclusion.  Previous studies have 316 

shown that  Gilliamella  and  Frischella  differ substantially in their metabolic repertoires , although 317 

they  are both saccharolytic fermenters  (41) . Also , even closely related  Gilliamella  strains share 318 

only part of their metabolic potential  (24, 35) . Accordingly, competition for nutritional resources 319 

is unlikely to fully explain the observed antagonism. This interpretation is further supported by 320

However, o ur  results  also indicated  that the spatial  niche is not completely overlapping 323

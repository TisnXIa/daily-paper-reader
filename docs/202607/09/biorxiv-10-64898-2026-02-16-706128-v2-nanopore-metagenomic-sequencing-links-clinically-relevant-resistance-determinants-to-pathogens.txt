# Abstract 33 

Culture-independent metagenomics enables the detection of plasmid-encoded antimicrobial 34 resistance (AMR) genes directly from clinical samples; however, the clinical significance of 35 these genes depends on their bacterial host and genomic context, which metagenomics 36 cannot fully infer. Nanopore sequencing technology intrinsically encodes epigenetic 37 modifications such as methylation, which can be leveraged for plasmid-host associations from 38 metagenomic data. Existing methods rely on the recovery of metagenome-assembled 39 genomes (MAGs), which can introduce bias toward abundant taxa and leave clinically 40 relevant, low-abundance pathogens unassociated. To address this limitation, we extended 41 methylation-based plasmid-host association from the MAG level to individual assembly 42 contigs and sequencing reads. The CUPID pipeline implements the calculation of contig and 43 read similarity scores, which compare weighted mean methylation rates across motifs 44 genetically shared between any contig or read pair. We validated this approach on a mock 45 metagenomic community composed of ten carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates, 46 where we achieved 93.8% accuracy at the contig level and 100% at the read level for 47 carbapenemase plasmid-host associations. When applied to metagenomic and 48 quasimetagenomic data of sixteen patient rectal swabs collected during routine hospital 49 surveillance, our approach assigned every detected plasmid-encoded carbapenemase to its 50 correct bacterial host at the contig level, using matched culture-based diagnostics and whole-51 genome sequencing as a ground truth. Read-level analysis identified additional associations 52 that were missed at the contig level, including a multi-host plasmid confirmed by established 53 diagnostics. These findings demonstrate a pathway from rapid AMR gene detection using 54 metagenomics to actionable surveillance for infection prevention, transmission tracing, and 55 outbreak investigation. 56 57 

# Impact statement 58 

Culture-independent metagenomics can detect antimicrobial resistance genes, but their 59 clinical significance depends on the bacterial host and genomic context. Here, we show that 60 nanopore-derived bacterial DNA methylation patterns can link carbapenemase genes to 61 pathogenic hosts and plasmid context directly from patient samples. This provides a route 62 from rapid antimicrobial resistance gene detection to actionable public health surveillance. 63 64

# Introduction 75 

Genomics is now central to infectious disease surveillance, supporting high-resolution 76 outbreak detection, transmission inference, source attribution, and antimicrobial resistance 77 (AMR) monitoring across public health and food safety systems (1,2). Whole-genome 78 sequencing (WGS) of cultured pathogens is now, for example, a recommended standard by 79 the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control and the European Food Safety 80 Authority (3,4). Portable, real-time nanopore sequencing is extending these capabilities 81 beyond centralized sequencing centers, making pathogen genomic surveillance increasingly 82 feasible in clinical microbiology laboratories, distributed public health surveillance networks, 83 and lower-resource settings (5–9). 84 In comparison to culture-based genomic characterization, rapid, culture-independent 85 metagenomic pathogen surveillance offers several advantages. Metagenomic sequencing can 86 be applied to a wide range of clinical sample types without culture-based enrichment, which 87 can be time-consuming, requires specialized biosafety infrastructure, and depends on 88 pathogen-specific growth requirements (3). Metagenomics can detect pathogens and their 89 subtypes, including the identification of unculturable or previously uncharacterized pathogens 90 through de novo genome assembly approaches. Metagenomic data can further detect 91 virulence- and AMR-associated genes and, where sequence linkage permits, place them in 92 pathogen or mobile-element contexts (such as plasmids), including those harbored within 93 commensal microbial communities (10,11). Recent advances in accurate long-read 94 metagenomics using nanopore sequencing technology can provide ( i) higher resolution for 95 read-based taxonomic classification and AMR annotations, ( ii ) more contiguous de novo 96 assemblies (contigs) from complex microbial environments, including the recovery of 97 circularized AMR-carrying plasmid contigs, and ( iii ) binning of these contigs into high-quality 98 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), enabling the reconstruction of near-complete or 99 closed genomes by binning of the recovered contigs (5,12). 100 While nanopore metagenomic sequencing has recently demonstrated improved pathogen and 101 AMR profiling compared with culture-based approaches (10), three analytical barriers continue 102 to limit the clinical interpretation of metagenomic data: ( i) insufficient sensitivity for detecting 103 low-abundance pathogens and AMR genes; ( ii ) difficulty distinguishing clinically relevant 104 organisms from background or non-viable DNA; and ( iii ) limited ability to link detected AMR 105 genes to their bacterial hosts, particularly when genes are plasmid-borne. The properties of 106 nanopore sequencing data may provide opportunities to overcome these barriers: Nanopore 107 sequencing measures disruptions in an ionic current as native DNA molecules pass through 108 a nanopore, generating raw signal data that can be basecalled into nucleotide sequence while 109 retaining information about molecule-level features such as epigenetic modifications (13). ( i)110 To increase sensitivity, the real-time signal acquisition of nanopore sequencing enables 111 adaptive sampling, in which the initial sequence from each molecule is rapidly basecalled and 112 aligned during sequencing so that only molecules matching predefined pathogen or AMR 113 targets are retained (14–16). ( ii ) The raw nanopore signals from native DNA sequencing can 114 support additional computational inference beyond consensus sequence alone. Ürel et al. 115 (2025) showed that signal-level features can be used to infer microbial viability in controlled 116 experimental and mock-community settings, suggesting a possible route to distinguishing 117 DNA from viable and non-viable organisms in metagenomic samples (17). ( iii ) Finally, native 118 nanopore sequencing can detect bacterial DNA methylation patterns, which can be used as 119 endogenous genomic signatures for metagenomic binning and plasmid-host associations 120

This approach leverages bacterial restriction-modification systems, in which 121 methyltransferases methylate specific host DNA motifs while cognate restriction enzymes 122 cleave unmethylated foreign DNA (19, 20). The Nanomotif tool exploits the methylation motifs 123 using nanopore sequencing data to identify methylated sequence patterns within contigs, 124 improve metagenomic binning, and associate unbinned contigs, including plasmids, with host 125 MAGs (18, 21, 22). 126 Linking plasmids to their pathogenic hosts is critical when detecting plasmid-encoded AMR 127 genes in metagenomic data. Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales exemplify plasmid-128 mediated AMR spread, with conjugative and mobilizable plasmids moving KPC, NDM, VIM, 129 and OXA-48-like genes across bacterial strains and species (23). Given that delays in 130 diagnosis and therapy of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales infections are 131 associated with high morbidity and mortality, rapid metagenomic identification of pathogens, 132 AMR determinants, and their genomic context could substantially improve infection 133 management and public health response (24, 25). However, existing methylation-based 134 plasmid-host association approaches depend on the recovery of the prospective microbial 135 host on the MAG level (18), which is poorly matched to clinical surveillance samples where 136 the microbial hosts of AMR determinant may be present at low abundance within a complex 137 mixture of host DNA, dominant commensal microbiota, and other microbial DNA. Applying 138 Nanomotif directly to carbapenemase-carrying plasmids in low-abundance clinical 139 metagenomes is therefore constrained, with its plasmid-host association workflow depending 140 on sufficient recovery of the prospective host MAG, while pathogens are mostly only 141 detectable at the contig or sequencing read level. Consequently, the use of nanopore-based 142 methylation information in metagenomics has so far provided limited evidence of clinical utility 143 and has not yet been validated against established culture-based diagnostics (18,21,22). 144 Here, we developed and validated a computational framework that extends methylation-based 145 plasmid-microbial host associations to the assembly and individual sequencing read level. We 146 used mock metagenomic data from clinically relevant carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales 147 

isolates to validate associations between plasmid and chromosomal contigs and reads 148 predicted by our computational framework. We then evaluated this framework on 149 metagenomic data derived from rectal swabs routinely collected from patients during clinical 150 surveillance for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales carriage. By comparing our 151 predictions against pathogen and carbapenemase predictions from established culture-based 152 diagnostics and matched whole-genome sequences, we show that nanopore-based 153 metagenomics can reliably distinguish plasmid-encoded AMR genes and associate them with 154 their respective bacterial hosts, increasing the sensitivity and precision of metagenomics-155 based pathogen surveillance. 156 157

# Methods 159 

Mock metagenomic community 160 

We generated mock metagenomic communities by pooling the WGS data from ten 161 carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates. These mock communities were used to 162 benchmark metagenomic associations between AMR genes and plasmids, with the matched 163 whole genomes as ground truth. 164 

DNA extraction 165 

Ten carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates (Table S1A) were recovered from 166 glycerol stocks stored at -80 °C and cultured overnight at 37 °C on BD® Columbia Blood Agar 167 plates. Genomic DNA was extracted from the isolates using a spin-column-based purification 168 method. Briefly, 10–20 colony-forming units (CFUs) from overnight cultures were resuspended 169 in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A 100 μL aliquot was adjusted to 1 mL with PBS 170 and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 2 min. Genomic DNA was extracted from the resulting pellet 171 using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), according to the 172 manufacturer’s protocol for Gram-negative bacteria (26). To improve DNA yield and purity, 40 173 μL of proteinase K was added, and the lysis incubation was extended to 2 h. An RNase 174 treatment step was incorporated after proteinase K incubation, following the manufacturer’s 175 instructions (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) (8). DNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit 176 4 Fluorometer (dsDNA BR kit). 177 

Whole-genome generation by nanopore sequencing 178 

Nanopore sequencing libraries were prepared using the Oxford Nanopore Rapid Barcoding 179 Kit (SQK-RBK114.96). Two barcodes were used per isolate to adjust for imbalanced 180 sequencing across barcodes. Per barcode, 200 ng of DNA was used. The final libraries were 181 sequenced on R10.4.1 PromethION flow cells for 72 h. The nanopore sequencing raw data 182 were basecalled using the Dorado v0.9.1 super-accuracy basecalling model v5.0.0 183 (dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.0.0) and with epigenetic modification calling enabled for 184 6mA, 4mC, and 5mC. Reads were demultiplexed using Dorado demux and filtered to retain 185 reads with a minimum Phred quality score of 9 and a minimum length of 200 bases using 186 Chopper v01.11.0 (28). Sequencing summary metrics were generated using SeqKit v2.10.1 187 (29). De novo genome assemblies were generated using Flye nano-hq v2.9.6 (30, 31). 188 Assemblies were subsequently polished using Dorado polish in bacterial mode. Assembly 189 depth was assessed using SAMtools depth v1.21 (32). Genome quality metrics, including 190 assembly completeness and contamination, were evaluated using CheckM2 v1.1.0 with the 191 

uniref100.KO.1.dmnd reference database (33). Polished de novo assemblies were analyzed 192 using the Pathogenwatch v2.3.1 platform (accessed 28 January 2026) for species 193 identification and, if available, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) (34). Assemblies were 194 further taxonomically classified using Kraken2 v2.1.3 (35) and the NCBI nucleotide database 195 (accessed 30 January 2026). AMR genes were identified at the contig level using 196 AMRFinderPlus v4.0.23 (36–38) (database version 2024-10-22.1). Contigs were annotated 197 as plasmids or chromosomes using MOB-suite (v3.1.9) (39), which also provides plasmid 198 typing and mobility predictions based on replicon markers, relaxase (MOB) typing, and mating-199 pair formation (MPF) systems. The MOB-recon module was applied specifically to AMR-200 carrying contigs classified as plasmids to determine plasmid incompatibility types. 201

Clinical metagenomics from rectal swabs 202 

Sixteen rectal swabs were routinely collected as part of the Technical University of Munich 203 hospital admission screening for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales . Swabs (Copan 204 Diagnostics, Brescia, Italy) were inserted rectally to a depth of 2–3 cm and rotated three times. 205 Following collection, swabs were plated onto BD® MacConkey agar (Becton Dickinson GmbH, 206 Heidelberg, Germany) and Thermo Scientific™ Brilliance™ extended-spectrum β-lactamase 207 agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 20–24 h. 208 

Established diagnostics 209 

Species identification was performed from a single CFU of Enterobacterales isolates per rectal 210 swab (Tables S1B&D) using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass 211 spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics GmbH, Bremen, Germany; MBT Compass 212 4.1 with MBT Compass Reference Library 2023), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 213 Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted on pure subcultures using 214 the VITEK® 2 system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France) with the AST-GN69 cards, and 215 carbapenem susceptibility was interpreted according to European Committee on Antimicrobial 216 Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines (27). Vitek® 2 reports categorical susceptibility 217 interpretations [susceptible/intermediate/resistant (S/I/R)] derived from discrete antimicrobial 218 concentration steps; where we refer to elevated non-susceptibility, we mean a shift from S to 219 I/R in the Vitek ® 2 categorical call. Carbapenemase production was assessed using a multiplex 220 immunochromatographic lateral flow assay (O.K.N.V.I Resist-5) that detects the presence of 221 the most common KPC, OXA-48-like, NDM, VIM, and IMP carbapenemases. After completion 222 of routine diagnostics, five to ten CFUs of each carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolate 223 were stored at -80 °C in glycerol stocks. The same isolates were thawed and processed for 224 WGS as described above. 225 

Metagenomic data generation 226 

Genomic DNA was extracted directly from the first eight (of overall 16) collected rectal swabs 227 (“metagenomic” samples), and from a negative extraction control (PBS). These swabs, stored 228 in Amies transport medium without charcoal (Copan Diagnostics, Brescia, Italy), were thawed 229 at 4 °C overnight. For lysis, the swab tip was transferred into a QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA 230 Kit PowerBead tube. The swab shaft was aseptically removed using sterile scissors and 231 tweezers, ensuring that no residual transport medium or gel was transferred. The PowerBead 232 tube with the swab tip was vortexed at high speed for 1 min. Subsequently, the QIAamp 233 PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit solution CD1 was added according to the manufacturer’s 234 instructions, followed by incubation at 65 °C for 10 min and bead beating. DNA extraction was 235 then completed according to the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit protocol. DNA 236 concentrations were measured using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (dsDNA BR kit). Nanopore 237 sequencing libraries were prepared with the SQK-RBK114.24 Rapid Barcoding Kit and 238 sequenced for 72 h on a MinION Mk1D device using two R10.4.1 MinION flow cells each. Two 239 barcodes were used per sample per DNA extract. A total of 40-200 ng of DNA in 10 μL per 240 barcode was used for each library preparation, depending on the respective DNA 241 concentration. 242 The remaining eight rectal swabs were incubated in 4 mL of brain heart infusion (BHI) broth 243 for 4 h at 36 °C (“quasimetagenomic” samples). Following incubation, a 1 mL aliquot of the 244 suspension was collected and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 minutes. The resulting pellet 245 was resuspended in 100 μL of the remaining BHI. DNA extraction and nanopore sequencing 246 were performed as previously described for the metagenomic samples. 247

Metagenomic data analysis 248 

The entire computational pipeline is available at GitHub: https://github.com/harikaurel/cupid, 249 including the Nanomotif-based CUPID pipeline for the implementation of contig similarity score 250 (css) and read similarity score (rss) calculations and visualizations. 251 

Read-level metagenomic data processing 252 

All nanopore sequencing data were basecalled using Dorado v0.9.1 with the SUP v5.0.0 253 basecalling model (dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.0.0) and with epigenetic modification 254 calling enabled for 6mA, 4mC, and 5mC. Reads were demultiplexed using Dorado demux and 255 filtered to retain reads with a minimum quality score of 9 and a minimum length of 200 bases 256 using Chopper v01.11.0 (28). Sequencing summary metrics were generated using SeqKit 257 v2.10.1 (29). To remove human genomic information, human sequencing reads were identified 258 by mapping to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using minimap2 v2.30 (40) with the 259 map-ont preset. Reads with human alignments were excluded, and unmapped reads were 260 retained as the host-depleted metagenomic dataset. Read-level AMR gene detection was 261 done using AMRFinderPlus v4.0.23 (36). Plasmid reads were detected by MOB-suite (v3.1.9) 262 (39), and read-level taxonomic classification was performed using Kraken2 v2.1.3 (35) and 263 the NCBI nucleotide database (accessed 30 January 2026). 264 

Metagenomic assemblies and binning 265 

For metagenomic assemblies (Figure 1A), we used nanoMDBG (v.1.0) (42,43) followed by 266 polishing using Dorado aligner and polish (in bacterial mode). Contig-based taxonomic 267 classification was performed with Kraken2 as described above. AMR genes were identified 268 using AMRFinderPlus v4.0.23 (36) as described above. In the mock metagenomic community, 269 the ground truth for each metagenomic contig was determined by aligning the sequencing 270 reads from each isolate to the mock community assemblies with minimap2 v2.30 (map-ont 271 preset) (40), retaining only primary alignments. 272 Plasmid replicon types and mobility features were inferred from contigs using MOB-suite 273 (v3.1.9) (Figure 1A). These plasmid contigs were additionally manually investigated to rule out 274 false-positive plasmid annotations: ( i) for the presence of genes involved in plasmid and/or 275 transposon function using Prokka (43), and ( ii ) for the occurrence of the sequence in at least 276 two strains after BLAST alignment against the full NCBI database at ≥95% nucleotide identity 277 (44). Finally, the plasmids were annotated for their insertion sequence (IS) elements by 278 BLAST-based homology search against the ISfinder nucleotide database (downloaded on 279 May 26th, 2026), requiring ≥80% nucleotide identity (45). IS detections were deduplicated by 280 the IS family to reduce redundancy arising from the high sequence similarity between IS family 281 members, where detections from the same IS family within 50 bases of each other were 282 considered to represent a single insertion event. Each carbapenemase detection was then 283 evaluated for the presence of IS elements within a conservative 500 bases-window upstream 284 (5’) and downstream (3’) of the gene boundaries, and putative composite transposons were 285 identified where IS elements of the same family were detected in inverted orientation on both 286 sides of the respective gene. 287 

Contig binning was performed using MaxBin2 (v2.2.7; 46), MetaBAT2 (v2.18; 288 https://bitbucket.org/berkeleylab/metabat), and VAMB (v5.0.4; vamb bin command with –m 289 2000 and –minfasta 200000; https://github.com/RasmussenLab/vamb). The resulting bins 290 were integrated using DASTool (v1.1.7; https://github.com/cmks/DAS_Tool). 291

Plasmid-microbial host associations 293 

Per-base epigenetic modification counts per sample were generated in BED format using the 294 modkit pileup function (v0.5.0; https://github.com/nanoporetech/modkit). We then used this 295 BED file and the FASTA file of the metagenomic assembly per sample to apply the Nanomotif 296 v0.8.0 (18) motif_discovery module and identify methylation motifs at the contig level. As 297 Nanomotif is normally applied to the bin level, we implemented the Nanomotif-based CUPID 298 pipeline for contig- and sequencing read-level association calculations 299 (https://github.com/harikaurel/cupid). 300 For contig-level associations (Figure 1B), we first modified the contig-to-bin assignment file 301 such that each contig was treated as its own bin (i.e., one contig per bin). Chromosomal 302 contigs that remained “Unclassified” by Kraken2 were discarded. We adjusted several default 303 Nanomotif parameters to make the motif detection more lenient and recover more putative 304 motifs from the metagenomic data (setting min_motifs_bin to 1, threshold_valid_coverage to 305 1, and --min_motif_score to 0.5). Motifs of a length of at least four bases in the resulting bin-306 motifs.tsv are used as input for the epimetheus tool (epimetheus methylation-pattern contig 307 function), which generates the motifs-scored-read-methylation.tsv output containing weighted 308 mean methylation scores per motif and contig. We used these methylation scores to calculate 309 pairwise contig distances using root mean square distance ( RMSD ), which is calculated based 310 on all genetically shared motifs between any AMR-carrying plasmid and chromosomal contig. 311 We then calculated the contig similarity score (css) as RMSS x n , where RMSS is calculated 312 as (1 − RMSD ) and n denotes the number of shared motifs between any contig pair. While the 313 taxonomic annotation of the chromosomal contig with the highest css is chosen as putative 314 host of the respective plasmid contig, CUPID additionally visualizes the overall contig similarity 315 structure to understand the robustness of these plasmid-host associations. To do so, CUPID 316 also calculates the css between all chromosomal contigs and then visualizes the contig 317 similarity structure with reference to the AMR-carrying plasmid contig using Principal 318 Coordinate Analysis (PCoA; using python scikit-bio v0.5.9). It additionally generates heatmaps 319 of per-motif weighted mean methylation values, comparing the plasmid against the top-scoring 320 chromosomal contig of each of the top five species-level taxa according to the css. The 321 number in parentheses in the heatmap cells indicates the number of motif observations per 322 contig. 323 Read-level associations follow the same principle as contig-level associations to calculate the 324 

read similarity score (rss), with the following exceptions: The motifs identified at the contig 325 level are used and supplied to the epimetheus methylation-pattern read-bam module, which 326 reports the per-read methylation quality of each motif at each position in the read as extracted 327 from the ML tag of the BAM file. These quality values are converted to probabilities by dividing 328 by 256, giving a range of [0, 1]. For each read, the mean methylation probability across the 329 positions of a given motif is reported as the read's mean methylation value. 330 331

# Results 345 

Standard clinical metagenomic analyses 346 

Metagenomic data was generated from 16 rectal swabs, which were routinely collected as part 347 of the Technical University of Munich hospital admission screening for carbapenem-resistant 348 

Enterobacterales (Methods). The presence of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales in the 349 samples was confirmed using established culture-based diagnostics and WGS (Table S1A; 350 Methods); half of the swabs (n=8) were processed directly (“metagenomic” samples) and the 351 other half of the swabs (n=8) were processed after short unselective enrichment 352 (“quasimetagenomic” samples) to increase metagenomic sensitivity (Table S1B; Methods). 353 We restricted our metagenomic AMR gene-host association analysis to carbapenemases and 354 their respective Enterobacterales hosts since these associations could be verified using 355 established diagnostics and WGS data of the respective isolates (Methods). 356 When applying traditional metagenomic assembly and binning to all (quasi)metagenomic data, 357 we found that binning recovered a pure MAG of the relevant carbapenem-resistant 358 

Enterobacterales species in only three of the 16 samples, with one of those three relevant 359 MAGs only consisting of a single contig (Methods; Table S2). Contig- and read-level plasmid-microbial host associations 367 

Application to mock metagenomic community 368 

We generated mock metagenomic data to first validate predicted plasmid-microbial host 369 associations. This data comprised pooled WGS data from ten clinically relevant carbapenem-370 resistant Enterobacterale s isolates, each with accompanying near-complete chromosomal 371 and plasmid assemblies (Table S1C; Methods). We assembled the mock community (Table 372 S1C), calculated the css and rss of all plasmid units carrying NDM, KPC, OXA-48, or VIM 373 carbapenemases (Figure 1), and compared the predicted microbial host associations with the 374 WGS-based ground truth (Table 1; Methods). This approach achieved high accuracy of 93.8% 375 (15/16) in plasmid-host association predictions on the contig level; the only AMR-carrying 376 plasmid contig that could not be associated with its correct microbial host was a hybrid 377 assembly (Table S3A). At the read-level, all carbapenemase gene variants and families were 378 linked to their expected microbial host (100%; 8/8). For NDM, rss resolved both expected hosts 379 in the mock community, assigning 78 NDM-carrying reads to Proteus mirabilis and 54 reads 380 to E. coli (Table S4A). 381 

Application to clinical metagenomics—Contig-level associations 382 

We then assessed the contig- and read-based plasmid associations in the rectal swab 383 (quasi)metagenomic data (Table S1B; Methods). # Discussion 528 

We show that the genomic and epigenetic information inherent to nanopore sequencing data 529 enables the association of clinically relevant carbapenemase genes with their bacterial hosts 530 in metagenomic and quasimetagenomic samples. While the long nanopore sequencing reads 531 facilitate the generation of contiguous assemblies for chromosomal and plasmid identification 532 from complex metagenomic data, methylation signatures encoded in the raw nanopore signal 533 enable the inference of associations between metagenomic assembly-based contigs and 534 individual sequencing reads through similar motif methylation. We leverage this information to 535 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license made available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 8, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.02.16.706128 doi: bioRxiv preprint

19 attribute carbapenemases as an example of clinically relevant AMR to their pathogenic hosts, 536 first validating our approach using mock communities from clinically relevant bacterial isolates, 537 then applying the optimized analysis pipeline to rectal swab metagenomics and 538 quasimetagenomic data. Comparison with established culture-based diagnostics and isolate 539 assemblies confirms that nanopore metagenomic data can associate both plasmid- and 540 chromosomally encoded resistance genes with their clinically relevant pathogenic hosts in the 541 tested samples. 542 We provide the first proof that plasmid-host associations derived from clinical metagenomics 543 and quasimetagenomics are feasible and concordant with established diagnostics. This is 544 especially relevant since the only available approach for nanopore-derived methylation-based 545 association is Nanomotif, which works on the metagenomic MAG level to refine metagenomic 546 binning (18,21,22,47). MAG recovery is assembly- and binning-dependent, so low-abundance 547 or divergent taxa can remain unbinned, biasing host–AMR gene links toward abundant 548 organisms (22). Especially in host-dominated clinical metagenomes, pathogen coverage may 549 often be too low for MAG recovery, causing clinically relevant AMR hosts to be missed. This 550 limitation was evident in the rectal swab datasets analysed in this study: Conventional 551 metagenomic assembly and binning recovered a pure MAG of the relevant carbapenem-552 resistant Enterobacterales species in only three of 16 metagenomic or quasimetagenomic 553 samples, and one of these three consisted of a single contig. On the contig and individual 554 sequencing read level, on the other hand, we were able to associate all carbapenemases 555 detected in the metagenomic and quasimetagenomic datasets with their correct bacterial host. 556 We also confirmed these associations by established diagnostics and whole-genome 557 assemblies of the relevant carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales isolates. 558 The contig-based pathogen detection and AMR association may offer a compromise between 559 bin- and read-level analyses. Contig-level analysis sits between reads and MAGs, retaining 560 AMR gene-carrying sequences that fail binning while providing longer context for taxonomic 561 assignment and resistance gene characterization. It has been shown that metagenomic 562 contigs can be robustly annotated on the bacterial strain level (48), which would enable 563 delineated strain-plasmid associations. We, however, also found that read-level associations 564 of AMR-encoding plasmids and pathogens can provide additional important evidence while 565 only resulting in a single potentially false plasmid-host association in our dataset. Read-level 566 associations can, for example, strengthen weak or unclear contig-level associations (e.g., Q6 567 and Q6), detect additional genes and associations missed by contig-level associations (e.g., 568 Q1 and M5), and sometimes result in higher gene resolution (e.g., Q8)—depending on where 569 the gene is located on the individual read and how good the assembly quality of the genomic 570 region is. Reads further have the advantage of being biological entities instead of 571 computational constructs such as contigs; contigs might fuse reads of different microbial 572 origins, precluding, for example, the association of one plasmid to several hosts (49). On the 573 read level, evidence can instead be accumulated across reads carrying the same AMR gene; 574 we here show that we can thus predict a potential multi-host scenario of a carbapenemase-575 carrying plasmid that would have been missed by contig-level analyses but was confirmed by 576 established diagnostics (e.g., Q1). 577 We further show that metagenomics can provide more granular pathogenic information than 578 established diagnostics. Matched WGS of rectal swab isolates confirmed that metagenomics 579 yields more detailed AMR gene subtype and pathogen strain information than established 580 diagnostics, a finding consistent with previous nanopore WGS studies (8). Crucially, 581 metagenomics can add context unavailable from routine diagnostics: whether an AMR gene 582 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license made available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 8, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.02.16.706128 doi: bioRxiv preprint

20 is linked to a pathogen, carried on a mobile plasmid, or embedded in a transmissible resistance 583 module. Although this does not yet replace phenotypic AST for therapy, it can guide infection 584 prevention and control escalation, transmission tracing, contact screening, and source 585 investigation (25). 586 Our study remains limited by several methodological constraints. Validation relied on cultured 587 isolates as the available reference standard, which may miss additional colonizing hosts and 588 underestimate true multi-host plasmid associations. While quasimetagenomic enrichment 589 improved sensitivity in comparison to metagenomics, it may alter community composition and 590 favor organisms that grow rapidly under the enrichment conditions. The contig and read 591 similarity scores are further newly developed metrics, requiring larger benchmarks to define 592 confidence thresholds, failure modes, and reporting standards. Finally, this evaluation focused 593 on carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales from rectal swabs, so performance should be 594 tested across additional sample types, taxa, AMR and other resistance and virulence genes, 595 plasmid backbones, and community structures. Mixed-strain colonization and closely related 596 species complexes might, for example, not allow for bacterial host delineation since strains of 597 the same species may share methylation motifs, assemble into fragmented or composite 598 contigs, or generate ambiguous plasmid-host links. Thus, our approach can support pathogen-599 host attribution, but strain-level plasmid assignment should be interpreted cautiously unless 600 supported by sufficient strain-specific sequence variation and coverage. This is particularly 601 relevant for clinically important species complexes, such as the K. pneumoniae species 602 complex, where routine diagnostics may not reliably distinguish closely related taxa (50). 603 Future benchmarking should include defined same-species and same-complex mixtures, 604 alongside clinical samples with independently confirmed mixed colonization. 605 In summary, through mock metagenomic communities and application to patient rectal swab 606 data, with cross-validation against established diagnostics and bacterial WGS, we 607 demonstrate that nanopore metagenomics can accurately associate plasmid- and 608 chromosomally encoded AMR genes with their clinically relevant pathogenic hosts. By 609 developing a contig- and read-based approach that overcomes limitations of standard binning, 610 we address an outstanding barrier to metagenomic diagnostics for pathogen and resistance 611 surveillance. Further sensitivity optimizations and diverse benchmarking and prospective 612 clinical validation studies remain warranted to test our approach’s generalizability and 613 establish it for routine surveillance and clinical practice. 614 615

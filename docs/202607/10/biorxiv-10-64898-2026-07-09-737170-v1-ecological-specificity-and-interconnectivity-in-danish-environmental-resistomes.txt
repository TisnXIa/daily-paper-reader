# Abstract 19 

20 

The environment plays a critical role in "One Health", facilitating the evolution, persistence, 21 

and dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). However, environmental 22 

resistomes have primarily been characterized through scattered studies of individual 23 

habitats, limiting our understanding of baseline resistome structure and connectivity 24 

across landscapes at a national level. Here, we analyzed metagenomes from 7,000 25 

Danish environmental samples (24 Tbp), collected from 21 distinct habitats including soils, 26 

sediments, aquatic environments and wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs). We 27 

identified core ARGs for establishing the environmental baseline, and habitat-associated 28 

indicator ARGs for facilitating source tracking. Using an additional 110 deep long-read 29 

metagenomes (9 Tbp data), we found a subset of cross-habitat commonly abundant 30 

ARGs was carried by diverse hosts and associated with mobile genetic elements, 31 

suggesting their potential role in resistome connectivity across ecosystems. Additionally, 32 

although natural habitats had much lower resistome relative abundance and 33 

transferability than human-associated habitats, some mobile environmental ARGs 34 

exhibited links to those in human pathogens. Our results support the implementation of 35 

environmental resistome surveillance by providing an environmental ARG baseline, 36 

enabling future ARG source-tracking and cross-habitat ARG dissemination assessment. 37 

38

horizontal gene transfer, ecological connectivity 40

# Introduction 41 

42 

Antibiotic resistance (AR) has been recognized as a growing threat to public health for 43 

many years 1. In Europe, annual deaths attributable to AR increased 26% over three years 44 

from 30,730 in 2016 to 38,710 in 2019 2. Globally, 1.27 million deaths were directly 45 

attributable to bacterial AR in 2019 3. Without action, the death toll is anticipated to 46 

overtake cancer 4. AR is ancient 5 and many clinically-relevant antibiotics are natural 47 

products synthesized as agents in presumed microbial chemical warfare. For example, 48 

glycopeptide biosynthesis and the associated self-resistance evolved approximately 150-49 

400 million years ago in Actinobacteria 6. While AR originates from microbial ecology and 50 

evolution 5, the current AR crisis, including its global spread and clinical impact, has been 51 

exacerbated by societal and environmental systems. 52 

53 

One key driver of the spread of AR is horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of antibiotic 54 

resistance genes (ARGs), determinants of resistance in bacteria. The ability of pathogens 55 

to rapidly acquire ARGs via HGT 7 has resulted in a growing demand for the development 56 

of new antibiotics, a process that is beset with practical and economic challenges 8.57 

Another major force underlying the spread of AR is the interconnection of human, animal, 58 

and environmental sectors, as summarised by the “One Health” framework. In brief, AR 59 

in one arena can be transmitted and propagated into another because of their inherent 60 

interconnectivity. For example, ARGs found in non-human settings can be acquired by 61 

human pathogens (for example, via human consumption of food animals), resulting in 62 

elevated rates of resistance 9,10 . To combat these risks, it is increasingly apparent that a 63 

concerted, systems-level effort that considers all sectors is required 11 .64 

65 

While substantial effort has been made to characterize AR in humans and animals in 66 

Denmark 12,13 , environmental AR has rarely been addressed as a systemic problem. The 67 

environment is a key player in the evolution and dissemination of ARGs 7, and is a 68 

reservoir for ARG acquisition and potential exchange with clinical pathogens 14,15 .69 

Previous studies have demonstrated the presence of near-identical ARGs encoded by 70 

soil bacteria and human pathogens. For example, one study observed near-identical 71

Here, we report analysis of the environmental resistome of Denmark and explore its 87 

features using a large-scale and geographically-dense sampling of 21 environmental 88 

habitat classes from the Microflora Danica (MFD) dataset 18 . Using 7,156 metagenomes, 89 

we present ARG diversity and abundance, and compare within- and between-habitat 90 

resistomes to identify a small but highly abundant fraction of core ARGs (i.e., presence in 91 

all samples in a habitat classification). In order to facilitate systems-level insights about 92 

interconnectivity, we identify indicator ARGs distinguishing environments, presenting 93 

potential targets for source-tracking. Deep long-read metagenomes for 110 samples 19,20

Through comparative genomics using these and other public genomes, we assess the 96 

HGT potential among human-, animal-, and environmentally-derived genomes, 97 

comparing different One Health sectors. This study provides an ecological framework for 98 

understanding the baseline ARGs that persist in the environment, ARGs that are 99 

characteristic of a habitat, and ARGs that are of higher potential in moving across habitats. 100 

Together, these insights provide a foundation needed to help inform future AR 101 

management efforts and to reveal how the environment is involved in ARG dissemination. 102

Sampling, DNA sequencing and quality control, and datasets acquisition 104 

In total, 7,156 sequenced samples were collected from environmental habitats across 105 

Denmark through collaborative sampling efforts in the Microflora Danica project (MFD) 18 .106 

These samples represent a broad range of natural and urban landscapes to map the 107 

national resistome profile (SI1 S1 Supplementary Methods). Detailed sampling, DNA 108 

extraction, DNA sequencing, and sequencing read quality control are described 109 

previously 18 . Briefly, extracted DNA samples were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 110 

6000 platform (PE150) producing a median depth of 5 Gbp and quality controlled using 111 

fastp 21 (v0.23.2) (see SI1 Figure S1 for workflow). 112

Freshwater; MFDO2: Standing freshwater; MFDO3: Lake, Rich pondweed. Our analyzed 118

Detection of core and indicator ARGs 170 

Core and indicator ARGs are particularly beneficial for long-term ARG monitoring, source-171 

tracking, and management. Core ARGs of a habitat were defined as those found in 100% 172 

samples of that habitat 38,39 . Candidate indicator ARGs were identified by the ‘indicspecies’ 173 

package 40 (v1.8.0, function multipatt ) using the ARG abundances in rpkm corrected by 174 

microbial fraction as input data matrix (delug=TRUE, statistical significance tested by 175 

permutation test of 999 permutations, and Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p-value <0.05). 176 

The association between an ARG and a biome was outputted as an indicator value, which 177 

is the product of detection specificity and sensitivity. Identified ARGs with a minimum 178 

indicator value of 0.5 (0-1, higher value means stronger indicator) and a minimum 0.5% 179 

relative abundances in all samples of a biome were characterized as indicator ARGs for 180 

that specific biome. 181

Measurement of ARG HGT potential within each habitat 204 

To investigate the ARG transfer potential in different sectors of the One Health cycle, we 205 

used MAGs and assemblies from Danish environmental samples, as well as published 206 

habitat-specific genomes of the environment and guts of healthy humans and farmed pigs. 207 

The mobility potential of ARGs was analyzed using the colocalization of ARGs and MGEs 208 

on assemblies and genomes, where the number of MGEs (i.e., transposases, integrases, 209 

and recombinases) within the assigned radii (0-10kb) 46 from each ARG were counted. 210 

For each identified ARG on a genome/MAG from a habitat, the distance between the 211 

midpoints of the seed ARG and each identified MGE on this genome were recorded. 212 

Distances were rounded to the nearest 100bp as a stepsize and filtered to a maximum of 213 

10kb (for example, 10kb represents 100 steps). The counts of MGEs at different distances 214 

(in stepsize) to this seed ARG were counted sequentially after ranking the distances to 215 

the ARGs ascendingly. Then, for each habitat, taking the median counts of MGEs for 216 

each distance to ARGs in stepsizes to represent the incidence of MGE for ARG at 217 

different distances. 218

# Results and discussion 265 

Danish environmental resistomes vary in abundance and diversity across 266 

habitats 267 

As the first step towards a Danish national environmental resistome survey, we 268 

investigated ARG abundance and diversity across 7,156 shallow metagenomes from the 269 

Microflora Danica project 18 broadly covering 3 different sample types (i.e., soil, sediment, 270 

and water), spanning 21 habitats from urban, natural, and subterranean areas (metadata 271 

in SI3). The ARG abundances varied across habitats and area types (all Kruskal-Wallis 272 

test p < 0.05, Figure 1a, SI1 Figure S4). We observed one order of magnitude difference 273 

in the total ARG abundances across different habitats (Figure 1a, SI2 Table S3), with the 274 

lowest abundance in natural saltwater sediments (median 45.3 rpkm microbial reads), 275 

and the highest in wastewater influents (median 1137.9 rpkm microbial reads). The post-276 

hoc analysis indicated that most sediment habitats had significantly lower ARG 277 

abundances compared to soils and waters (Dunn’s test with BH-p.adj < 0.05, SI1 Figure 278 

S5), which agreed with recent work of a smaller scale 57 . Salinity was associated with 279 

ARG abundance variation, particularly in sediment and water habitats, likely through its 280 

influence on microbial community composition 58 and its role as an ecological barrier for 281 

microbial and resistome dispersal across habitats 59 , thereby contributing to the observed 282 

differences in ARG abundances. (Figure 1a). 283 

Additionally, ARG abundances were associated with urbanization level (based on the 284 

habitat classification at the “Area type” level), as urban samples had significantly higher 285 

ARG abundances than natural samples (SI1 Figure S4b). Compared to non-Danish 286 

datasets, Danish ARG abundances in the WWTPs and farmed pig guts (SI1 Figure S6, 287 

ANOSIM, R = 0.809, P = 0.001), and human guts 60 were lower than those observed in 288 

other regions (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p-value <0.05). Compared to other One 289 

Health sectors in Denmark, data sourced from farmed pig guts had higher ARG 290 

abundances than data sourced from healthy human guts and our environmental data (SI1 291 

Figure S6, ANOSIM, R = 0.913, P = 0.001). The strong separation among dataset sources 292 

observed through ANOSIM indicates that between-source variation substantially 293

Resistome diversity. Danish environmental resistomes generally separated based on 298 

habitat in ordination analysis (Figure 1b), and such separation explained 17% of the 299 

resistome dissimilarity ( adonis2 PERMANOVA R 2 = 0.17, p=0.001, 999 permutations). 300 

Broadly, resistomes showed habitat-associated clustering among soils, sediments, and 301 

waters (Figure 1b), with partial overlap among some habitats and clear separation among 302 

others. Similar habitat-associated clustering was observed after spatial thinning (SI1 303 

Table S7), indicating that the pattern was robust to local sampling density. In general, soil 304 

resistomes shared ordination space, with the exception of “Temperate heath and scrub” 305 

and “Bogs, mires and fens” soils. These broad habitat classifications encompass 306 

substantial variation in environmental conditions (e.g. nutrient availability, pH and 307 

moisture), which is likely to support highly heterogeneous microbial communities and, 308 

consequently, more diverse resistome profiles 20 . This variation likely reflects underlying 309 

differences in microbial community structure, which was strongly associated with 310 

resistome composition (spatially-thinned subset in 10 km reference grid: Procrustes 311 

correlation = 0.87, protest P = 0.0001; Mantel r = 0,855, P < 0.001, SI1 Table S8), 312 

consistent with previous observations that microbial community composition is a major 313 

determinant of resistome composition 61 . In addition, salinity appeared to separate 314 

resistome profiles for sediment, soil, and water samples (Figure 1b), likely reflecting 315 

salinity-driven differences in microbial community composition. Saline and non-saline 316 

sediments and waters formed distinct clusters, and coastal soils were more dissimilar to 317 

natural and urban soils. Impact of salinity on resistome abundance and diversity has also 318 

been reported in oceans 62 . Interestingly, urban soil (i.e., “Soil, Urban, Greenspaces” with 319 

n=702) formed tighter resistome clusters compared to natural soils of comparable sample 320 

sizes (e.g., “Soil, Natural, Coastal” with n=242, “Soil, Natural, Dunes” with n=570, “Soil, 321 

Natural, Bogs, mires and fens” with n=514, Figure 1b). This pattern may partly reflect 322 

reduced microbiome diversity in human-disturbed soils compared with natural soils 18 ,323 

potentially contributing to resistome homogenisation among disturbed soils. Together 324

Core ARGs for the environmental baseline resistome 371 

Baseline assessment of ARGs in the environment can establish a factual foundation for 372 

multiple environmental monitoring objectives, including impact assessment and 373 

stewardship practices, but this baseline is largely lacking 70 . Here, we defined the baseline 374 

resistome of Denmark using “core” ARGs, i.e., those ARGs which are consistently 375 

detected across all samples within a habitat. Core ARGs thus represent a minimal, 376 

characteristic, and representative set of ARGs with stable associations to specific habitats. 377 

Based on short-read mapping results, we found that ARG subtypes of higher prevalence 378 

(detected in ≥80% samples in a given habitat) were generally of higher abundance 379 

compared to rarer ARGs with RPKM microbial read values typically in the range of 1-10 380 

and 0.1-1, respectively (Figure 2a). A total of 69 core ARG subtypes (representing 0.4-381 

11.8% of all ARGs detected in a given environment) were identified (Figure 2bce), 382 

representing the majority of total ARG abundances (7.5-81.6%, Figure 2f). These 383 

subtypes include macB , mexF , mexW , mexB , muxB , and novA , which have also been 384 

reported in other studies as core ARGs present in a variety of habitats 71–73 . The majority 385 

of other detected ARGs (i.e., 37-83% detected ARGs, Figure 2ae) were “rare” (i.e., 386 

detected in ≤ 10% samples in a given habitat), contributing to a small fraction of the total 387 

ARG abundances (i.e., 1-7%, Figure 2f). The consistent observation of a high abundance 388 

but low diversity of core resistomes in human, engineered, aquatic, and terrestrial 389 

samples 38,39,74 , could arise from conserved microbial physiological functions. Most of the 390 

core ARGs (44/69) encoded efflux pumps, which are intrinsic characteristics of microbial 391 

communities (e.g. in defense systems 74 , metabolic functions (C-, S-, and P-cycling) and 392 

signaling 15,74 (SI2 Table S4)). These genes are ubiquitous and have a broad range of 393 

substrates 58 , for example, macB has been implicated in iron scavenging 76 . While these 394 

genes are generally considered lower risk because they are often host-restricted and 395

Some ARGs show ecological connectivity 491 

While core and indicator ARGs offer insight into the resistome similarity within habitats 492 

and differences between habitats, they do not capture the potential spread of ARGs 493 

across environments. To investigate potential ARG connectivity across habitats, we 494 

examined ARGs that are commonly abundant across habitats for their nearby MGEs and 495 

microbial hosts. 496 

Among all the 1,810 detected ARG subtypes based on short-read metagenomes, a total 497 

of 150 ARG subtypes contributed to the top 80% of the total ARG abundance for any one 498 

habitat (SI1 Table S9, SI2 Table S8). Among these 150 ARGs, 83 were commonly 499 

abundant ARGs (found in at least two MFDO1 levels), suggesting their potential 500 

transferability across habitats (SI2 Table S8). We explored the genomic context of these 501 

83 ARGs using Nanopore (ONT) long-read sequencing from 110 samples representing 502 

11 habitats (87 MFD samples 18,19 and 23 Danish AS samples 20 ) (Figure 4a, SI2 Table 503 

S2). Most of the 83 commonly abundant ARGs found based-on short-read data were 504 

detected in the long read data (84.3%: 70/83). Of these, 68.7% (57/83) had MGE within 505 

5 kb radius of the ARG (SI2 Table S8, Figure 4bc), compared with 47% (216/460, SI2 506 

Table S9) for non-commonly-abundant ARGs, indicating a greater MGE association 507 

among commonly abundant ARGs and suggesting a greater potential for horizontal 508 

dissemination. A fifth (17/83) of these ARGs were frequently found next to MGE (i.e. in at 509 

least a third of the long reads from which these ARGs were identified, SI2 Table S8, SI1 510 

Table S10, Figure 4b), suggesting their frequent association with MGEs and high HGT 511 

likelihood. CARD searches further confirmed 14/17 MGE-associated ARGs have 512 

previously been reported to be plasmid-associated 83 (SI2 Table S8). 513 

Generally, we found that stronger associations between ARGs and MGEs indicated 514 

broader host ranges (Figure 4d). One set of these commonly abundant ARGs had limited 515 

associations to MGEs, but exhibited broad host ranges extending beyond the genus level. 516 

This likely stemmed from the long-term microbial evolution of specific ecological 517 

microbiota, such as the many vancomycin-ARGs in soil-dwelling vancomycin-producing 518

Environmental ARG mobility is shaped by habitats and ARG types, largely 546 

non-viral and pathogen-linked 547 

The spread of ARGs within and between One Health sectors (animal, human and 548 

environment), is key information needed to tackle current AR challenges. We next 549 

investigated HGT potential of the MFD data (assemblies and MAGs from the Nanopore 550 

datasets) and compared it to publicly available MAGs derived from human gut, pig gut, 551 

marine, and soils, encompassing human, animal, and environmental (global scale) 552 

sectors, while MFD data representing the environmental (Danish national-scale) sector, 553 

to determine whether ARG dissemination potential varied across habitats and ARG types. 554 

Overall, the environmental sector had lower ARG HGT potential compared to the clinical 555 

and animal sectors. As observed in Figure 5a, the incidence of MGEs is always higher in 556 

human pathogens than in animal guts and environmental habitats, regardless of the 557 

genetic distance from an ARG. This may be partly driven by the representation of human-558 

associated elements in reference databases. Additionally, although soils had the highest 559 

proportion of ARG-carrying MAGs (15.2%, 1,923/12,635 MAGs, SI Table S10) and multi-560 

ARG-carrying MAGs (27.6%, 530/1,923), they had the lowest proportion of ARG-carrying 561 

MAGs with a nearby MGE (11.6%, compared with 29.0% in AS and 38.3% in sediments, 562 

within 5kb radius of ARG). This suggests soils to be reservoirs of ARGs 85 where ARGs 563 

are largely maintained on the chromosome (Figure 5b) and shaped by long-term microbial 564 

evolution, but anthropogenic activity and environmental connectivity select and enrich 565 

those that are mobile. Furthermore, WWTPs had greater ARG HGT potential than soils 566

Although viruses are proposed as a key reservoir for ARG transfer in different studies 87 ,578 

there is no consensus on the contribution of viruses to ARG spread. In this study, viruses 579 

were rarely found to encode ARGs, suggesting that they are unlikely to substantially 580 

contribute to the spread of ARGs in Danish soils, sediments, and AS. Out of all the 7,042 581 

complete or HQ viral contigs, no viruses contained ARGs (SI2 Table S11a). Expanding 582 

the investigation to all 92,036 MQ and low-quality (LQ) viral contigs revealed only 4 583 

potential ARG-carrying LQ viral contigs in Danish AS and soil (Figure 5b). This suggests 584 

that viral carriage of ARGs may not be widespread (or may be unstable) in the 585 

environment 88,89 , but may be of selective advantage under human impact 90 . Our study 586 

used bulk metagenomes, which likely limits viral genome recovery compared with virus-587 

enriched viromes 91 . Given the limited detection of virus-borne ARGs, we note that it is 588 

not possible to draw strong conclusions about viral participation in ARG spread across 589 

Danish environmental habitats. Future efforts focused on viruses in ARG ecology could 590 

use deeper long-read sequencing or virus-enrichment approaches to recover more 591 

complete viral communities. 592

# Conclusion 629 

Our study provides the first national-scale cross-habitat resistome characterization by 630 

surveying more than 7,000 environmental samples, and using both the short-read and 631 

long-read sequencing data. Investigating the environmental resistome is an important part 632 

of the One Health approach to combating AR. For efficient and cost-effective 633 

environmental resistome surveillance, establishing baseline and target ARGs for 634 

actionable measures is essential. Here, we assess the distribution and spread of ARGs 635 

across Danish habitats, to record the ARGs that represent a consistent, naturally 636 

occurring "baseline". Additionally, the breadth of habitats covered enables investigation 637 

of candidate indicator ARGs, permitting ecological source-tracking and ARG “leakage” 638 

assessment between urban and natural habitats. By distinguishing baseline and indicator 639 

ARGs, our study provides a framework for prioritizing ARG signals relevant to monitoring 640 

and stewardship. Additionally, we show that despite the nationally-restricted and reduced 641 

antibiotic use in Danish livestock 99 , Denmark is not free from potential transmission 642 

between environmental and pathogen ARGs. This potential transmission is shown by the 643 

co-occurrence of MGE-associated highly-similar ARGs between human pathogens and 644 

the environment, implying that environmental ARG monitoring is highly relevant. 645 

While our findings reveal habitat-specific patterns in genes associated with AR, we must 646 

emphasise that large-scale resistome studies are unable to characterise ARG functions 647 

based on homology alone. For example, beta-lactam ARG identification is especially 648 

technically limited due to the sharing of functional domains and motifs between ARG and 649 

non-ARG sequences 100 . Future efforts to develop ARG-specific curation criteria for 650 

sequence identity and functional confidence, rather than relying solely on homology-651 

based detection in metagenomes, could substantially improve the certainty of ARG 652 

identification. In addition, linking ARGs to microbial hosts, MGEs, and transfer potential 653 

will further help resolve which environmental resistomes are most likely to contribute to 654 

future clinical resistance. Here, we aimed to develop a country-wide profile of all genes 655 

that directly and indirectly contribute to AR. This broad foundation is beneficial when 656 

planning for future scenarios under the selection pressures of different antibiotics. All in 657 

all, our metagenome-based profiling highlights the role of environmental habitats in 658

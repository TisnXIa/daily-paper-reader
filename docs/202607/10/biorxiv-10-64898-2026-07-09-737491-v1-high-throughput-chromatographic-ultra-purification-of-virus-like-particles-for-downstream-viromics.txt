# Abstract 19 

Virus -like particles (VLPs) are an abundant component of microbiomes with critical ecological 20 

rol es such as population control through viral predation and horizontal gene transfer. Studying 21 

the collection of viruses in microbiomes (the virome) through metagenomics has provided 22 

important insights into the composition and functions of VLPs in different environments. 23 

However, the current gold -standard method for VLP purification, CsCl density gradient 24 

ultracentrifugation (CsCl), is low throughput, time consuming and suffers from biases which 25 

limits the ability to study viromes in larger sample sets and ca n interfere with data interpretation. 26 

Here we present an anion exchange (AEX) chromatography -based approach for the purification 27 

of VLPs from microbiome samples that allows for significant increases in throughput and 28 

reproducibility while achieving VLP pur ity levels similar to or higher than CsCl. We used 29 

microbiome samples of known composition to first establish and evaluate the AEX approaches 30 

and compare them to CsCl. We implemented the AEX approach both for fast performance liquid 31 

chromatography (FPLC) a nd in multi -well plates. We compared the VLPs purified with CsCl 32 

and AEX using shotgun metagenomic sequencing and found that AEX performs similarly to or 33 

better than CsCl for purification of VLPs. AEX purified VLP -fractions captured significantly 34 

more vira l DNA compared to CsCl. We also found that both AEX and CsCl were capable of 35 

capturing viruses present at extremely low relative abundances (<0.001%). Additionally, we 36 

found that DNase digestion and CsCl may bias against filamentous phage morphologies. Fin ally, 37 

we purified VLPs from conventional murine feces using AEX and CsCl. AEX purified murine 38 

fecal VLPs had a much higher viral DNA content (85%) than CsCl (41%). While there were 39 

some differences in viral contigs assembled from AEX and CsCl VLP metagenom es, these 40 

method unique viral contigs made up only small proportions (<8%) of the relative abundance in 41

# Introduction 45 

Virus -like particles, VLPs, are defined as extracellular particles resembling viruses that 46 

may, or may not be, infectious and carr y nucleic acid 1. This general term was developed to 47 

describe filterable particles that fluoresce under epifluorescence microscopy with a DNA stain 48 

and therefore encompasses a wide repertoire of both viral and `virus -like` particles. Non -viral 49 

VLPs include ultrasmall filterable bacteria 2, gene transfer agents (GTAs) 3 and extracellular 50 

membrane vesicles (MVs) 4, all of which are non -infectious particles that carry bacterial DNA. In 51 

most environments, the majority of VLPs are bacterial viruses (bacteriophage) w hile eukaryotic 52 

viruses and other VLPs make up a smaller proportion 5–7. Bacteriophage (phage) play critical 53 

roles in microbiomes by controlling bacterial populations and contributing to nutrient cycling 54 

through predation and lysis 8, augmenting host fitness through auxiliary metabolic genes and 55 

lysogenic conversion 9,10 , and facilitating rapid and dynamic horizontal gene transfer through 56 

transduction 11 . Phage are becoming increasingly recognized as a catalyst for altering microbiome 57 

composition 12 and function 13 and even host physiology 14 –16 in mammalian hosts. 58 

Due to the importance of the collection of viruses, including phages, in microbiomes, 59 

more and more scientists are turning towards the study of viromes using metagenomic 60 

sequencing. To obtain “clean” me tagenomes of viromes that truly represent nucleic acid carried 61 

by VLPs rather than contamination from cellular organisms, VLPs must be ultra -purified from 62 

the associated microbiome. The current gold -standard method for VLP ultra -purification is 63 

cesium chlo ride density gradient ultra -centrifugation (CsCl), which usually follows an initial 64 

concentration, filtering and DNAse digest 17,18 . The use of density gradient ultra -centrifugation 65 

for VLP purification represents a major bottleneck for virome studies as on ly a limited number 66 

of samples can be purified in an ultra -centrifuge. Commonly, ultra -centrifuges have space for 6 67

In summary, the current methods used to purify VLPs from microbiome samples for 74 

metagenomic sequencing are in efficient, low throughput and suffer from biases . Relatively 75 

recently, l iquid chromatography methods ha ve been developed to efficiently concentrate and 76 

purify single VLPs from pure cultures of bacteriophages or eukaryotic viruses 24 . While 77 

tra ditional resin -based columns are not designed for large molecular weight particles like VLPs, 78 

monolithic columns, made from a porous material with large channels , are more suited for VLPs. 79 

The large channel size on monolithic columns reduces back pressure and associated shear forces 80 

and pr events exclusion of large biomolecules 24 –26 . Additionally, monolithic columns are flow 81 

rate independent which means that purifications can be scaled appropriately without concern for 82 

change in resolution. Monolithic chroma tography has already been recognized as an alternative 83 

to density gradients for purification of single VLPs 27 and many studies have successfully used 84 

monoliths for this purpose 27 –31 . Despite the routine use of monoliths for VLP purification from 85 

relatively simple sample matrices, the application of monoliths for simultaneous purification of 86 

diverse VLPs from complex sample matrices, such as generally required in viromics studies, has 87 

not been thoroughly assessed 32 . We performed a n evaluation of fast perform ance liquid 88

# Conclusions 311 

The challenges associated with CsCl purifications are a barrier for widespread and large -312 

scale viromics research. Here, we show that AEX chromatography is a reliable alternative to the 313 

gold standard CsCl method for VLP purification prior to DNA sequencing. AEX 314 

chromatography significantly reduces sample processing time, allows for automation when 315 

FPLC is used and parallelized processing of large sample numbers when multi -well plates are 316 

used, and improves viral purity compared to CsCl purifications. The pro portion of bacterial DNA 317 

in the VLPs purified by both the LC and Well chromatography methods was consistently lower 318 

than in the CsCl -purified VLPs. While chromatography -purified VLPs were not devoid of 319 

bacterial DNA, we found that in gnotobiotic mouse samp les with phage spike -ins, much of the 320 

bacterial DNA was associated with carriage of bacterial DNA in VLPs (Fig. 2). Similarly, we 321 

found evidence of VLP -packaged bacterial DNA in conventional murine fecal microbiomes, 322

to the LC -purified VLPs. Ultimately, however, future work using approaches such as electron 324 

microscopy and/or proteomics will be needed to definitively determine if this bacterial DNA is 325 

packaged into VLP s or free. 326 

While the LC method has the advantage of automation using autosamplers and automated 327 

fraction collectors, the Well method has a throughput advantage. A standard table -top centrifuge 328 

with a 4 -bucket swing -out rotor can be used to process 4 x 24 -well plates (96 samples) in parallel 329 

thereby significantly increasing throughput and making large -scale viromics experiments 330 

feasible. AEX multi -well plates also exist in 96 -well format, which allows for increased 331 

throughput, however the volume that can be loaded in each well is much smaller, making use of 332 

96 -well plates only applicable if sample concentrations are very high and thus required loading 333 

volumes low. Interestingly, we did notice differences between the LC and Well -purified VLP -334 

fractions despite the use of the same AEX column material and purification method. For 335 

example, the ES18 phage did not elute off the Well columns as efficiently as it eluted off the LC 336 

column and conversely, the M13 phage was present at higher relative abundances in the We ll 337 

Elute fractions compared to the LC. This variability may be due to differences in processing 338 

caused by the fact that the Well plate was loaded, washed, eluted, and CIPed with multiple 339 

separate loads that were completely spun through the column material, while in the LC method a 340 

continuous flow of buffer occurs and the column never falls dry during sample purification. 341 

Future work can use diverse monolithic column types, buffer systems and elution 342 

gradients to optimize VLP purification methods for stud ying viromes from specific environments 343 

or to better enrich specific viruses or VLP types. For example, monolithic hydrophobic 344 

interaction (HIC) and multi -modal columns (combining AEX and HIC) have both been 345

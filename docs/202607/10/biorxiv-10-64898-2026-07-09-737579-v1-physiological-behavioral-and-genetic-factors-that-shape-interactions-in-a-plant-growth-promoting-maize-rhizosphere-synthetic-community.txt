Abstract 13 

Profiling microbiomes is an important way to understand the function and composition of 14 

communities in the wild, but natural microbiomes are often highly comple x and often 15 

unamendable to experimentation to reveal cause and effect relationships . By using a small group 16 

of cultivable strains t o represent those found in the wild , synthetic communities are one solution 17 

to this problem . Here we describe the MA ize Rhizosphere Synthetic Community (MARSc), a 18 

genome -enabled 31 -member bacterial community representative of the diversity found on the 19 

roots of maize grown in Iowa soils. This community is built around Pseudomonas putida 20 

KT2440, a model maize rhizosphere colonist and synthetic biology chassis. We characterized 21 

microbe -microbe interactions and biofilm formation of MARSc members in a variety of 22 

environmental contexts, finding that both behaviors are broadly controlled by nutrient levels. 23 

Genomic analysis and microbiome profiling of these organisms revealed that annotated biofilm 24 

genes (such as surface attachment and exopolysaccharide production) correlated to rhizosphere 25 

colonization, but neither trait correlated to in vitro biofilm formation . In vitro interactions assay 26 

findings were surprisingly consistent with co -correlations of rhizosphere abundance amongst 27 

MARSc members on roots. Finally, we found that when applied to the roots, MARSc can 28 

increase maize growth under nitrogen -limiting conditions . Altogether, MARSc is a useful tool 29 

for identify ing some of the factors influencing rhizosphere microbiome assembly and will be a 30 

strong foundation for further work in this area. 31

Introduction 46 

The microbiome is known to have vast effects on the environment around it . In plants, this 47 

community can be manipulated in ways that address current issues , includ ing alleviating abiotic 48 

stresses such as drought (1 –4) and heat (5, 6) , protection from disease (7 –9) , or the need to 49 

decrease synthetic nitrogen inputs (10, 11) . Some microbes can even help increase the food 50 

supply via plant growth promotion (12 –14) . The rhizosphere microbiome hosts many 51 

interactions between soil, microbe s, and plant s, making the establishment of causal relationships 52 

difficult and hinder ing our ability to predictably alter its composition (15, 16) .53 

Both cooperative and competitive interactions may alter community structure , frequently relying 54 

on secreted pu blic goods . Q uorum sensing molecules like autoinducer -2 (AI -2) can regulat e55 

biofilm production , and siderophores such as pyoverdine can control iron competition (17, 18) .56 

Bacteria can produc e phytohormones such as auxins (19) , and plants can respond by exud ing 57 

metabolites through their roots (20) . Up to 40% of photosynthates are secreted into the 58 

rhizosphere , and the ir composition can significantly affect microbial metabolism (21 –23) .59 

Benzoxazinoids are highly abundant in maize root exudate s and act as an anti -microbial for some 60 

an d a chemoattractant for others, such as plant growth -promoting Pseudomonas putida (24 –26) .61 

The predominant secreted maize benzoxazinoid is DIMBOA (2,4 -dihydroxy -7-methoxy -1,4 -62 

benzoxazin -3-one) , which rapidly breaks down into MBOA (6 -methoxybenzoxazolin -2-one) that 63 

can linger in the soil for months (27) , thereby having a strong influence on microbial community 64 

structure (24, 28) . Stressed plant s can also alter root exudat ion patterns to recruit specific 65 

microbes to facilitate desirable interactions (29) . Collectively, diffusible metabolites shap e the 66 

rhizosphere microbiome in a multitude of ways , including how bacteria interact with one 67 

another .68 

Most bacteria are believed to live in multispecies biofilms (30) , which may be influenced by one 69 

an other’s secreted metabolites. Given the benefits of the biofilm lifestyle, including protection 70 

from environmental stressors and enhanced access to nutrient -rich root surfaces , t he ability to 71 

form biofilms likely contributes to successful rhizosphere colonization (31 –38) . Biofilm 72 

formation is co ntext -dependent, such that the type and availability of nutrients could influence 73 

cooperative and competitive behaviors. Several s tudies have shown root exudates have 74 

stimulated biofilm formation by multiple genera, including Bacillus and Pseudomonas (39 –43) .75 

One approach to understanding the complex rhizosphere interactions is to use a synthetic 76 

community (SynCom) , comprised microbes isolated from the plant root s (44) . SynComs have 77 

been used to identify keystone species in microbiomes and in biofilms, discover emergent 78 

behaviors, and untangl e biofilm formation processes and rhizosphere competence traits (9, 45 –79 

47) . Moreover, SynCom s can link genomic information with microbial behaviors , improv ing our 80 

ability to predict phenotype from genotype . W hen coupled with plant colonization data , this may 81 

reveal traits required for successful colonization .82

Here we describe the Maize Rhizosphere Synthetic community (MARSc) as a model for the 83 

maize rhizosphere microbiome . MARSc was designed around Pseudomonas putida KT2440 84 

(proposed to be re -named P. alloputida (48) ), a model rhizosphere colonist and tractable 85 

synthetic biology chassis organism (49 –52) . We hypothesize d that bacteria isolated from the 86 

roots of maize grown in a long -term cropping system with low inorganic nitrogen inputs are 87 

more likely to possess traits related to the alleviation of low nitrogen stress than bacteria isolated 88 

from conventionally grown maize . By exploring microbe -microbe interactions , measuring 89 

biofilm formation , and min ing the genomes of MARSc members , we provid e insight into traits 90 

predictive of root colonization and competitiveness in the rhizosphere of maize grown under 91 

varying nitrogen fertilizer inputs . Importantly, MARSc increased maize growth under nitrogen -92 

limiting conditions . Altogether, MARSc has enabled us to identify a variety of factors 93 

influencing rhizosphere microbiome assembly, laying the foundation for further work in this 94 

area. 95

Development of the MA ize Rhizosphere Synthetic community (MARSc) 98 

We isolated bacteria from the soil, rhizosphere , and endosphere of maize grown in soil that 99 

received low inorganic fertilizer inputs as part of a long -term cropping system study (53) . More 100 

than 400 isolates were classified by 16S rRNA sequencing , from which we selected 40 isolates 101 

that represented the taxonomic diversity previously reported for maize rhizosphere s in the same 102 

soil (54, 55) . We screened these isolates for their interactions with each other and Pseudomonas 103 

putida KT2440 , by measuring zones of inhibition or stimulation of growth , where the spot is the 104 

“producer” and the lawn is the “receiver” (Figure 1B) . We examined these interactions on a 105 

variety of nutrient rich (King’s B [KB]; ½ -strength Trypticase soy agar [TSA ]) or more nutrient 106 

limited (R2A) conditions , with or with out FeCl 3. While each medium had unique interactions 107 

(Figure s 1A, S1), R2A had slightly less inhibitory and more stimulatory interactions than the 108 

richer ½-TSA , and 74% of interactions were consistent between the two (Figure 1C ). When 109 

comparing organisms that grew on all 3 media, the prevalence and magnitude of inhibition was 110 

great er on high -nutrient KB and lowest on low -nutrient R2A . Through these assays , we 111 

eliminated phenotypically redundant isolates, selecting 31 members for MARSc, representing 112 

phylogenetically diverse genera (Figure 2, Table S1 ) (56) .113 

We further characterized phenotypes that might influence plant -microbe or microbe -microbe 114 

interactions , including production of AI -2, alkaline proteases, and indole acetic acid (IAA) , as 115 

well as IAA degradation (Table s S2-S3). Autoinducer -2 production was rare while extracellular 116 

protease production was common, with varying levels of production. Six MARSc members make 117 

IAA, and four Betaproteobacteria can degrade it, including Variovorax R-27 , a genus shown to 118 

reverse the inhibition of root growth promotion caused by many other bacteria (47) .119 

Interactions can le ad to emergence of inhibitory, stimulatory, or surface colonial expansion 120 

phenotypes 121

Influence of root exudates and soil extracts on growth and biofilm formation 175 

Since r hizosphere microb es live at the interface between the root and the soil , we explored how 176 

R2A media supplemented with soil extracts (SE) and maize root exudates (RE) influence d177 

growth and biofilm forming capabilities. RE significantly stimulated growth of Agrobacterium 178 

E-04 , Pedobacter R-06 , and Exiguobacterium R-17 , while growth of Ralstonia R-29 was 179 

inhibited . SE stimulated the growth of Micro coccus S-10 and Pedobacter R-06 and inhibited 180 

growth of Variovorax R-27 and Luteibacter E-22 (Figure 3A, Figure S5) . SE had a limited effect 181 

on biofilm formation , while RE was very stimulatory (Figure 3B), though increased biofilm 182 

formation may reflect the stimulatory effect of RE on growth in general. However, s ome strains, 183 

including Bacillus S-18, only form biofilm in the presence of RE , despite no significant growth 184 

promotion by RE . Moreover, biofilm formation in response to the amendments frequently 185 

occur red differently for two strains of the same genus (Streptomyces E-08 and R -07; 186 

Pseudoduganella S-14 and R -31 ; Pseudomonas KT2440 and E -05 ). There were no strains that 187 

were only stimulated by SE . Collectively, RE had a greater influence on growth and biofilm 188 

formation , possibly th rough nutrients or signals that promote biofilm formation. 189

Effects of MBOA on growth and biofilm formation 200 

One major component of young maize root exudates are benzoxazinoids, which are known to 201 

shape the maize rhizosphere microbiome (24, 28, 60) . Of these, MBOA is the most prevalent in 202 

both concentration and persistence in the rhizosphere (27) and the most inhibitory to bacteria 203 

(24) . We examined the influence of MBOA on growth and biofilm formation using a tolerance 204 

index (24) , which reflects growth or biofilm formation across a range of MBOA concentrations ,205 

compared to a control . For planktonic cultures, MBOA inhibited the growth of most 206 

Pseudomonadota isolates, while other phyla were unaffected, though it stimulated growth of 207 

Microbacterium E-13 (Figure 4A) . Tolerance to MBOA has been partly linked to cell wall 208 

structure (24) , which may explain why the Actinomycetota and Bacillota are fairly toleran t. The 209 

significant growth inhibition of Ralstonia R-29 by MBOA may be a contributing factor to the 210 

growth inhibition observed in RE (Figure 3). 211 

MBOA influenced biofilm formation differently from planktonic growth/tolerance . In R2A , 13 212 

strains formed biofilm , and MBOA amendments inhibited biofilm formation in 3, stimulated 3, 213 

and the rest were unaffected (Figure 4B). Several Pseudomonadota strains inhibited by MBOA 214 

in planktonic cultures were unaffected during biofilm formation , indicating biofilm lifestyle may 215 

facilitate MBOA toleran ce . Intriguingly , Microbacterium E-13 did not form biofilm in any other 216 

condition tested but produced a striking dark red biofilm that dramatically increased with 217 

increasing MBOA concentrations (Figure 4C). Many maize rhizosphere competent colonists, 218 

such as Microbacterium sp ecies , can degrade MBOA to the red -pigmented AMPO (2 -amino -7-219 

methoxyphenoxazin -3-one) by the lactonase BxdA (24, 61) . BLASTP for BxdA in the 220 

Microbacterium E-13 genome (56) identified a lactonase capable of converting MBOA to 221 

AMPO. Yet, while many maize rhizosphere Sphingobium species isolates are s trong AMPO -222 

formers (24, 61) , Sphingobium R-21 does not appear to be capable of MBOA degradation despite 223 

possess ing 3 similar (62 -65% identity) BxdA orthologs . Des pite this, Sphingobium R-21 was not 224 

inhibited by MBOA , suggesting that while not visibly forming AMPO, R -21 is likely MBOA 225 

tolerant like other Sphingobium species. Collectively, our findings indicate that the biofilm 226 

lifestyle may influence MBOA tolerance, which could in turn influence maize rhizosphere 227 

competence. 228

Correlations between annotated genes and biofilm formation 265 

We were curious whether PLant -associated BActeria (PLaBAse) (64) gene categories could be 266 

used to predict biofilm formation by MARSc strains . We found no correlation between the 267 

number of annotated genes related to biofilm formation and in vitro biofilm form ation (Figure 268 

6A), nor was any one gene or group of genes predictive of biofilm formation . For example, 269 

Pseudoduganella S-14 was consistently one of the strongest biofilm formers yet had significantly 270 

fewer biofilm -related genes than Pseudoduganella R-31 , which formed less biofilm than S-14 271 

(Figure 6A). Moreover, there was no correlation between PLaBAse gene categori es and biofilm 272 

formation or MBOA biofilm tolerance , although there were several strong primarily negative 273 

correlations with planktonic MBOA tolerance. Interestingly, the phytohormone gibberellin and 274 

GABA production gene categories had significant positive correlations to both biofilm and 275 

MBOA tolerance (Figure 6B). GABA is also associated with regulating the switch between 276 

planktonic and biofilm lifestyles in some Pseudomonads (65) , which could explain positive 277 

correlations with biofilm formation . The strong correlation between gibberellin production genes 278 

and MBOA tolerance is likely due to a single ferredoxin gene found in 9 MARSc members , of 279 

which only Methylobacterium R-20 was significant ly inhibit ed by MBOA (Figure 4A ). 280 

Other genes correlated to biofilm formation include polysaccharide production ( algD ,281 

pgaC |icaA ) and a cyclic -di -GMP regulator (hsbR ). Polysaccharide production is a vital element 282 

of biofilm formation and the HsbR -HsbA system has been shown to regulate the switch between 283 

sessile and planktonic lifestyles in P. aeruginosa (37, 66) . Seven MARSc members possess at 284 

least one of the two. Curiousl y, pgaC |icaA is negatively correlated to biofilm formation , despite 285 

its role in PGA polysaccharide production (67) . Importantly, liaR is positively correlated with 286 

biofilm formation in the presence of MBOA (Figure 6C) ; LiaR regul ates cell envelope stress 287 

response and contributes to antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation (68 –70) . liaR orthologs 288 

are present in 22 members of MARSc , including all Actinomycetota and Bacillota . Notably, E-289 

13, E -08, and E -05 (Figure 4B) all possess liaR orthologs , which suggest s that the ability to 290 

adjust to cytoplasmic membrane -perturbing compounds is an important trait for biofilm 291 

formation in the presence of MBOA . The negative correlation between violacein production 292 

genes vioABE and biofilm formation in MBOA is likely due to the strong inhibition of MBOA on 293 

the growth of Pseudoduganella , the only MARSc members with vioABE (Figure 4B) .294

Discussion 365 

Genome -enabled SynComs are important tool s for revealing mechanisms behind microbiome 366 

assembly and function, including emergent behaviors that can arise within communities. In this 367 

study, we created MARS c to represent the observed phylogeny of bacteria on maize roots at the 368 

developmental stage in which maize is poised for high nitrogen uptake , with the rationale that 369 

plant selection for a microbiome facilitating nitrogen acquisition would be in place by then (54, 370 

55) . MARSc was centered around the model rhizosphere colonist and synthetic biology chassis 371 

P. putida KT2440 , providing biotechnological flexibility . We assessed a range of physiological, 372 

behavioral, and genetic factors to not only create the SynCom but also for predicting interactions 373 

amongst members and their influence on plant health. MARSc members are rhizosphere 374 

competent and, importantly, MARSc exhibits nitrogen biofertilizer capabili ties. Since plant 375 

growth promotion is observed in a synthetic growth medium, one or more MARSc members 376 

likely directly influences plant nitrogen acquisition. Yet, rhizosphere competency and plant 377 

growth promoting properties were not fully apparent from in vitro assays or genome mining. 378 

In vitro interactions among members of MARSc were broadly controlled by nutrient level s, 379 

which is consistent with the stress -gradient hypothesis : oligotrophic conditions favor neutral or 380 

mutualistic interactions, while copiotrophic conditions favor competition (75, 76) . Inhibitory 381 

interactions were generally “producer -determined ”, likely resulting from the production of 382

receivers (45, 57) . Further , the frequency and magnitude of many inhibitory interactions were 384 

defined by iron availability. Notably, inhibition by Pseudomonads was due to production of 385 

pyoverdines, making iron less available for other s and inhibiting their growth. In contrast, 386 

stimulatory interactions were “receiver -determined ”, likely arising from nutrient limitations that 387 

are r elieved by nearby organisms produc ing a limiting metabolite , such as with Paenibacillus E-388 

12 . Stimulation of colonial expansion /motility was an unexpected interaction between some 389 

MARSc members . Most prominently , Dyadobacter E-01 moved great distances , in a swarming -390 

like pattern, when spotted atop other organisms , likely due to their prod uction of surfactants ,391 

EPS , and/or signal molecules . As described elsewhere (45) (77, 78) , this emergent behavior only 392 

occurred in low nutrient media and involved a Bacteroidota . Ecological advantages for this 393 

cooperativity are unclear , but it may facilitate nutrient acquisition .394 

The importance of diffusible metabolites was evident in the strong influence of root exudates ,395 

soil extract, and the root metabolite MBOA on growth and biofilm formation properties, 396 

although those traits were not predictive rhizosphere competence . Root exudate s had more of an 397 

effect than soil extracts on planktonic growth , and stimulation of planktonic growth did not 398 

reflect stimulation of biofilm formation . While o ther studies have shown that in vitro biofilm 399 

formation or MBOA tolerance can correlate with rhizosphere colonization (22, 24, 79, 80) , we 400 

did not observe similar correlations, even for Microbacterium E-13 , where biofilm formation 401 

emerges when exposed to MBOA. Surprisingly , planktonic MBOA tolerance was generally 402 

negatively correlated with rhizosphere abundance. One possibility is that individuals who are 403 

less tolerant of MBOA rely on other microbes in the community to degrade it , such as by E -13 404 

and possibly by other MARSc members as well. Consistent with a synergistic community -level 405 

interaction was our observation that biofilm formation by individual strains exposed to 406 

metabolites produced by others in the absence of cell -to -cell contact . Phyla/ class -level drop -outs 407 

of community members did not significantly change biofilm formation, suggests there is 408 

functional redundancy with in our SynCom in modulating the metabolite environment . While 409 

spent media from individual MARSc members could replicate the inhibition it did not typically 410 

replicate enhancement of biofilm formation observed in filter plates . Growth in the presence of 411 

spent media can be stimulated by decreased concentrations of inhibitory compounds or by 412 

increased availability of nutrients secreted by other microbes ( cross -feedin g) and can be inhibited 413 

by antibacterial compounds (81, 82) . The influence of s ynergistic interspecies cooperation and 414 

active metabolite exchange in biofilm formation is clear (83, 84) , yet how it influence s the 415 

rhizosphere competence of MARSc members over time is less clear .416 

The best predictor of rhizosphere competence was based on genome mining, where genes for 417 

EPS production and plant derived substrate usage were significantly positively correlated with 418 

MARSc member rhizosphere abundance ; both trait s are known to be associated with rhizosphere 419 

colonization and /or biofilm composition (37, 85, 86) . While biofilm formation may be an 420 

important rhizosphere competence trait , our approach of only examining single -species biofilms 421 

in vitro may not adequately reflect what occurs in mixed species communities. We observed that 422 

an average of 33% of the PLaBAse defined plant -derived substrate usage genes were 423 

significantly positively correlated with rhizosphere abundance. No other gene category was 424 

represented as substantially as plant -derived substrate usage, whether there was a positive or 425

Excitingly, we observed that MARSc increased the shoot biomass of maize under medium /hig h428 

nitrogen fertilization levels when compared to untreated controls , when planted in a gnotobiotic 429 

synthetic substrate . An earlier iteration of MARSc showed growth promotion in a soil -sand mix 430 

with a history of low inorganic nitrogen inputs (71) , but that soil contained high levels of organic 431 

nitrogen and an indigenous microbiota . These findings indicate that MARSc biofertilizer 432 

capabilities is not solely due to enhanced soil nitrogen mineralization , as our synthetic substrate 433 

did not include organic matter . Alternatively, plant growth promotion could be a consequence of 434 

the ability of MARSc to manipulate phytohormone levels in the rhizosphere . Ten MARSc 435 

members can either produce or degrade the phytohormone IAA , which has been linked to growth 436 

stimulation of plants (47) and 6 of th ese were present in greater than 1% relative abundance in 437 

the rhizosphere . Moreover, Pseudoduganella R-31 and S -14 are Oxalobacteraceae , which can 438 

enhance maize growth under nitrogen -limiting conditions (87) . It’s possible that these MARSc 439 

members stimulate root development independent of IAA production or degradation, despite 440 

the ir low abundan ce in the rhizosphere. 441 

In vitro interactions assay findings were surprisingly consistent with co -correlations of 442 

rhizosphere abundance amongst MARSc members on roots. For example, t he prevalence of 443 

negative interactions between P. putida KT2440 and other MARSc members was consistent 444 

between experiments , while the rest of MARSc is dominated by positive correlations. 445 

Pseudomonas E-05 exhibited inhibitory interactions in copiotrophic conditions and stimulatory 446 

interactions in oligotrophic conditions ; in the rhizosphere it had primarily positive co -447 

correlations, which may indicate the rhizosphere is an oligotrophic growth environment. 448 

Variovorax R-27 also had primarily negative co -correlations in the rhizosphere , which 449 

corresponds to its frequent inhibition by other MARSc members in vitro . Finally, Paenibacillus 450 

E-12 ’s growth was stimulated by nearly all MARSc members, which corresponds to a high 451 

number of positive co -correlations in the rhizosphere and increased growth in the presence of 452 

MARSc metabolites .453 

Here, we have describe d MARSc, a plant -growth -promoting consortium resource for untangling 454 

the complex microbe -microbe and microbe -plant interactions occurring in the maize rhizosphere .455 

Modeled after the maize rhizosphere community of Midwest USA corn belt soils , it has the 456 

potential to elucidate biofertilizer mechanisms, an attractive biotechnology solution to reduce 457 

inorganic fertilizers inputs into these agroecosystems. With this consortium w e found that 458 

interactions between members are broadly controlled by nutrient levels, and metabolite exchange 459 

among st members can change their behavior. Moreover, in vitro interactions of individual 460 

members were commonly consistent with rhizosphere colonization properties, although genome 461 

mining reveled EPS production and surface attachment were also correlated to colonization. 462 

Going forward, these findings lay the foundation for building our understanding of the 463 

interactions that shape microbiome assembly and establish MARSc as a model for studying 464 

interactions in the maize rhizosphere as well as investigating mechanisms of plant -growth -465 

promotion .466

Abbreviated Methods 468 

An expanded version of the methods below can be found in the supplemental information. 469 

Isolation of MARSc members and genome annotation 470 

Bacteria were isolated from the bulk soil, rhizosphere, or endosphere of maize grown in soil with 471 

low inorganic nitrogen inputs from a long -term cropping system study site (55) . MARSc 472 

genomes (56) were annotated with Bakta (88) , reCOGnizer (89) , and PLaBAse (64) .473 

Interaction assays 474 

We assessed all pairwise interactions between MARSc members by spreading isolates onto solid 475 

medium, then spotting with others (45) . Plates were incubated at 30˚C and zones of inhibition or 476 

stimulation were measured daily in millimeters. 477 

Single strain biofilms 478 

Overnight cultures were diluted to OD 600 = 0.1 in fresh medium, then 10 μL of culture was added 479 

to 180 μL of media in a microplate with peg lids (90) . Plates were incubated at 27˚C. Lids were 480 

removed, rinsed in deionized water to remove planktonic cells, and dried. Biofilms were stained 481 

with crystal violet, decolorized with 33% acetic acid, and quantified by OD 540 . MBOA’s effect on 482 

biofilm formation was assessed by adding various concentrations to the medium, and the data 483 

summarized using a tolerance index (24) .484 

MARSc interaction biofilm assays 485 

We used Transwell 96 -well plates (filter plates) as a base for the biofilm peg lids (62) . MARSc 486 

members were cultivated and diluted as above and randomly assigned to wells. The plates were 487 

incubated at room temperature (~22˚C). Lids were rinsed, stained, and quantified as above. The 488 

liquid from the plate reservoir was filtered and then used in spent media biofilm assays with 489 

individual strains using a 50/50 mix of fresh and spent media. Drop -out biofilm assays were 490 

conducted the same as the Transwell assays, with omission of each phylum/ Proteobacteria class. 491 

MARSc rhizosphere colonization and maize growth promotion assays 492 

Strains were grown in R2A, diluted to OD 600 = 0.1 in phosphate buffer (PB), and mixed to 493 

generate the inoculum. Pre -germinated Phz51 seedlings (91) were incubated in PB or inoculum 494 

for one hour. Seedlings were planted in an autoclaved Turface mix and watered with Murashige 495 

and Skoog plant growth medium with NH 4NO 3 (92) . For the rhizosphere colonization assay, SE 496 

was also included, and plants were grown for 21 days. Plants were harvested and the majority of 497 

the loose substrate from roots was removed. Roots were placed in PB, vortexed, and sonicated to 498 

wash off rhizosphere soil. The rhizosphere washate was centrifuged and the pellet was frozen for 499 

DNA extraction. For the maize growth promotion assay, plants were grown for 34 days, then 500 

shoots were removed, dried, and weighed. 501

(GAGTTTGATYMTGGCTCAG) and 1492R (CGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT) 16S rRNA 506 

primers. DNA was PCR amplified, verified by gel electrophoresis, and pooled into libraries by 507

Abstract 1

Enzymatic depolymerization of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) has advanced rapidly, alongside 2

a growing volume of publicly available metagenomic data from microbial communities under 3

sustained selective pressure from plastic exposure. Reasoning that such environments may 4

harbor underexplored polyester-active enzymes, we developed a targeted mining workflow that 5

screens exclusively plastic-associated datasets through multi-step bioinformatic filtering— 6

integrating catalytic-motif screening, disulfide-topology validation, structural-similarity scoring, 7

and phylogenetic profiling—to recover high-confidence PETase candidates. Applied to 277 8

plastic-associated metagenomes, the pipeline yielded 21 non-redundant candidates, several of 9

which combine the Type I catalytic motif (GHSMGGGG) with Type II-like extended loops and 10 

secondary disulfide bonds. Two candidates were experimentally confirmed as PET hydrolases; 11 

the more active, PET -KR1, is a thermostable enzyme ( Tm = 66.5 °C) that depolymerizes PET across 12 

a broad temperature range, with markedly higher productivity on powdered than on film 13 

substrate. PET -KR1 achieved optimal depolymerization at 50 °C, yet at 60–65 °C, where total 14 

yields declined, the product pool was more strongly enriched in the terminal monomer TPA, 15 

suggesting that thermostability and substrate accessibility are the primary targets for further 16 

engineering. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed a conserved hydrophobic binding network 17 

around the catalytic serine, consistent with established PETase substrate-recognition modes, and 18 

rational disulfide engineering raised the melting temperature by 3.5 °C, confirming amenability 19 

to further optimization. Overall, PET -KR1 expands the scaffold space available for PETase 20 

engineering, while the discovery workflow, built entirely on publicly available tools and open-21

Introduction 8

Global plastic production has risen from 2 million tons in 1950 to over 430 million tons in 2024 1 .9

It is estimated that 60% of the total produced plastics have been discarded in landfills and the 10 

environment with 4.8 to 12.7 million tons entering the oceans each year 2,3 . Postconsumer plastic 11 

waste is mainly managed by landfill disposal (~25%), incineration (~40%), and recycling (<30%) 12  

> 2,4

. Incineration decreases waste volume but releases harmful emissions, making it less favorable 13 

despite energy recovery 5 . Growing environmental awareness has driven the demand for 14 

improved recycling technologies and policies to reduce plastic use and enhance recyclability 6–8 .15 

Plastics are currently recycled mainly through mechanical or chemical routes 9 . While mechanical 16 

recycling can reduce environmental impacts, it is significantly limited by the complexity of plastic 17 

compositions, inefficient sorting processes, and the reduced quality of the recycled products 9,10 .18 

Chemical recycling, on the other hand, holds promise for advancing a sustainable circular 19 

economy 11 . However, its implementation is currently constrained by high energy demands and 20

the toxicity of chemical catalysts, which translate into elevated operational costs and 1

environmental footprint 11,12 . Addressing these challenges requires innovative approaches to 2

increase recycling rates and simultaneously establish techno-economic feasibility and 3

sustainability. 4

Biocatalytic recycling, which leverages microorganisms or enzymes to depolymerize plastics, 5

presents an eco-friendly alternative to conventional recycling methods 13 . In particular, enzyme-6

catalyzed depolymerization has shown significant promise, with 495 documented plastic-active 7

enzyme activities cataloged in PAZy: The Plastics-Active Enzymes Database 14 . Among these, 320 8

enzymes are identified as polyethylene terephthalate (PET) hydrolases (PETases), with others 9

displaying specific activity against polyurethane (PUR) and polyamide (PA) 14 . Notably, other 10 

types of plastics containing a carbon-carbon backbone remain recalcitrant to biocatalytic 11 

cleavage 15 . The focus on PET-degrading enzymes is driven by the global prevalence of PET, one 12 

of the most widely used types of plastic 16 . The structure of PET comprising repeating units of 13 

terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG) linked by ester bonds—gives it excellent 14 

properties for textile and packaging industries, such as high mechanical, thermal and chemical 15 

stability 17–19 . However, these same properties render PET a persistent pollutant that is 16 

challenging to degrade and recycle 20 . PET waste and pollution has been detected in various 17 

environmental compartments, including soils, sediments, groundwater, and marine ecosystems, 18 

in both macroplastic and microplastic forms 21 . Moreover, it has been shown to bioaccumulate 19 

within living organisms through the food chain, leading to harmful metabolic effects 21,22 .20 

Therefore, the discovery and engineering of PET-degrading enzymes are crucial for the 21

Benchmark engineered PETases, such as LCC-ICCG and Kubu-PM12, demonstrate industrial 3

viability, achieving >90% amorphous PET conversion at 70 °C 23,24 . The company Carbios has 4

advanced enzymatic recycling to near-commercial scale, with its delayed Longlaville plant (50 5

kt/year capacity) targeting production start by H1 2028 and a first licensing agreement for a plant 6

in China announced in 2026, underscoring the commercial momentum behind enzymatic PET 7

depolymerization 25,26 . These key advancements highlight the continuous maturation of enzyme 8

technology towards the more sustainable handling of plastic waste. Yet, further expansion of the 9

repertoire of the available PET-degrading enzymes diversity remains essential for understanding 10 

and optimizing performance across diverse waste streams, crystallinity levels, and process 11 

conditions. A key determinant of process performance is thermostability. Efficient enzymatic PET 12 

depolymerization typically requires sustained activity near the glass-transition temperature of 13 

amorphous PET (Tg ≈ 70 °C), where increased polymer chain mobility enhances enzyme access 14 

to ester bonds — making thermostability a critical criterion in both the discovery and engineering 15 

of new PETase candidates 27 .16 

Over the past decade, PETase discovery has evolved from targeted culture isolation to more 17 

sophisticated and high-throughput computational and metagenomic strategies. The archetypal 18 

PET-degrading enzyme IsPETase, isolated from Ideonella sakaiensis grown on PET bottles 28 , and 19 

the thermostable PETase LCC derived from leaf-branch compost metagenomes 29 established the 20 

field, yet their performance limitations spurred diverse discovery paradigms. Recent advances 21 

include sequence clustering of 1,894 candidates yielding industrial enzymes like Kubu-PM12 24 ,22

While these approaches have greatly expanded the PETase sequence space, they have primarily 5

drawn on general protein databases, environmental metagenomes, or reconstructed ancestors, 6

without yet fully leveraging (meta)genomic datasets from environments exposed to plastics. 7

Anthropogenic plastic debris represents a persistent and chemically homogeneous carbon source 8

in natural environments. Microbial communities colonizing plastic surfaces therefore experience 9

sustained exposure to polyester polymers and associated additives. Given the relatively recent 10 

and widespread introduction of plastic materials into the environment, we reasoned that 11 

prolonged exposure to synthetic polyesters could impose a selective pressure favoring 12 

microorganisms that harbor enzymes capable of acting on these substrates 35,36 . We therefore 13 

hypothesized that plastic-associated metagenomes represent a promising but underexplored 14 

reservoir for polyester-active hydrolases, and that targeted mining of these datasets might 15 

recover PETase candidates with features distinct from those identified in general sequence 16 

databases or non-plastic environments. 17 

To address this gap, in the present work, we have exclusively targeted plastic-associated 18 

metagenomic datasets and have analyzed them bioinformatically using ProteoSeeker 37 , our open 19 

source metagenomic protein discovery platform. Through a multi-step screening strategy— 20 

incorporating PETase-specific sequence motifs, structural similarity to benchmark PETases, 21 

molecular docking, and phylogenetic profiling—we identified 21 high-confidence candidates. 22

Experimental validation revealed two novel PET-hydrolyzing enzymes, including PET -KR1, a 1

thermostable PETase ( Tm = 66.5 °C). PET -KR1 additionally combines a Type I catalytic motif with 2

Type II-like structural features, illustrating the architectural diversity recoverable through this 3

approach. Biochemical characterization, molecular simulations, and rational engineering were 4

subsequently employed to investigate its catalytic properties and potential as an engineering 5

scaffold. Beyond the discovery of PET -KR1, our findings establish plastic-associated 6

metagenomes as a tractable and still largely untapped reservoir for PET-degrading enzymes and 7

provide a reproducible workflow for mining them. 8

Bioinformatic Screening 15 

Metagenomic datasets associated with plastic environments were retrieved from the European 16 

Nucleotide Archive (ENA) 38 using the following query in advanced search for Raw reads as Data 17 

type: (sample_description="plastic" OR sample_title="plastic") AND library_strategy="WGS" 18 

AND library_source="METAGENOMIC". The ENA search returned 334 RUN accession codes 19 

(search performed in November 2024), which were subsequently curated to exclude entries with 20

Raw next-generation sequencing reads were analyzed using ProteoSeeker (v1.0) 37 in both seek 3

and taxonomy modes. Seek mode performed targeted protein domain screening to identify 4

sequences matching user-specified Pfam 39 families from assembled contigs, while taxonomy 5

mode generated comprehensive taxonomic classification across all predicted proteins. 6

ProteoSeeker performs automated quality control, contig assembly via MEGAHIT 40 , gene 7

prediction using FragGeneScanRs 41 , and protein family screening via HMMER 42 against user-8

specified protein domains. In November 2024, screening targeted four Pfam accessions 9

corresponding to protein families enriched in characterized PETases: Cutinase (PF01083.26), 10 

Chlorophyllase (PF07224.15), Chlorophyllase2 (PF12740.11), and Dienelactone hydrolase family 11 

(PF01738.22), reflecting the absence of dedicated PETase domains at that time. These proxy 12 

families were selected based on their representation in biochemically characterized PET 13 

hydrolases (IsPETase, LCC, PHL7) 43 .14 

This analysis identified >77 million putative proteins across all 277 metagenomes. Pooled seek 15 

(Pfam-targeted) and taxonomy (domain-agnostic) outputs were filtered to identify putative PET-16 

degrading enzymes using the following criteria: (i) presence of conserved sequence motifs (Gly-17 

His-Ser-Met-Gly-Gly-Gly-Gly and Gly-Trp-Ser-Met-Gly-Gly-Gly-Gly), historically associated with 18 

Type I-like and Type II-like PETases 13 , respectively, and (ii) length between 250–350 amino acids, 19 

consistent with the size range of characterized PETases. These filters yielded 54 candidates, from 20 

which N-terminal signal peptides were predicted and trimmed using Phobius 44 .21

Candidate sequences were further evaluated for the presence of a C-terminal disulfide bond, a 1

common structural feature of characterized PETases, known to contribute to enzyme stability 2

and catalytic efficiency. Specifically, sequences were first screened for the presence of at least 3

two cysteine residues in the C-terminal tail region. Selected candidates were then aligned against 4

a reference set of known PET hydrolases (IsPETase, LCC, and TfCut2) using MAFFT Multiple 5

sequence alignment (MSA) 45 to verify cysteine positioning and validate putative disulfide bond 6

topology. Additionally, the presence of Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad and other features historically 7

associated with Type II-like PETases, such as extended loops and a putative secondary disulfide, 8

were evaluated. This stringent filtering resulted in 30 putative PETases, which were then 9

clustered using CD-HIT (v4.8.1) 46 with a sequence identity threshold of 100%. This approach 10 

removed exact sequence duplicates, yielding a final set of 21 unique putative PETase sequences. 11 

As a final structural validation, predicted structural models of all 21 unique sequences were 12 

generated using ColabFold (AlphaFold 2 with MMseqs2) 47,48 and superimposed with the crystal 13 

structure of LCC (PDB: 4EB0) 49 via TM-Align 50 . All candidates exhibited TM-scores ≥ 0.9 (mean 14 

0.95 ± 0.01), demonstrating highly conserved α/β -hydrolase folds consistent with PETase 15 

architecture. 16

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted by utilizing MAFFT MSA for the 21 putative PETases and 13 5

hydrolases (DLH variants) serving as stable outgroups. Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic analysis 9

was performed in IQ-TREE 3.0.1 52 (-m TEST -bb 1000 -nt AUTO), with ModelFinder 53 running the 10 

optimal LG+G4 substitution model and 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates for branch support. 11 

Trees were visualized in iTOL v6 54 , revealing PETase-linked clades rooted against stable 12 

outgroups, with candidates distributed across established Type I/II radiations and putative 13 

intermediate lineages. 14 

To identify key functional PETase motifs at the sequence level, MSA was performed on the 21 15 

putative PETases plus 15 reference PETases (IsPETase, LCC, Mipa-P, Kubu-P, PHL7, etc.) using 16 

MAFFT. The resulting alignment was visualized using the ESPript 3.0 server 55 to annotate critical 17 

structural and functional characteristics, including the conserved catalytic triad (Ser-His-Asp) 18 

essential for ester hydrolysis, distinctive Type I/II motifs (GHSMGGGG/GWSMGGGG) that define 19 

PETase structural subclasses, C-terminal disulfide cysteines important for stability, and 20 

characteristic extended loops. 21

Recombinant Expression and Purification of PETase Candidates 1

Genes encoding PETcand1 (PET -KR1), PETcand9, PETcand15, and PETcand16 were codon-2

optimized for Escherichia coli expression and synthesized commercially in the pET-29b(+) vector 3

(Twist Bioscience), generating pET-PETcand(1-16) constructs. The inserts comprised the signal-4

peptide-free (mature) enzyme sequences cloned between the Nde I and Xho I restriction sites, 5

resulting in proteins bearing a C-terminal 6XHis tag for affinity purification. 6

E. coli SHuffle® T7 Express chemically competent cells (New England Biolabs) were transformed 7

with the recombinant pET-PET candX plasmids to enable cytosolic disulfide bond formation and 8

recombinant protein production. Cultures were grown in LB medium supplemented with 9

kanamycin (50 μg mL⁻¹) at 37 °C and 220 rpm to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6 –0.8. At this stage protein 10 

production was induced by addition of 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), 11 

followed by incubation at 18 °C for 16–20 h with shaking at 220 rpm. 12 

Cells were harvested by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in lysis buffer 13 

(25 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.3). Cells were lysed by sonication on ice 14 

using a SONICS Vibra-Cell ultrasonic processor at 100% amplitude for 20 cycles (20 s on/20 s off). 15 

The resulting lysates were clarified by centrifugation (17,000 × g, 45 min, 4 °C) and applied to an 16 

immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) column packed with Protino Ni-NTA Agarose 17 

(MACHEREY-NAGEL). The column was equilibrated with lysis buffer, washed with buffer 18 

containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole (pH 8.3), and bound proteins were 19 

eluted with 25 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole (pH 8.3). The PETase content (% 20 

w/w) in the purified protein fraction after IMAC purification was determined by SDS-PAGE using 21

PET depolymerization kinetics and product analysis 1

To evaluate the PET depolymerization profile of PET -KR1, reactions were performed at 40, 45, 2

50, 55 and 60 °C for up to 168 h , with sampling at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 168 h .3

Reactions (1 mL total volume) contained an aPET film coupon (8.5 mg) and were initiated by 4

adding 26 μL SEC -purified PET -KR1 to 974 μL of 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 8.3) in 1.5 5

mL microcentrifuge tubes (final enzyme concentration 500 nM), followed by incubation at the 6

indicated temperature with shaking at 300 rpm. At each time point, soluble aromatic products 7

were quantified by measuring absorbance at 242 nm (NanoDrop), and signals were baseline-8

corrected by subtraction of the corresponding time-zero reading. Absorbance values were 9

converted to TPA eq using a TPA standard curve, and specific activity ( μmol products h -1 mg enzyme -1 )10 

was calculated from the initial rates of the linear region of product formation when linearity was 11 

observed; for conditions that plateaued early, specific activities were not determined. Final 12 

specific productivity ( μmol products h -1 mg enzyme -1 ) was calculated at 168 h from the endpoint TPA eq 13 

values and compared across temperatures. 14 

PET film depolymerization products were quantified spectrophotometrically as TPAeq, providing 15 

a rapid method for comparative kinetic screening across multiple temperatures. UV-based 16 

quantification of soluble aromatic PET hydrolysis products has previously been shown to 17 

correlate well with HPLC-derived measurements for relative activity comparisons and kinetic 18 

profiling 56 . For the following cryo-milled PET experiments, product concentrations were 19 

determined by HPLC to enable individual quantification of TPA, MHET, and BHET and analysis of 20 

product distributions. 21

Targeted screening of 277 plastic-associated metagenomes yields 21 high-confidence PETase 2

candidates 3

Analysis of 277 metagenomes from environments exposed to plastics using ProteoSeeker 37 4

identified over 77 million predicted proteins (Figure 1A). At the time of the analysis (November 5

2024), no dedicated PETase Pfam domain existed, necessitating use of custom Hidden Markov 6

Models (HMMs) and proxy domains—cutinase (PF01083), chlorophyllase (PF07224, PF12740), 7

and dienelactone hydrolase (PF01738)—consistent with contemporary strategies reported in 8

very recently published studies 30,32,33,70 . Consequently, seek mode (Pfam-targeted) and 9

taxonomy mode (domain-agnostic) outputs from ProteoSeeker were pooled without domain 10 

exclusion, yielding 54 candidates bearing canonical PETase Type I/II catalytic motifs (Gly-His-Ser-11 

Met-Gly-Gly-Gly-Gly or Gly-Trp-Ser-Met-Gly-Gly-Gly-Gly). All 54 candidates passed length 12 

filtering (250–350 amino acid residues). C-terminal disulfide analysis— requiring ≥2 cysteines at 13 

specific positions validated by MAFFT MSA with characterized PETases—identified 30 candidates. 14 

CD-HIT (100% identity) dereplication yielded 21 unique sequences. TM-Align superposition of 15 

AlphaFold models vs LCC (PDB:4EB0) confirmed all 21 exhibited TM-scores ≥0.9 (mean 16 

0.95±0.01), demonstrating highly conserved α/β -hydrolase folds regardless of Pfam status. 17

annotations. Satisfyingly, re-analysis of these 21 final candidates using current HMMER 19

Temperature and pH dependence of PET ‑KR1 activity 8

To understand better the functional window of PET -KR1, its esterase activity was quantified using 9

the proxy substrate pNPB across a broad temperature range (30– 90 °C) at pH 8.0. PET -KR1 10 

maintained high relative activity between 30 and 65 °C, reaching a maximum at 65 °C. Then, it 11 

exhibited a steep activity decline at ≥70 °C (Figure 4B), consistent with thermal inactivation as 12 

assay temperatures approach the protein’s unfolding transition. This trend is in agreement with 13 

the aforementioned DSF analysis , which had revealed a Tm of 66.5 °C (Figure 4A), where Tm14 

corresponds to the midpoint of the thermal unfolding transition (i.e., ~50% of the protein 15 

population unfolded under the assay conditions) 73 . As the folded fraction decreases and 16 

unfolded species accumulate, catalytic activity typically drops due to loss of the active-site 17 

geometry and, in many cases, irreversible denaturation pathways such as aggregation 74,75 .18 

Together, the close agreement between the temperature optimum (65 °C), the DSF-derived T m19 

(66.5 °C ), and the sharp activity loss above 70 °C supports thermostability as a key factor limiting 20 

PET -KR1 performance at elevated temperatures. 21

Engineering potential of PET -KR1 11 

Consistent with previous studies showing that metagenome-derived enzymes can provide robust 12 

starting scaffolds for protein engineering efforts 82 , the results obtained in this study indicate that 13 

thermostability of PET -KR1 is likely a major limiting factor during PET depolymerization. Although 14 

PET -KR1 exhibits a temperature optimum at 65 °C during pNPB hydrolysis, PET depolymerization 15 

experiments showed an apparent process optimum at lower temperatures (45–50 °C). This 16 

indicates that further stabilization could improve the overall performance under process relevant 17 

conditions. Therefore, rational disulfide bond engineering was investigated as a promising 18 

stabilizing strategy. 19

Conclusions 8

In this study, we developed a targeted metagenomic discovery pipeline and applied it to 277 9

publicly available plastic-associated metagenomes, shortlisting 21 high-confidence PETase 10 

candidates from more than 77 million predicted proteins through successive sequence- and 11 

structure-based filtering. Experimental validation identified two novel PET hydrolases, including 12 

PET -KR1, a thermostable enzyme ( Tm = 66.5 °C) that combines a Type I catalytic motif with Type 13 

II-like structural features. PET -KR1 depolymerized PET across a broad temperature range with 14 

markedly higher productivity on powdered than on film PET substrate, consistent with greater 15 

substrate accessibility. While less active overall at higher temperatures (60–65 °C), PET -KR1 16 

generated a product pool more strongly enriched in the terminal monomer TPA. Molecular 17 

simulations identified a conserved hydrophobic substrate-binding network around the catalytic 18 

serine, and rational engineering of the N206C/S260C disulfide raised thermostability by 3.5 °C, 19 

confirming that PET -KR1 is amenable to further optimization. 20

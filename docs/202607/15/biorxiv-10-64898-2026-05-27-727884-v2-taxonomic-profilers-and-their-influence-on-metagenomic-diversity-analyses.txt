ABSTRACT 19 

Estimating taxonomic profiles is a central task in microbiome research. Several bioinformatic 20 tools have been developed for this purpose, differing in algorithmic strategy, reference database 21 flexibility, sensitivity parameters, and the type of abundance they estimate. As a result, 22 taxonomic profiles carry an unwanted methodological signal whose driving characteristics 23 remains understudied. While benchmarks have evaluated the performance of some of these 24 tools, they rely on simulated data; little work has been done to compare them using real 25 metagenomes in the presence of noise and uncharacterised diversity. Overall, the impact of 26 taxonomic profiler choice and parameterisation on scientific conclusions remains poorly 27 understood. 28 First, we provide a much-needed characterisation of four taxonomic profilers to help 29 researchers better understand the available bioinformatic tools and inform their methodological 30 choices. Then, we leverage 1,211 shotgun metagenomes from eight datasets to compare these 31 taxonomic profilers across 13 methodological designs. Based on diversity indices, we found 32 substantial variability in estimated taxonomic composition depending on methodological 33 features such as reference database and algorithmic strategy. Alpha diversity analysis was 34 substantially sensitive totool choice (particularly among k-mer-based tools) and reference 35 database. Beta diversity showed sensitivity to both database and parameter choices, yet this 36 variability barely affected statistical inference. 37 Our findings highlight the sensitivity of taxonomic diversity analyses to taxonomic profiling 38 methodology and the importance for researchers to consider assessing the robustness of their 39 results to choice of tool, parameter, and reference database. Crucially, differences in sample 40 diversity across methodologies are symptomatic of differences in estimated taxonomic 41 composition, which can affect any analysis based on taxonomic abundances. AUTHOR SUMMARY 47 

Microbiome research relies on bioinformatic tools to determine which microbes are present in 48 a sample and estimate their relative abundances, a process known as taxonomic profiling .49 Because this task requires comparing tens of millions of DNA sequences against thousands of 50 microbial genomes, a wide range of computational strategies have been developed to make 51 profiling accurate and efficient. As a result, many taxonomic profilers are now available, yet 52 do not provide the same results. Although previous studies have evaluated their precision and 53 sensitivity, less attention has been given to how the choice of profiler may influence the 54 scientific conclusions drawn from microbiome data. Here, we selected four widely used 55 taxonomic profilers to examine whether researchers would reach the same biological 56 conclusions when comparing microbiomes across groups of samples, such as healthy and 57 diseased individuals. We show that different profilers can lead to different biological 58 conclusions and identify key tool characteristics that contribute to these discrepancies. 59 Moreover, we provide a comparative overview of these four profilers, offering practical 60 guidance for researchers seeking to choose the most appropriate tool for their study. These 61 findings highlight the importance of software choice in microbiome research and support more 62 transparent and reproducible data analysis practices. 63

INTRODUCTION 64 

The last decade saw the rise of metagenomics as a central approach in microbiome research. 65 As sequencing costs decreased, the accessibility of metagenomics increased, with the total 66 number of samples deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive nearly quadrupling between 67 2020 and 2023 [1]. To process this growing, massive body of data, bioinformatic tools are 68 being developed, published and updated regularly. As a result, researchers must make 69 methodological choices at nearly every step of data processing, with the potential of affecting 70 measurements and scientific conclusions. Here, we focus on algorithms designed to estimate 71 microbiome community composition from shotgun metagenomes, often referred to as 72 

taxonomic profilers . Our aim is to help researchers understand the differences between popular 73 tools and evaluate how bioinformatic parameters can affect the estimation of taxonomic 74 composition diversity-based analyses. 75 Recent efforts from the field of metagenomics have provided recommendations for data 76 processing [2–5] and classification [6–11], machine learning [12,13], statistical analyses [14– 77 17], and general best practices [14,18–22]. Guidance based on systematic benchmarking have 78 also emerged to help researchers choose or refine their bioinformatic methodology [23–26]. 79 The Critical Assessment of Metagenome Interpretation (CAMI), a large scale effort to create a 80 publicly available benchmarking framework for metagenomics, has standardised the evaluation 81 of bioinformatic tools using simulated datasets [24,25]. CAMI, as well as other benchmarking 82 studies [10,27,28], offers critical insights into accuracy and performance and highlight the 83 context-dependency of what constitutes a “better tool”, underscoring that there is no one-size-84 fits-all methodology in metagenomics [29]. Overall, what constitutes the “right” tool depends 85 on multiple factors such as a sample’s origin, complexity, phylogenetic diversity, and 86 sequencing depth. Although many tools perform well in estimating taxonomic profiles, 87 choosing the right one and using it adequately remains a challenge for researchers. 88

5Benchmarking metagenome taxonomic profilers requires a known ground truth, often taking 89 the form of simulated metagenomes. With over 95% of prokaryotic species being genomically 90 uncharacterised[30], simulated metagenomes are not likely to well represent real microbiomes, 91 especially those of understudied environments or hosts. Moreover, benchmarking tools against 92 one another implicitly assumes that any two “good enough” tools should produce converging 93 outputs. Yet for taxonomic profilers, it is not well established whether all tools should yield 94 the same result, and therefore whether they are strictly comparable. Importantly, every method 95 has its own biases and limitations, and some authors have argued against methodological 96 standardisation [29,31], favouring instead more transparent reporting [32]. To better select and 97 justify taxonomic profiling methodology, researchers thus need a clear understanding of what 98 is estimated, the methodological limits, and the extent to which these tools can be used 99 interchangeably. In this paper, we qualitatively compare the characteristics of four taxonomic 100 profiling tools. Then, we quantitatively assess how user choices influence sample diversity 101 estimates and statistical comparisons using real metagenomes. 102 

TAXONOMIC PROFILING TOOLS 103 

Several taxonomic profilers for shotgun metagenomics have been published over the last 104 decade. These tools compare reads to a set of reference sequences, producing either taxonomic 105 or sequence relative abundances [33]. Tools that use a DNA-to-markers (D2M) approach align 106 metagenome reads to their own collection of genes and estimate relative taxonomic abundances 107 by normalising the number of aligned reads by gene length. Two popular tools based on that 108 approach are MetaPhlAn [34] (Metagenomic Phylogenetic Analysis) and mOTUs [35] (Marker 109 gene-based Operational Taxonomic Unit). Alternatively, Kraken2 [36] and Sourmash [37,38] 110 use a DNA-to-DNA (D2D) approach, meaning they rely on matching the k-mers of 111 metagenome reads to a collection of genomes [7], and thus estimate relative sequence 112 abundances. These are not normalised by genome length, as this information is unknown for 113

Therefore, 115 sequence abundances are biased by true genome length, as longer genomes will produce more 116 reads than shorter genomes [33]. Despite this, tools from both the D2M and D2D approaches 117 are widely used in microbiome research. 118 Importantly, it must be highlighted that distinct abundance types produce different taxonomic 119 profiles that are not equivalent and cannot be interconverted [39,40]. This recently exposed 120 nuance [33] between the D2M and D2D tools can lead to incorrect comparisons, as both 121 approaches cannot yield the same abundance profiles. However, the causes of this divergence 122 can go beyond the type of abundance estimated. These tools can also differ in their algorithmic 123 strategy, database flexibility, reference taxonomy, and strategy for detecting unknown species. 124

Unknown species detection strategy and database flexibility 131 

The D2M tools evaluated here can detect many species lacking a reference genome or 132 taxonomy. Both MetaPhlAn and mOTUs include in their reference database genes from 133 uncharacterised species, extracted from public metagenomes. However, these tools employ 134 fundamentally different strategies to find unknown species. MetaPhlAn takes a “genome first” 135 approach, clustering over a million reference genomes and MAGs into known species-level 136 genome bins (kSGBs, containing at least one reference genome) and unknown SGBs (uSGB, 137 containing only MAGs). These SGBs are then used to identify over 5 million clade-specific 138 marker genes to create a reference database. MetaPhlAn aligns metagenomic reads to these 139 genes to determine sample taxonomic composition. Conversely, mOTUs takes a “marker first” 140 approach, mining publicly available genomes and metagenomes for 10 universal phylogenetic 141 marker genes. Markers found in reference genomes inherit their taxonomy directly and form 142 ref-mOTUs, while markers found in metagenome assemblies and MAGs are clustered into 143 species-level units (meta-mOTUs and ext-mOTUs, respectively) based on their similarity. 144 These new units are then taxonomically labelled at higher rank based on their similarity to the 145 closest known species, yielding a reference database of genes to which mOTUs aligns 146 metagenomic reads. 147

8In comparison, D2D tools such as Kraken and Sourmash do not have a built-in strategy for 148 unknown species detection. However, since their database can be any set of taxonomically 149 labelled genomes (often RefSeq or GTDB databases), users can assemble MAGs from the 150 metagenomes whose composition they wish to estimate and build a custom reference database 151 (or complement an existing one). This feature is especially useful for studying microbial 152 communities whose members are poorly represented in public databases. Sourmash can append 153 genomes to an existing index, whereas Kraken requires the entire database index to be rebuild, 154 a more resource-hungry process. Of note, as of version 4, mOTUs [41] can now find marker 155 genes in user-provided genomes, to add them to the reference database using an extension 156 program, though this feature is not evaluated here. 157 

Algorithmic approaches to k-mer matching 158 

The D2D tools evaluated here differ mainly in terms of algorithmic strategy. Both Kraken and 159 Sourmash rely on matching k-mers (each “word” of length k in a read) exactly between reads 160 and reference genomes. Essentially, reads are assigned to sequences with which they share the 161 most k-mers; however, each tool uses different heuristics to accomplish this task. 162 

Briefly, Kraken2 classifies a read using the genome that has the highest number of exact k-mer 163 matches with that read. When a read’s k-mers matches two genomes equally well, Kraken2 164 assigns it the lowest taxonomic rank [42] these genomes have in common. Abundances are 165 based on the number of k-mers per taxonomic rank and are usually re-estimated using Bracken, 166 a tool that redistributes reads from higher taxonomic ranks down to the desired level (e.g., 167 Species) based on Bayes’ theorem. It is generally recommended to use Bracken when 168 estimating taxonomic compositions with Kraken, because it helps increase the taxonomic 169 classification rate at the species level. Because it classifies each read independently, Kraken2 170 defines the confidence required for a read to be labelled (the --confidence parameter). 171

9Importantly, even if a reference genome is only matched by one read from a metagenome, that 172 taxon will be reported as part of its composition (though at extremely low abundance). 173 Sourmash takes a different approach: it finds the smallest set of reference genomes that contain 174 all the identifiable k-mers of a metagenome [38]. To do this, it finds the genome covering the 175 largest amount of k-mers, removes these k-mers from the query (the metagenome), then repeats 176 this process iteratively. Sourmash estimates abundances based on the number of k-mers 177 uniquely matched to all detected genomes and, contrary to Kraken, will not report a taxon 178 matched by very few reads; its --threshold_bp parameter controls the minimum size for a k-179 mer set to be retained. Currently, there is no consensus on which parameter values are optimal 180 across analytical contexts when comparing Kraken and Sourmash, although great efforts were 181 made recently to benchmark Kraken’s --confidence parameter [39]. In both cases, default 182 settings may not be optimal and their modification requires careful consideration of the trade-183 offs between sensitivity and specificity [27], an assessment delegated to the user. 184 

Reference database taxonomy 185 

Two taxonomies dominate prokaryotic classification in metagenomics: NCBI and GTDB. 186 Whereas NCBI taxonomy integrates historical nomenclature alongside phylogenetic data, 187 GTDB is exclusively based on phylogenomic relationships and is designed to ensure 188 monophyly across clades. However, GTDB does not always follow the The International Code 189 

of Nomenclature for Prokaryotes [43], which can be problematic in clinical or epidemiological 190 contexts, depending on research objectives. Consequently, a metagenome’s observed diversity 191 can easily be influenced by the taxonomy of the reference database. For example, if Shigella 192 

taxa are present in the sample, the relative abundance of E. coli will appear higher with GTDB 193 than with NCBI as GTDB considers Shigella to be E. coli strains. 194

10 As they simply rely on a set of reference genomes, D2D tools allow the user to choose the 195 reference taxonomy by selecting a RefSeq database (NCBI taxonomy) or a GTDB database. 196 D2M tools, on the other hand, are restricted to databases specifically built by their authors, 197 which thus constrains the taxonomy. It is therefore likely that genus or species diversity 198 estimations will vary depending on which tool is used, and hypotheses based on diversity 199 indices may reveal different conclusions accordingly. Of note, mOTUs provides a mapping file 200 between NCBI and GTDB taxonomies, allowing the user to convert the output. 201 Overall, these differences in tool characteristics and algorithmic flexibility highlight the need 202 to assess the extent to which taxonomic profilers diverge in their estimation of a metagenome’s 203 taxonomic composition and identify which methodological features, beyond abundance type, 204 contribute to these divergences. This knowledge will help guide methodological choices 205 through a clearer understanding of the influence of tool characteristics on downstream analyses. 206 In this work, we first evaluate the sensitivity of intra-sample (alpha) and inter-sample (beta) 207 diversity indices across four taxonomic profilers and methodological choices using real 208 metagenomes. We then illustrate how such variability can influence the outcomes of hypothesis 209 testing, before discussing which tool characteristics likely drive this sensitivity and proposing 210 best practices to improve comparability, transparency and robustness in metagenomic research. 211

Alpha diversity 242 

Four alpha diversity metrics were calculated: richness [53], Shannon entropy [54], and the 243 inverse of the Simpson index [55]. All three are widely used owing to their early adoption in 244 microbial ecology studies. In addition, the Tail statistic [56], developed specifically for 245 microbiome research was also used. Richness represents the number of detected species, which 246 can be highly sensitive to sequencing depth and taxonomic profiling tool parameters. In the 247 context of microbial compositions, the Shannon index quantifies the uncertainty in predicting 248 the taxon identity of a randomly drawn sequence, with higher values reflecting greater diversity. 249 The Simpson index is the probability that two randomly drawn sequences belong to the same 250 taxa, with lower values indicating greater diversity. For ease of interpretation, we use the 251 inverse Simpson index, representing the effective number of equally abundant taxa that would 252 produce the same diversity. Finally, the Tail statistic is the weighted standard deviation of taxon 253 ranks, with the distance of each rank measured to the most abundant taxon and weighted by its 254 relative abundance. This statistic was developed to better reflect low-abundance taxa in a 255 diversity index. 256 MetaPhlAn and mOTUs cannot interchange their reference database. Thus, diversity estimates 257 can be affected simply by the number of available taxa in each database, and the impact of tool 258 choice cannot be de-confounded from the impact of database choice within the D2M approach. 259 Kraken2 and Sourmash, on the other hand, can use the same reference database. Even then, 260 diversity estimates will be influenced by Kraken2's --confidence threshold or Sourmash's --261 

threshold-bp , which have no single “optimal” value and directly affect the number of reported 262 species. Therefore, to account for the large differences in representative species across 263

Effect sizes are 277 expressed as rank-biserial correlations [62]. 278 

Beta diversity 279 

Beta diversity is used to evaluate whether microbiota compositions differ more between groups 280 of samples than within them. To that end, distance or dissimilarity indices can be used, which 281 quantify the compositional similarity between every pair of samples. Here, we focus on the 282 Bray-Curtis index because, in addition to being widely used, it accounts for relative abundances, 283 which is something taxonomic profilers try to estimate. Alternative indices have also been 284 developed to account for phylogenetic distances between taxa[63] or data compositionality 285 [64,65], but their comparison is beyond the scope of this paper. 286

15 For each methodology-dataset combination, a between-sample Bray-Curtis dissimilarity 287 matrix was computed. Ordinations were performed on each of these matrices using Principal 288 coordinates analysis (PCoA, sometimes also referred to as metric multidimensional scaling 289 [66]) using the cmdscale function from the stats packages in R. Separately for each dataset, 290 Procrustes correlations [67] were computed between the ordinations of each pair of 291 methodologies using the protest function of the vegan package [68] (v2.7-3). For each of these 292 methodology pairs, we report the median correlation ± MAD. Methodologies were clustered 293 hierarchically based on the median correlation, using the hclust function from the stats package 294 [69] (v4.5.2). Finally, the eigenvectors of each Procrustes matrix were extracted to visually 295 assess the clustering of methodologies for each dataset. 296 To assess the impact of methodology on scientific conclusions based on beta diversity 297 comparison, we performed a permutational analysis of variance [70] (PERMANOVA) using 298 the adonis2 function of the vegan package on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities for each dataset-299 methodology combination using the model Dissimilarity Matrix ~ Group . As for the Mann-300 Whitney test, P-values were not adjusted and are shown alongside the proportion of variance 301 explained, quantified by a coefficient of determination (R 2), which is the ratio of the between 302 groups and total sum of squares. 303

DISCUSSION 400 

In this work, we address a literature gap by describing four taxonomic profilers for shotgun 401 metagenomics and examining how their characteristics can co-vary with diversity assessment 402 of microbiomes. Applying these tools to 1,221 metagenomes spanning eight datasets, we show 403 how methodological choices affect alpha and beta diversity measurements and their 404 downstream statistical outcomes. We show that study conclusions can shift depending on 405 which taxonomic profiler is chosen and how it is applied, particularly for alpha diversity. 406 Unlike benchmark studies relying on simulated metagenomes, this work focusses on real 407 metagenomes, reflecting the full complexity of microbiomes rather than the current, incomplete 408 knowledge of them. By doing so, we complement the valuable benchmarking question “which 409 tool is the most performant?” with the question “how do methodological choices affect what 410 we conclude?”, providing a relatable angle to researchers hoping to apply these tools to real 411 data. While metagenomics continues to grow in popularity and accessibility, published 412 resources guiding researchers through the analytical landscape are increasingly needed, as is 413 awareness of the importance of methodological design and a better understanding of 414 bioinformatic tools. We hope this work equips researchers with the means to avoid inadequate 415 analyses, cherry-picking, poor reproducibility, and misleading conclusions. 416 When measuring alpha diversity, regardless of the reference database used, the choice of 417 taxonomic profiler introduces substantial variations in diversity estimation. When controlling 418 for reference database size and sensitivity parameter trade-offs, no tool consistently over- or 419 under-estimated diversity relative to others. Shannon and Inverse Simpson indices were more 420 sensitive to the choice of taxonomic profiler than to the choice of reference database, 421 particularly when comparing Kraken and Sourmash. In contrast, richness and Tail indices were 422 especially tool-sensitive when RefSeq was used as a reference. This suggests that alpha 423 diversity co-varies with the algorithmic strategy (choice of tool) and, when species count 424

25 carries relatively more weight (i.e., with richness and Tail), with the breadth of the reference 425 database (or, indirectly, sample identifiability). Importantly, reference databases cannot be 426 interchanged between D2M tools, preventing us from de-confounding their influence. It is 427 therefore possible that the strategy used for including unknown species (i.e., meta-OTUs for 428 mOTUs vs. SGBs for MetaPhlAn) is driving some of the observed variability. Interestingly, 429 tool sensitivity in D2M taxonomic profilers was significantly lower, despite the mOTUs4 430 reference database containing over two times as many species as MetaPhlAn’s 2025 database 431 version ( Table 3 ). 432 The alpha diversity indices themselves varied substantially between methodologies. The 433 outcomes of hypothesis testing based on these indices varied too, though much less with beta 434 diversity. More surprisingly, we found that even tools designed to estimate the same type of 435 relative abundance, using the same reference database, can produce diversity estimates 436 diverging sufficiently to lead to different statistical conclusions. However, variability in 437 measured diversity could reflect dataset characteristics such as biome of origin, phylogenetic 438 diversity, or even sequencing depth, though quantifying it would require a larger number of 439 independent datasets. We identify this as potential for future work. These patterns of sensitivity 440 to methodological choices underscore the need for exercising caution when comparing alpha 441 diversity across studies that use different methodologies. Without transparent reporting of 442 methodology parameters, comparability of scientific conclusions based on the analysis of alpha 443 diversity is limited. 444 Beta diversity told a different story: although the measured indices themselves were sensitive 445 to methodological choices ( Figure 3 ), this sensitivity rarely affected statistical outcomes with 446 the datasets used here ( Figure 5 ). For D2M methodologies, pairwise sample dissimilarities 447 were nearly insensitive to the choice of tool or database version, based on Procrustes analyses. 448

Despite these approach-specific 451 observations, PERMANOVA significance and effect size remained stable across almost all 452 methodologies ( Figure 5 ). However, this apparent stability does not constitute evidence that 453 PERMANOVA outcomes are robust to methodology; only that observable changes in 454 ordination space do not necessarily result in methodology-dependent conclusions. Moreover, 455 the Kraken-Sourmash comparison suggests that more comprehensive databases, such as GTDB, 456 may improve the resolution of beta diversity analyses by capturing a broader range of 457 distinguishing features across microbial communities. However, the impact of database 458 completeness warrants more thorough investigation, particularly using unpublished 459 metagenomic datasets that reference databases will not have been trained on. 460 

CONCLUSION 461 

Taken together, these results underscore the substantial influence of methodological choices 462 on the assessment of microbial taxonomic community composition and diversity with short-463 read metagenomes. Consistent with previous findings [27,39,71], our results highlight that the 464 reference database is a major driver of this influence. Larger reference databases may reduce 465 sensitivity to the choice tool when the D2D approach is chosen, a promising trend given the 466 rapid expansion of available genomic resources [72]. Furthermore, novel tools such as Sylph 467 [73], which estimates both taxonomic and sequence abundances from a single, modifiable 468 database, may reveal additional sources of methodological variability worth characterising in 469 future work. 470 The increasing diversity of bioinformatic tools and the growing accessibility of running 471 multiple in silico experiments on the same samples raises the risk of inadvertent [74] or 472

It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 14, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.05.27.727884 doi: bioRxiv preprint

27 deliberate cherry-picking [75]. In a field where outcomes are demonstrably sensitive to 473 methodological choices, transparent reporting and justification of methodological decisions are 474 essential for reproducibility and interpretability. In light of our results, we recommend that 475 researchers: (1) review the available tools and current benchmarks to choose appropriately 476 given their research objectives; (2) state and justify the choice of tool(s), parameters, and 477 reference database(s) in their methods section; (3) assess the robustness of their conclusions to 478 choice of reference database; and (4) exercise caution when interpreting and comparing 479 diversity across studies, knowing that the observed signals may be influenced by 480 methodological choices. 481 Future work incorporating more datasets and a broader range of methodologies could study 482 how methodological parameters interact with dataset characteristics such as biome of origin, 483 taxonomic diversity, and sequencing depth, enabling more targeted recommendations. 484 Valuable research directions include evaluating the sensitivity of complementary diversity 485 indices (e.g., Faith [76] for alpha diversity; UniFrac [63] and Aitchison distances [64] for beta 486 diversity) to methodological variation, investigating the impact of methodology on differential 487 abundance analyses, and assessing how incorporating MAGs from a study's own metagenomes 488 into the reference database affects conclusions. Ultimately, we hope this work empowers 489 researchers to make more informed, transparent, and reproducible choices in metagenomic 490 analyses, ensuring that biological interpretations are driven by data, not by tools. 491

# Abstract 363 

Background: 364 

Plant microbiomes can contribute to host adaptation in extreme environments, particularly deserts where 365 

high temperatures, intense radiation, water limitation, and nutrient-poor soils constrain plant survival. 366 

Citrullus colocynthis is a desert-adapted cucurbit with medicinal and agricultural relevance, yet integrated 367 

understanding of its microbiome across seasons, tissues, habitats, and functional traits remains limited. 368 

Here, we asked whether spatiotemporal microbiome dynamics in C. colocynthis are linked to microbial 369 

functional potential and cultivable traits relevant to persistence under arid conditions. 370 

Results: 371 

To address this, we profiled the microbiome of C. colocynthis across two sites, two seasons, and multiple 372 

compartments using 16S rRNA amplicon screening, shotgun metagenomics, culture-based phenotyping, 373 

and genome analysis. Amplicon profiling provided an exploratory framework and showed that plant-374 

associated bacterial communities were shaped primarily by season and tissue type, with roots emerging as 375 

the most season-responsive compartment. Shotgun metagenomics confirmed stronger seasonal 376 

restructuring in root bacterial communities than in leaves and extended taxonomic profiling to fungal and 377 

archaeal fractions, which were interpreted descriptively because of low read representation. Summer root 378 

communities were enriched in actinobacterial genera, including Saccharopolyspora , Amycolatopsis ,379 

Pseudonocardia , and Nonomuraea , while functional profiling indicated coordinated shifts in central 380 

metabolism, cofactor salvage, exopolysaccharide-related pathways, and redox-associated functions. 381 

Twenty-four cultured bacterial isolates exhibited diverse stress-tolerance and plant growth-promoting 382 

traits, and whole-genome analyses identified biosynthetic, osmoprotective, oxidative-stress, and 383 

phytohormone-related gene content. Pangenome analysis of Pseudomonas orientalis revealed an open 384 

pangenome and lifestyle-associated accessory genes linked to host-associated functions. 385 

Conclusions: 386

# 1. Introduction 393 

Increasing heat, drought, and salinity are major constraints on global agriculture and make crop resilience 394 

a growing priority [1, 2] [3, 4]. In desert systems, plant persistence is not explained by plant physiology 395 

alone; microbial partners can contribute to tolerance under water limitation, high temperature, and poor 396 

nutrient availability [5-9]. However, the extent to which these associations vary across plant habitats and 397 

seasons remains somewhat unresolved. Several studies show that plant-associated microbial communities 398 

change between wet and dry periods, with leaves and roots often responding more strongly than 399 

surrounding soils [10-14]. These temporal and spatial patterns are important for linking community 400 

change to possible functions. 401 

402 

Citrullus colocynthis provides a useful system for studying these processes under natural desert 403 

conditions. This cucurbit has both medicinal and agricultural relevance [15-19]and shows physiological 404 

adaptation to extreme temperatures [20]. Previous studies have reported disease-suppressive and growth-405 

promoting endophytes and rhizobacteria [21], antibacterial endophytic actinobacteria [22], tissue- and 406 

site-dependent fungal endophytes [23], and limited fungal diversity in wider desert surveys that included 407 

this species [24]. Our earlier bacterial microbiome study also showed diverse plant-associated bacteria, 408 

many with known plant growth-promoting potential [4], and later culture-based work showed that 409

rhizosphere isolates can carry multiple arid-adaptation traits [25]. These findings support examining the 410 

C. colocynthis holobiont in a way that connects community dynamics with functional traits. 411 

412 

The holobiont concept treats the plant and its associated microbes as an interacting system shaped by both 413 

host and microbial processes [26, 27]. Within this view, plant-associated and free-living microbes may 414 

differ in traits relevant to host interaction. Plant-associated Pseudomonas , for example, often contain 415 

genes linked to rhizosphere competence, including antibiotic and siderophore pathways, as well as 416 

functions involved in induced systemic resistance. Many beneficial bacteria also produce indole-3-acetic 417 

acid (IAA), commonly via tryptophan-dependent pathways, which affect plant development and defense 418 

[28-31]. These observations provide a basis for asking whether seasonal community changes, habitat 419 

specificity in leaves, roots, and soils, and microbial functions associated with stress tolerance are 420 

connected. 421 

422 

Here, we examined whether spatiotemporal microbiome dynamics and microbial traits reflect the 423 

ecological demands of a desert plant. We profiled C. colocynthis tissues and surrounding soils from two 424 

UAE emirates during rainy and dry seasons using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and shotgun 425 

metagenomics. We also isolated endophytic strains from leaves and roots, sequenced their genomes, and 426 

evaluated stress tolerance and plant growth-promoting traits, including in vitro phytohormone production 427 

and antimicrobial activity. By combining this data with previous work, we aim to clarify how the C. 428 

colocynthis holobiont may contribute to resilience in arid environments. 429

# 2. Methods 431 

## 2.1. Sample collection 432 

Leaf, root, root zone, and bulk soil samples were collected from three C. colocynthis plants in natural 433 

populations in Al Ain city (24°27'50.8" N 55°38'37.1" E) and in the Ras Al Khaimah (RAK) emirate 434 

(25°33'11.7" N 55°53'18.9" E), UAE, as described previously [4]. The study included three biological 435 

replicates, each with three technical replicates per sample type and season. The locations were selected 436 

for their distinct rainfall patterns and geography. Endophytic bacteria were isolated from freshly collected 437 

leaf and root samples from the Al Ain site in January 2022, and samples collected in March and August 438 

2022 were used for microbiome analysis. 439 

## 2.2. Amplicon Profiling and Metagenomic Analyses 440 

2.2.1. DNA extraction 441 

Root and leaf samples were surface sterilized (1% bleach, 3 min; two sterile water washes; 70% ethanol, 442 

3 min; six sterile water washes) [3]. The final wash water was plated on nutrient agar (NA, HiMedia, 443 

Mumbai, India) and potato dextrose agar (PDA, Conda Lab, Madrid, Spain) to assess sterility. DNA was 444 

extracted using the XpressDNA Plant Kit (MagGenome, India) following the manufacturer's protocol. 445 

However, root samples were incubated at 56 °C for an additional 30 minutes during the lysis step. DNA 446 

was extracted from 2g of soil using a combination of the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro (Qiagen, Hilden, 447 

Germany) and XpressDNA Soil Kit (MagGenome). These extractions were repeated and pooled within 448 

the same biological replicate (i.e., technical replicates were pooled) until sufficient DNA was obtained for 449 

the respective sequencing strategies. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were confirmed as 450 

previously described [4]. 451

2.2.2. 16S rRNA Amplicon-based Bacterial Profiling 453 

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing was used to assess DNA suitability for downstream metagenomics and 454 

to provide preliminary insights into seasonal and geographic variation in bacterial community structure. 455 

DNA samples were amplified, trimmed, and quality assessed as described previously [4]. The generated 456 

ASV files were used for analyses with MicrobiomeAnalyst2 [32]and the updated Greengenes database 457 

[33]. Alpha diversity was assessed using Shannon [34] and Chao1 [35] indices, while NMDS was used to 458 

examine beta diversity, which guided subsequent metagenomic analysis. 459 

460 

2.2.3. Metagenome-based Microbial Profiling 461 

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq PE150 (150 bp paired-end) 462 

platform, generating approximately 20 GB of raw data per sample. The quality of the PE reads was 463 

assessed with FastQC v.0.11.9 [36]. Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using 464 

Trimmomatic v.0.39 [37]. The trimmed reads were taxonomically profiled using Kraken 2 [38]. 465 

466 

2.2.4. Seasonal and Geographic Comparisons of Metagenome-derived Microbial Communities 467 

Kraken-based reports were combined with metadata to compare leaf and root microbial communities 468 

across seasons and locations. Analyses were restricted to leaf and root profiles, with viral assignments and 469 

other sample classes excluded. Bacterial, fungal, and archaeal assignments were retained, but low 470 

representation of fungal and archaeal reads limited these groups to descriptive phylum- and order-level 471 

summaries. Kraken lineage-style reports were parsed to retain terminal assignments, collapse profiles to 472 

comparable taxonomic levels, and generate relative-abundance tables for downstream analyses. 473 

For bacterial genus-level comparisons, alpha diversity was summarized using observed genera, Shannon 474 

diversity, and Pielou evenness [39]. Compositional turnover was assessed using Bray-Curtis 475 

dissimilarities [40], metric multidimensional scaling, and PERMANOVA [41] within each location-tissue 476 

subgroup. Seasonal changes in alpha diversity and genus-level relative abundance were tested using exact 477

2.2.5. Functional Profiling using Root Samples 481 

Functional profiling was restricted to root samples because this compartment exhibited the strongest 482 

seasonal restructuring and serves as the main interface for plant–microbe interactions under abiotic stress. 483 

Analyses used HUMAnN gene-family, pathway-abundance, pathway-coverage, and merged relative-484 

abundance outputs [42, 43]. The merged pathway-abundance matrix was used for downstream analyses. 485 

Unstratified pathway outputs were used for community-level ordination and seasonal comparisons, while 486 

stratified outputs identified contributing taxa. Pathway names with provenance tags, including plant, 487 

yeast, and archaeal labels, were retained when linked to MetaCyc reactions; these labels indicate pathway 488 

provenance rather than quantitative profiling of those groups. Analyses focused on pathways related to 489 

exopolysaccharide production, biofilm formation, one-carbon metabolism, amino-acid biosynthesis, 490 

cofactor salvage, and redox buffering. 491

## 2.3. Bacterial Culture Identification and Genome Annotation 493 

2.3.1. Isolation 494 

Root and leaf samples were surface-sterilized as described above, homogenized in 10 mL sterile water, 495 

diluted 1:10, and stored at 4°C for 7 days before 6 serial dilutions. The final three dilutions were plated on 496 

half-strength NA and starch nitrate agar (SNA) supplemented with cycloheximide (100 mg/L), dried at 497 

room temperature, and incubated inverted at 28°C. Colonies were purified by repeated sub-culturing, 498 

grouped by morphotype, and maintained on antibiotic-free Luria-Bertani (LB) agar (HiMedia). 499

2.3.3. Whole-Genome Sequencing (WGS) 513 

The DNA used for initial identification was also used to prepare Illumina and ONT WGS libraries. Reads 514 

were trimmed and hybrid assemblies generated as described previously [45], using Unicycler v.0.4.8 [46], 515

ANI calculator [51]. ANI values ≥95–96% were considered consistent with the same species, whereas 519 

lower values indicated potential novel species. 520

## 2.4. Culture-based Biochemical Assays and Stress Tolerance 530 

2.4.1. Stress Tolerance 531 

Drought tolerance was tested in LB broth supplemented with 10-30% PEG8000 [58]. Cultures were 532 

incubated overnight at 30°C and 150 rpm, and OD600 readings were used to classify isolates as sensitive 533 

(≤0.29), moderately tolerant (0.3–0.39), or tolerant ( ≥0.4). Salinity tolerance was assessed on LB agar 534 

containing 2-12% NaCl (w/v) in 2% increments and incubated for 7 days. Heat tolerance was tested at 535 

22°C and 30–55°C in 5°C increments; plates with no growth after 7 days were transferred to 22°C to 536 

assess recovery. pH tolerance was tested in LB broth adjusted to pH 4-10 with 1 M HCl or NaOH, and the 537 

broth was incubated under the same conditions as the drought assay. 538 

539 

2.4.2. Qualitative Enzyme Activity Assays 540 

Qualitative screening used milk agar (HiMedia) for nitrogen dissolution [59], Pikovskaya’s agar 541 

(HiMedia) with 5% bromophenol blue for phosphate solubilization, M9 salts (HiMedia) with 1% (w/v) 542 

citrus pectin for pectinase, or 1% CMC for cellulase, starch agar (HiMedia) for amylase, Aleksandrow 543 

agar (HiMedia) for potassium solubilization, and nitrogen-free Dworkin and Foster’s salts minimal agar 544 

(DF; [60]) with 2 g (NH 4 )2 SO 4 or 3 mM ACC (Sigma-Aldrich, Maryland, USA) as sole nitrogen source. 545 

Plates were incubated at 35  for up to 7 days; pectinase, amylase, and cellulase activity were visualized 546 

with Gram's iodine or Congo red. All assays were done in triplicate. IAA production was assessed in LB 547 

broth containing 2 g/L L-tryptophan after incubation at 37  and 150 rpm for 24 hours, followed by 548 

Salkowski’s reagent addition (2:1) and dark incubation for 30 min [61]; a red color indicated IAA 549 

production. 550

## 2.5. Pangenome analysis of Pseudomonas orientalis 559 

Publicly available P. orientalis genomes were downloaded from NCBI in FASTA format and analyzed 560 

with the B20 genome. Genomes were annotated with Prokka v.5.30.2 [62], assessed with BUSCO, and 561 

high-quality GFF3 files were used for Roary pan-genome analysis [63] at ≥95% BLASTp identity. The 562 

resulting gene presence/absence matrix was visualized in R [64]. Pan-genome openness was modeled 563 

using K = κ⋅n −γ

[65], where γ > 1 indicates a closed pan-genome and 0 < γ ≤ 1 an open pan-genome. 564 

TWILIGHT [66] was used to identify lineage-specific accessory-gene adaptations in complete free-living 565 

and plant-associated genomes, including B20. Genes were classified as core, accessory, intermediate, or 566 

unique, and phylogenetic mapping was used to assess selection and ecological associations. Pan-genome 567 

fluidity and diversity were estimated with ANGSD [67] using P. aeruginosa mutation rates (10 ¹¹), and 568 

effective population size was calculated as θ = 2Ne μ. Lifestyle differences were tested using Wilcoxon 569 

tests in R (P < 0.05). 570 571

# 3. Results 572 

## 3.1. Seasonal dynamics of microbial communities reflect environmental changes 573 

3.1.1. Amplicon profiling provides an exploratory overview of bacterial community structure. 574 

Amplicon profiling provided an exploratory overview of the bacterial community structure prior to 575 

shotgun metagenomic analysis. Plant-associated communities were shaped primarily by season and tissue 576 

type, with little effect of sampling location. Richness decreased from winter to summer in both leaves and 577 

roots, while ordination showed clear seasonal separation across plant tissues. In contrast, root-zone soil 578 

communities remained comparatively stable across seasons, although root-zone and bulk soil samples 579 

differed compositionally. Together, these patterns identified leaf and root tissues, particularly roots, as the 580 

most responsive compartments for downstream metagenomic analysis. Full amplicon diversity, ordination 581 

and taxonomic summaries are provided in the Supplement (Suppl. Notes S2–3 and Figures S1–4). 582 

583 

3.1.2. Metagenomic profiling reveals root-associated seasonal restructuring. 584 

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and Kraken2 classification were used to characterize bacterial genus-585 

level profiles across leaf and root samples from both sites and seasons. Alpha diversity was summarized 586 

using observed genera, Shannon diversity, and Pielou evenness, with full seasonal comparison statistics 587 

provided in Supplementary Table S1. Leaf communities showed little seasonal change in diversity at 588 

either site and consistently had higher Shannon diversity and evenness than root communities. In contrast, 589 

root communities showed consistent summer declines in Shannon diversity and evenness at both sites, 590 

while observed genus richness remained comparatively stable (Figure 1A–C). 591

# 4. Discussion 790 

This study examined how the Citrullus colocynthis microbiome varies across seasons, tissues, and 791 

locations, and how these community patterns connect to stress-related functions. By combining an 792 

exploratory 16S rRNA amplicon screen with shotgun metagenomics, culture-based phenotyping, and 793 

genome analysis, the study links bacterial community structure to metagenomic taxonomic and functional 794 

shifts and to traits observed in representative cultured isolates. This integrated design extends prior single-795 

season or culture-focused work on C. colocynthis by linking community dynamics to microbial functions 796 

relevant to desert persistence. 797 

798 

The amplicon data established the system's initial ecological structure and guided the downstream 799 

metagenomic focus. Plant-associated bacterial communities were shaped mainly by season and tissue 800 

type, with little effect of sampling location; richness declined from winter to summer in leaves and roots, 801 

and ordination separated plant tissues by season. In contrast, root-zone soils were comparatively stable 802 

across seasons, but differed compositionally from bulk soils. These patterns identified leaves and roots, 803 

particularly roots, as the most responsive compartments for deeper metagenomic and functional analysis, 804 

while detailed amplicon results are provided in Supplementary Notes S2–3 and Supplementary Figures 805 

S1–S4. 806 

807 

Shotgun metagenomics extended the amplicon framework by providing genus-level bacterial resolution 808 

and broader microbial taxonomic coverage. Both approaches showed stronger seasonal responsiveness in 809 

roots than in leaves, although root Shannon diversity increased in the amplicon data while decreasing in 810 

the metagenomic profiles. This reflects the distinct units each method summarizes: ASV-level evenness 811 

across a broader low-abundance tail in the amplicon screen versus read-level dominance among classified 812 

genera in the shotgun data, where summer expansion of highly abundant actinobacterial genera 813 

suppressed effective diversity while observed genus richness remained stable. Together, the datasets 814

# 5. Conclusion 915 

This study shows that bacterial communities associated with Citrullus colocynthis are structured primarily 916 

by season and tissue type, with roots showing the strongest seasonal restructuring. The 16S rRNA 917 

amplicon screen provided an exploratory framework for selecting the main metagenomic focus, while 918 

shotgun metagenomics extended the analysis to bacterial, fungal, and archaeal taxonomic profiles and 919 

showed that summer root communities were marked by bacterial taxonomic turnover and coordinated 920 

functional shifts. Culture-based assays and genome analyses linked representative isolates to stress 921 

tolerance, plant growth-promoting traits, and biosynthetic potential. In Pseudomonas orientalis ,922 

pangenome analysis further showed that accessory genome content differed between plant-associated and 923 

free-living lifestyles, implicating tryptophan-dependent IAA-related genes in host association and a 924 

bacteriocin-protection gene in environmental competition. Together, these results connect community 925 

composition, functional capacity, cultivable microbial traits, and lifestyle-associated genome variation in 926 

a desert plant holobiont, identifying desert-native bacteria and pathways to inform future microbiome-927 

guided strategies for arid agriculture. 928

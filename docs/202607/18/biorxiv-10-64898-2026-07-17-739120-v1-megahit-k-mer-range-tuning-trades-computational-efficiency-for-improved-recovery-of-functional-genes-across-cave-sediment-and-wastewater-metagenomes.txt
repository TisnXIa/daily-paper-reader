## ABSTRACT 18 

Shotgun metagenomics is a powerful approach for profiling complex microbial ecosystems and discovering functional genes, including antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs). De novo assembly with tools such as MEGAHIT commonly uses multiple k-mer lengths, but the effect of reduced k-mer sets on functional gene recovery has received limited attention. Here, we quantify the trade-off between assembly speed and functional-gene recovery using 17 cave sediment metagenomes and 10 wastewater metagenomes assembled under 19 MEGAHIT k-mer scenarios. In cave metagenomes, finer-grained k-mer ranges recovered more BGCs and, in several pairwise comparisons, more ARGs, but required longer runtimes. In wastewater metagenomes, finer-grained settings most clearly affected ARG recovery, whereas BGC counts did not differ significantly after Friedman testing. These results indicate that reduced k-mer sets can lower computational cost but may miss biologically relevant functional signal, depending on the dataset and downstream target. The study provides a quantitative basis for selecting MEGAHIT k-mer parameters according to whether computational efficiency or functional gene discovery is the primary aim.

## INTRODUCTION 32 

Metagenomics (Oulas et al., 2015; P ´erez-Cobas et al., 2020) is a field of study that allows researchers to 33 

explore the vast microbial diversity present in diverse environments, including surface and subsurface 34 

soil and water habitats, man-made environments or human-associated microbiomes (Guo et al., 2015; 35 

Che et al., 2019; Turrini et al., 2020; Chiciudean et al., 2022; Vojvoda Zeljko et al., 2024; Gorecki et al., 36 

2024). By analyzing DNA sequences directly extracted from these complex samples, metagenomics 37 

enables the identification and characterization of novel microbial species and their functional potential. 38 

Metagenomic assembly is a powerful method for exploring whole microbial communities, including 39 

species which cannot be cultivated in a laboratory. Specialized data analysis tools and pipelines enable 40 

the prospecting of microbial species for possibly novel functional genes and study their encoded products 41 

and associated metabolic traits. Such novel genes of interest include antimicrobial resistance genes 42 

(ARGs) (Tenover, 2006) and biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) (Cimermancic et al., 2014), with a broad 43 

impact on understanding microbial interactions, dispersal and occurrence of antimicrobial resistance, or 44

ARGs confer resistance to various antimicrobials upon their host microorganisms (Aminov and 46 

Mackie, 2007). BGCs, also known as metabolic gene clusters, consist of sets of non-homologous genes, 47 

which are involved in metabolic pathways (Lebedeva et al., 2021). BGCs are predicted to play key roles 48 

in defense mechanisms against antimicrobials. Another role of BGCs is the production of secondary 49 

metabolites involved in microbial competition (Rutledge and Challis, 2015). Consequently, the screening 50 

of ARGs and BGCs across diverse microbial communities is considered a promising approach to facilitate 51 

discoveries with potential implications for innovative industrial bioprocesses (Zada et al., 2022). 52 

In recent years, several pipelines were developed for metagenomic assembly, binning and functional 53 

annotation using domain-specific languages such as Nextflow (Di Tommaso et al., 2017), which was used 54 

to build the nf-core family of data analysis pipelines (Ewels et al., 2020). In the field of metagenomics, 55 

relevant examples of pipelines include nf-core/mag (Krakau et al., 2022) for assembly and binning, and 56 

nf-core/funcscan (Yates et al., 2024) for functional annotation, including gene annotation and the discovery 57 

of ARGs and BGCs. These pipelines automate the specialized workflows from raw sequenced reads to 58 

assembled and annotated (meta)genomes. Once configured, they reproducibly generate assembly and 59 

annotation outputs from any input data, greatly simplifying re-analysis. 60 

A crucial step in any metagenomics pipeline is the metagenome assembly of raw sequenced reads 61 

into contiguous sequences or contigs. Numerous tools and approaches have been proposed for this and 62 

several studies evaluated the performance of these tools (Van der Walt et al., 2017; Vollmers et al., 2017; 63 

Sczyrba et al., 2017; Ayling et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). Software packages such as metaSPAdes (Nurk 64 

et al., 2017) and MEGAHIT (Li et al., 2015) are usually recommended, the former generally having 65 

better accuracy, while the latter offers better efficiency (considerably smaller runtimes and memory 66 

requirements), without bringing a significant drop in accuracy. 67 

For example, Ayling et al. (2020) proposed new approaches for metagenome assembly with short reads, 68 

by describing the existing approaches for metagenome assemblers, including tools like MEGAHIT and 69 

metaSPAdes. Van der Walt et al. (2017) compared the performance of various metagenome assemblers on 70 

complex metagenomes. The authors provided a workflow that aims to guide researchers on selecting the 71 

best metagenome assembler for their metagenomic project, taking into account several aspects, including 72 

available computational resources. Among their recommendations, the MEGAHIT software package 73 

was highlighted due to several advantages over competing tools. Similar to Van der Walt et al. (2017), 74 

Vollmers et al. (2017) compared and evaluated the performance of several metagenome assembly tools, 75 

including MEGAHIT, on metagenomic samples, from the perspective of a microbiologist. Sczyrba et al. 76 

(2017) performed a critical evaluation of the metagenome interpretation for several metagenomic software 77 

tools, including MEGAHIT. Finally, Yang et al. (2021) surveyed several computational tools, including 78 

MEGAHIT, used to generate metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from metagenomic sequencing 79 

data, and provided guidelines for selecting the appropriate tool for MAG generation. 80 

Several advantages of MEGAHIT were repeatedly highlighted in the previously mentioned studies: 81 

it requires less time and less memory compared to SPAdes for assembling complex datasets; it is an 82 

open-source tool; it offers a relatively high flexibility; it provides the highest assembly cost-efficiency 83 

trade-off; it has high sensitivity, i.e., a generally higher probability to include low-abundant community 84 

members in the assemblies; and it is very well documented. 85 

A common strength of both metaSPAdes and MEGAHIT is that they rely on a multiple k-mer-based 86 

approach. Assemblers that use multiple k-mer sizes generally have a better performance compared to 87 

those assemblers using a single k-mer size (Ayling et al., 2020). For example, setting values for the k-mer 88 

size range in the MEGAHIT assembler is possible via parameters k-min , k-max and k-step , where k-min is 89 

the minimum k-mer size, k-max is the maximum k-mer length and k-step is an incremental step. These 3 90 

parameters can effectively specify a range of k-mer sizes, for which MEGAHIT creates a set of succinct 91 

de Bruijn graphs which are then integrated to form the final assembly. For example, Vollmers et al. (2017) 92 

have used a k-mer range defined by k-min =21, k-max =101 and k-step of 10, which yields the following 93 

set of k-mer sizes: 21, 31, 41, 51, 61, 71, 81, 91 and 101. If the desired k-mer range cannot be represented 94 

by such an arithmetic progression, it can be explicitly defined using the k-list parameter. 95 

In the context of metagenome assemblers that employ multiple k-mer sizes, the relationship between 96 

the k-mer sizes and the quality of resulting assemblies has generally been unexplored. An exception is 97 

the work of Qayyum et al. (2025), who compared three scenarios related to k-mer sizes in MEGAHIT 98 

and found that a reduced set of k-mer sizes produced similar quality metrics as those from larger (more 99

## METHODS 121 

The data on which this study was conceived originated from 17 cave samples as follows: 5 sediment 122 

samples from Clo s¸ ani Cave, 4 sediment samples from Ferice Cave, one sediment sample from Bears’ Cave, 123 

and 7 sediment samples collected from Movile Cave in Romania, as previously described (Chiciudean 124 

et al., 2022). These samples were sequenced and paired-end reads were generated using PE150 on the 125 

Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The raw sequenced reads that were generated from these samples were 126 

uploaded to the NCBI SRA database under the project accession number PRJNA777757. The per-sample 127 

accession IDs are listed in Table S1. A second dataset for wastewater metagenomes from a wastewater 128 

system on a university campus was also included (Fierer et al., 2022). Specifically, this dataset was 129 

originally published under NCBI project accession number PRJNA875025 and contains 188 wastewater 130 

samples collected from 17 locations on the University of Colorado campus. A subset of 10 wastewater 131 

samples was randomly selected for this study. The individual accession IDs, sample IDs, read-pair counts 132 

and base counts for these 10 samples are provided in Table S2. 133 

The nf-core/mag pipeline v. 3.0.2 (Krakau et al., 2022) was used for metagenome assembly and 134 

binning. Pipeline executions were set up for each of the assembly scenarios depicted in Table 1. These 135 

assembly scenarios are represented as tuples of values for each of the three k-mer parameters (i.e., k-136 

min , k-max , k-step ), taking into consideration the general constraints imposed by MEGAHIT for these 137 

parameters, as follows. The parameter k-min must be an odd integer ≥ 15. The parameter k-max must be 138 

an odd integer ≤ 127, but greater than or equal to k-min . The parameter k-step must be an even integer 139 

≥ 2 and ≤ 28, such that all k-mer sizes are odd, within a valid range from 15 to 127. MEGAHIT v. 140 

1.2.9 (Li et al., 2015) was used for the metagenome assembly and quality metrics were collected from its 141 

logs. In order to increase specificity, mercy k-mers were excluded and only contigs of a minimum length 142 

of 2000 bp were retained. The values for the k-mer length parameters ( k-min , k-max and k-step ) were 143 

configured as depicted in Table 1. 144 

In contrast to the previous study by C ˘arunta et al. (2024), we excluded the comparison of the resulting 145 

assemblies on metrics related to the number of metagenomic bins (e.g., filt bins , unfilt bins ). These 146 

quality metrics related to metagenomic bins were excluded here because they did not have any relationship 147 

with the identification of ARGs and BGCs, these functional genes being searched within all contigs of 148 

samples and thus were not affected by the binning process. Another reason for excluding these metrics is 149 

the fact that in the previous study they did not have an overall significant effect on the three assembly 150 

parameters (C ˘arunta et al., 2024). This exclusion of quality metrics thus reduced the complexity of the 151 

analyses performed within this study. Note that the terms ”quality metric” and ”variable of interest” are 152 

used interchangeably within this study. 153

The sample-specific assemblies created with MEGAHIT were given as input to nf-core/funcscan (Yates 154

the assemblies created from all the scenarios was set up, enabling the identification of ARGs using 156 

AMRFinderPlus v. 3.12.8 (Feldgarden et al., 2021) and the identification of BGCs using GECCO v. 157 

0.9.10 (Carroll et al., 2021). 158 

Spearman’s rank correlations between various variables (from Table 2) were calculated to study 159 

the strength and type of their association (positive vs. negative correlation). Furthermore, Friedman’s 160 

test followed by Conover’s all-pairs comparisons test for unreplicated blocked data were applied to 161 

identify significant differences between the different scenarios, considering 11 dependent variables that 162 

were collected within this study (Table 2). Statistical analyses were performed in the R programming 163 

language v. 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2024) using the friedman.test function from the stats package and 164 

the frdAllPairsConoverTest function from the PMCMRplus package v. 1.9.12 (Pohlert, 2023), with 165 

Bonferroni correction of p-values for multiple comparisons. The results were considered statistically 166 

significant at a p-value threshold of 0.05. Hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance was performed 167 

on Conover’s test statistics with the hclust function from the stats package with the ward.D2 method, in 168 

order to underline similarity patterns within the scenario pairs and variables, respectively. 169 

All analyses were performed on a virtual computing cluster built on top of OpenStack using HTCondor 170 

v. 8.8.15 (Bockelman et al., 2021), with NFS storage and 8 execution nodes, each with 64 AMD EPYC 171 

7702 2.0 GHz vCPUs and 256 GB of memory. In order to ensure an adequate comparison of runtimes 172 

across the different scenarios, dedicated access to the cluster was ensured and each MEGAHIT process 173 

was allowed to use the full amount of vCPUs and memory available on a single cluster node. 174 

## RESULTS 175 

A total of 19 assembly scenarios were analyzed in this study and the corresponding values for the 176 

parameters k-min , k-max and k-step in MEGAHIT are presented in Table 1. The different settings for these 177 

scenarios were chosen as variations from the one employed by Vollmers et al. (2017) (i.e., k-min =21, 178 

k-max =101 and k-step =10). These scenarios were tested on both previously mentioned datasets. From 179 

the scenario-specific results of the nf-core/mag and nf-core/funcscan pipelines, the following quality 180 

metrics were collected and analyzed: megahit s , contigs , rRNA genes , L50 , max contig , N50 , total bp ,181 

ARG , ARG len , BGC and BGC len . Specifically, information related to the first seven variables ( megahit s ,182 

contigs , rRNA genes , L50 , max contig , N50 and total bp ) was provided by MEGAHIT. For the last 183 

four variables, namely ARG , ARG len , BGC and BGC len , data were collected from the results of the 184 

nf-core/funcscan pipeline. These metrics are defined in Table 2. 185

## DISCUSSION 278 

In this study, we investigated the role of the k-mer size ranges in MEGAHIT on metagenomic assembly 279 

in the context of microbial community-wide identification of ARGs and BGCs from cave and wastewater 280 

microbiomes. We observed that different k-mer settings were associated with differences in the corre-281 

sponding values for variables of interest from one scenario to another. Only pairs of scenarios with a 282 

single difference at the level of k-min , k-max or k-step were considered for further analysis. 283 

Analysis of cave dataset 284 

For the cave metagenome samples specifically, we observed that the pairs of scenarios that were compared 285 

revealed different patterns in terms of test statistic values and p-values computed via Conover’s all-pairs 286 

comparisons test for unreplicated blocked data (see Fig. 2 and Table S7). Scenarios with smaller k-287 

step had increased runtimes compared to scenarios with larger values for this parameter (and a smaller 288 

corresponding number of k-mer sizes in their range). 289

Comparative analysis between caves and wastewater dataset results 423 

In this study, two different environments, namely karstic caves and a wastewater system, were studied 424 

to identify functional genes (ARGs and BGCs). In terms of samples, 17 sediment samples collected 425 

from 4 sites (Clo s¸ ani Cave, Ferice Cave, Bears’ Cave and Movile Cave from Romania) were used for the 426 

first analysis, while 10 wastewater samples from a campus wastewater system were used in the second 427 

analysis. The same experimental setup was applied for both datasets in terms of k-mer length parameter 428 

settings (for assembly scenarios), bioinformatics tools, pipelines and evaluation metrics. 429 

The two datasets showed the same main trade-off: coarser k-mer ranges had reduced runtime, whereas 430 

finer-grained settings preserved more biological signal. In cave samples, this pattern was strongest for 431 

BGC recovery and was also visible for ARG counts. In wastewater samples, the clearest gene-level effect 432 

was on ARG recovery, while BGC did not remain significant after the overall Friedman test. Across both 433 

datasets, increasing k-max generally increased N50 and runtime. By contrast, the effect of k-min was less 434 

uniform across datasets and should be interpreted more cautiously than the k-step and k-max effects. 435

## CONCLUSIONS 457 

This study revealed that the selection of k-mer parameters in MEGAHIT is a critical decision that 458 

introduces a trade-off between computational cost and functional gene discovery. Additionally, the 459 

functional gene discovery also depends on the input data (sequenced reads) for the assembly tool. Based 460 

on our findings, we offer the following recommendations: (1) When aiming to maximize the discovery of 461 

ARGs and BGCs, researchers should consider using fine-grained k-mer sets (i.e., k-step ≤ 4 with k-max 462 

close to its upper limit) in similar discovery-oriented analyses, accepting longer assembly times as the cost 463 

of a more complete assembly and maximizing the recovery of functional elements. (2) If computational 464 

resources are limited and ARG or BGC discovery is not the primary aim, coarser k-mer ranges can be 465 

used, but with the caveat that some ARGs and BGCs may be missed. 466 

Future work would be required in order to replicate these results on a variety of metagenomic datasets 467 

from diverse environments, including a wider range of sample types. In addition, future studies could 468 

envisage the implementation of optimization algorithms in order to maximize or minimize the values of 469 

the desired quality metrics relative to the 3 parameters that control the k-mer range in MEGAHIT. 470

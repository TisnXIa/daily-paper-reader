Abstract 7

Predicting phenotype from genotype in extant organisms is increasingly tractable through the accumulation of genome se-8

quences and the development of machine-learning algorithms. Here we show that machine learning can be applied to reconstructed 9

ancestral gene content, extending these predictions into the past. We trained models on a diverse set of bacterial phenotypes and 10 

found that introducing noise into gene content profiles allows predictions to generalize over larger evolutionary distances. For 11 

phenotypes with signal spread across many genes - such as metabolic oxygen use, cell envelope architecture and optimal growth 12 

temperature - noise augmentation extends resolution back to the root of the bacterial domain, while for other phenotypes -13 

including GC content and sporulation - the range remains more limited. We therefore conclude that the last bacterial common 14 

ancestor (LBCA) was likely an anaerobic, double-membraned, and moderately thermophilic bacterium (46-75 ◦C). Moreover, this 15 

work provides a general approach for learning about the genomic basis of phenotypes and drawing inferences about their early 16 

evolution. 17 

I. I NTRODUCTION 18 

Understanding how genome features give rise to organismal phenotypes is a central question in evolutionary biology [1], [2], 19 

[3]. In microbes, phenotypes such as metabolism, oxygen use, cell envelope architecture, and temperature preference define 20 

ecological niches and shape evolutionary success in a given environment. 21 

Prediction of microbial phenotypes from genomic data has traditionally relied on comparative genomics: identifying specific 22 

genes and pathways through curated functional annotations linked to a target phenotype [4], [5]. These approaches work well 23 

for traits whose genetic basis is already known, but lose resolution when a phenotype is encoded by many genes distributed 24 

across the genome [6], [7], or when functional annotations are incomplete or biased by the absence of close homologs in 25 

model organisms [8], [9]. 26 

The accumulation of microbial genome sequences has enabled a shift toward data-driven approaches. Machine-learning 27 

(ML) models learn predictive patterns directly from genome-wide features, integrating information across thousands of genes 28 

and capturing non-linear and partially redundant associations that are difficult to identify by hand [10], [11]. ML has been 29 

applied to a broad range of microbial phenotypes, including antibiotic resistance [12], [13], metabolic traits [14], [15], and 30 

environmental adaptations [16]. 31 

Phenotypes are encoded in genomes in many ways: through the presence - or sequence - of individual genes, combinations 32 

of genes, gene dosage, and broader regulatory architecture. How this information is encoded and distributed across the genome 33 

remains an open question. From an ML perspective, prediction that generalizes requires genomic patterns that remain informative 34 

beyond closely related taxa and across deep evolutionary distances, rather than lineage-specific shortcuts that inflate accuracy 35 

on near relatives [10], [17]. Whether a phenotype’s signal is concentrated in a few key genes or distributed redundantly across 36 

many, how much of the genome is needed to recover it, and how robust it is to gene loss are therefore not only questions 37

Robust phenotype predictors also enable inference beyond extant organisms, in particular the prediction of phenotypes 40 

for ancestral genomes [18], [14]. Such inferences can reconstruct how key biological traits have evolved through time and 41 

link phenotypic change to environmental and biological conditions across Earth history. In this setting, models are trained 42 

on present-day genomes and applied to reconstructed ancestral ones. However, strong performance on extant data does not 43 

guarantee reliable ancestral inference, for two reasons. First, predictive power decreases over deep evolutionary distances, both 44 

because models generalize poorly and because the way a phenotype is encoded can itself shift over time [19], [20]. Second, 45 

reconstructed ancestral genomes are inherently incomplete and noisy, which can obscure even persistent phenotype signals [21], 46 

[22]. Reliable ancestral prediction therefore requires understanding how phenotype signal is distributed across the genome and 47 

how robust it is to gene loss and noise. 48 

In this work we focus on five microbial phenotypes that span distinct biological mechanisms and levels of predictive difficulty: 49 

oxygen use, cell envelope structure, sporulation ability, optimal growth temperature (OGT), and genomic GC content. Together 50 

they provide a diverse testbed for studying genome-based phenotype prediction across evolutionary scales. We represent each 51 

genome by gene family copy numbers, using COGs assigned by eggNOG-mapper [23], [24] because they map robustly across 52 

prokaryotic genomes. This representation provides a compact summary of functional gene content and matches the output 53 

of ancestral-genome reconstruction methods based on phylogenetic reconciliation [21], [25], [26]. Training predictors directly 54 

on copy-number profiles therefore makes them applicable to reconstructed ancestral genomes, enabling phenotype inference 55 

beyond extant taxa and across deep timescales. 56 

To investigate how phenotypes can be predicted across deep evolutionary distances, and where in the genome they are 57 

encoded, we have built the pipeline in Fig. 1. We start with extant phenotype-annotated genomes, represented as gene family 58 

copy numbers, and systematically evaluate predictive performance under taxonomy-aware train–test splits that progressively 59 

increase the evolutionary distance between training and test sets. We then examine how phenotype signals are distributed 60

often separate cleanly in this embedding, suggesting that phenotype signal is reflected in genome-wide feature patterns and is 72

Models trained on closely related genomes can exploit lineage-specific shortcuts that inflate performance on randomly sampled 75 

test sets. To assess generalization across evolutionary distances, we perform taxonomy-aware train–test splits at five taxonomic 76 

levels (family, order, class, phylum, and domain), generating 30 independent splits per level and phenotype.Given the substantial 77 

class imbalance, we use the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) as the primary performance metric, complemented by 78 

balanced accuracy, recall, F1 score, and the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) to assess the calibration of predicted probabilities. 79 

Since some phenotypes show high within-group homogeneity, see Supplementary Section 2; Figs. 2, and 3, a phylogeny-only 80 

predictor could perform reasonably well by exploiting shared ancestry rather than genomic content. To distinguish these cases, 81 

we compared XGBoost predictions against a phylogeny-based predictions that infers each test genome’s phenotype from its 82 

position on the GTDB tree alone, see Methods, Section IV-B), using identical train–test splits and evaluation metrics. Results 83 

for each phenotype are summarized below. 84 

1) Oxygen use: We here define oxygen use as the ability of organisms to grow in the presence of molecular oxygen. It has 85 

recently been predicted from genomic data based on a machine learning classifiers using genomic features at multiple levels of 86 

resolution, including Pfam protein families [30], [16], annotation-free amino-acid triplet representations [31], and COG-based 87 

genome profiles [14]. Here, we use GTDB genomes annotated with oxygen-use information from the BacDive database [32] 88 

and focus on the binary classification of aerobic and anaerobic organisms. We extend the framework of [14] by evaluating 89 

prediction performance across large evolutionary distances. 90 

Aerobes substantially outnumber anaerobes in our dataset, see Fig. 2(a), left, yet XGBoost maintains strong predictive 91 

performance with well-calibrated confidence estimates even under domain-level holdout splits, see Fig. 2(a), right; Supple-92 

mentary Table 1, where phylogeny-based predictions show a rapid decline in accuracy. This contrast suggests that the learned 93 

signal reflects functional genomic properties of oxygen use rather than mere phylogenetic proximity, generalizing across deep 94 

evolutionary distances. 95

2) Cell envelope: The cell envelope is a deeply conserved physiological trait used to broadly classify bacteria as monoderms 96 

(single membrane, thick peptidoglycan) or diderms (inner and outer membranes, thin peptidoglycan). This classification broadly, 97 

though imperfectly, overlaps with Gram-positive and Gram-negative staining [33], [34]. 98 

We formulate a binary classification task distinguishing between monoderm and diderm organisms. Genomes in the 1007-99 

taxon dataset from [14] were classified as monoderm or diderm based on a literature search [35]. The dataset is highly biased 100 

toward diderm organisms, with most phyla being exclusively monoderm or diderm, see Fig. 2(b), left. Despite substantial 101 

class imbalance, cell envelope phenotype remains highly predictable under deep taxonomic holdout splits, reflecting broadly 102 

conserved genomic signatures across bacterial diversity. Model calibration was also strong across splits, suggesting that 103 

predicted probabilities are reliable and not merely a consequence of class imbalance, see Fig. 2(b), right. Notably, phylogeny-104 

based predictions also perform well up to the phylum level, suggesting that monoderm/diderm identity largely follows deep 105 

phylogenetic structure with limited within-phylum variation. 106 

3) Sporulation: Sporulation is a survival strategy in which some bacteria produce dormant, highly resistant spores in response 107 

to unfavorable conditions [36], [37]. 108 

We used sporulation annotations from [38] and formulated a binary classification task distinguishing sporulating from non-109 

sporulating organisms. In contrast to oxygen use and cell envelope structure, sporulation is phylogenetically restricted, with 110 

sporulating taxa concentrated within a limited number of phyla and most samples annotated as non-sporulating, see Fig. 2(c), 111 

left. Accordingly, predictive performance declines sharply at class and phylum level holdouts, see Fig. 2(c), right; Table 3), 112 

despite models remaining well calibrated across splits, suggesting the model captures lineage-specific genomic signatures rather 113 

than universally conserved sporulation signals, which is consistent with the known restriction of sporulation to specific bacterial 114 

lineages [37]. Notably, cell envelope biosynthesis and sporulation are evolutionarily linked, with multiple independent co-losses 115 

of both traits observed across the class Bacilli, suggesting shared regulatory and biosynthetic dependencies between the two 116 

phenotypes. 117

protein stability, and cellular organization [39]. Predicting OGT from genomic data has been widely explored using machine 120 

learning approaches that leverage features such as amino acid composition [40], and protein family profiles [16], [41]. 121 

Growth temperature optima and ranges were sourced from the BacDive [32] and TEMPURA [42] databases. Annotations 122 

from primary literature were used when multiple sources were available and only species with at least 3 recorded growth 123

B. Determining the conserved genomic basis of phenotypes 146 

While the genome completeness experiments discussed in Section II-A reveal how much genomic information on average is 147 

required for accurate phenotype prediction across evolutionary distances, they do not explain which genes carry the predictive 148 

signal or how this signal is distributed across the genome. For annotated data and a trained predictive model, feature selection 149 

methods fall into two broad categories: model-specific, and model-agnostic. 150 

A commonly used model-specific approach is SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), see Fig. 4(a), which quantifies a 151 

feature’s contribution to prediction by measuring its effect across all possible feature subsets [46]. SHAP identifies the features 152 

most strongly used by the model, but reflects model behavior rather than direct biological or causal relationships [47], and can 153 

be difficult to interpret when features are correlated [48]. 154

To address these limitations, we additionally apply a model-agnostic method based on Markov blanket (MB) discovery [49]. 155 

In this framework, genomic features and the target phenotype are treated as nodes in a Bayesian network, see Fig. 4(b), and 156 

the MB is defined as the minimal set of features that renders the target conditionally independent of all others: 157 

P (target | MB ) = P (target | all features ). (1) Together, these methods allow us to move beyond model accuracy toward biological insight. Features identified by both 158 

SHAP and the Markov blanket represent high-confidence, conditionally indispensable signals, whereas SHAP-only features 159 

likely reflect predictive but redundant correlations, and Markov-blanket-only features capture weak yet essential dependencies 160 

underexploited by the model. 161

For oxygen use, both f train add and the MB size are small, indicating that most predictive performance can be recovered from 181 

only a few features. However, prediction accuracy remains high after removing hundreds of top-SHAP features or retraining on 182

small subset of informative features, as their removal at test time causes a rapid decline in performance. Cell envelope structure 184

In contrast, OGT and GC content rely on a broader feature set, allowing more features to be removed before performance 186

C. Ancestral phenotype prediction 233 

A key motivation for representing genomes as gene family copy numbers is that ancestral copy number profiles can be inferred 234 

using phylogenetic reconciliation methods [26], enabling phenotype prediction beyond extant taxa [14]. However, ancestral 235 

genomes are reconstructed probabilistically rather than observed directly, and inferred gene content becomes increasingly 236 

uncertain toward the root due to cumulative gene duplications, transfers, and losses [54]. Robust phenotype predictors must 237 

therefore tolerate substantial noise and incompleteness in genomic features. 238 

Training on noise-augmented data improves robustness by exposing models to controlled perturbations, encouraging noise-239 

invariant representations that are less sensitive to feature corruption at inference time [55], [56], [57], [58]. We augmented 240 

the training data with synthetic noisy genomes. For each genome, multiple noisy replicas were generated by introducing 241 

false-negative and false-positive gene counts, simulating gene loss and spurious gene presence that can arise during genome 242 

assembly and annotation, see Fig. 6 (a). The noise levels were sampled from predefined distributions to capture a range of 243

For each phenotype, we evaluated multiple augmentation configurations and selected the scheme that 244 

maximized predictive performance on noisy test datasets. Full details of the noise model and parameter selection procedure 245

For oxygen use and cell envelope phenotypes, we observe a clear improvement in model accuracy across the full range of 247 

noise levels in the test datasets, see Fig. 6 (a,b). For the sporulation phenotype, this approach not only improves performance 248 

under high noise conditions but also acts as an effective regularization mechanism, leading to improved accuracy even on 249 

nearly noise-free test data, see Fig. 6(c). For optimal growth temperature, we observe a modest performance improvement 250 

without compromising baseline performance at low noise levels, see Fig. 6(d). In contrast, for GC content prediction, gains at 251 

higher noise levels are accompanied by a slight reduction in performance at low noise levels, indicating a trade-off between 252 

robustness and accuracy in low-noise regimes, see Fig. 6(e). 253 

Next, we applied the best-performing models to ancestral phenotype inference. Our predictions suggest that the last bacterial 254 

common ancestor (LBCA) was likely an anaerobic, non-sporulating, moderately thermophilic diderm, see Fig. 7(a-e) and 255 

Supplementary Table 7. The models differ in prediction confidence across the ancestral timescale, with cell envelope showing 256 

the highest confidence and GC content the lowest, see Fig. 7(f). For GC content, ancestral states therefore cannot be inferred 257 

with comparable confidence. This is consistent with its lineage-restricted genomic signal identified in Section II-B: even after 258 

noise augmentation, the GC content predictor recovers only modest accuracy on noisy genomes, see Fig. 6(e) and already 259 

degrades sharply at class- and phylum-level holdouts in extant data, see Fig. 3; Table I, suggesting that noise augmentation 260 

can reinforce a redundant signal but cannot recover one whose informative components are inherently lineage-specific. 261 

III. D ISCUSSION 262 

Overall, our results suggest that many bacterial phenotypes of evolutionary interest can be inferred from gene content 263 

reconstructions back to the root of the bacterial domain, because information pooled from multiple phenotype-relevant genes 264 

can help to overcome the uncertainty inherent in single gene reconstructions. To understand why some ancient phenotypes can 265 

be predicted and some cannot, we investigated how their genomic signal is structured and how well prediction generalizes across 266 

evolutionary distances. We found that oxygen use and cell envelope structure are underpinned by broadly conserved, genome-267 

wide signals: even after removing the majority of genomic features, predictive performance is largely retained, and models 268 

generalize well even under phylum- and domain-level holdout splits. Sporulation, OGT, and GC content, by contrast, show 269 

sharper performance declines with evolutionary distance, consistent with more lineage-restricted or environmentally contingent 270 

genomic encoding. At least part of the explanation may be that cell envelope architecture and oxygen metabolism are directly 271 

implemented by specific gene families (for example, those encoding components of the aerobic electron transport chain, or the 272 

enzymes that produce outer-membrane lipopolysaccharides and their transporters). By contrast, organisms with particular OGTs 273 

or genomic GC contents may tend to possess certain gene families (enabling prediction over shorter evolutionary distances), 274 

but these properties are not, as far as is currently known, directly “caused” by a specific set of genes. This explanation does not 275 

account for the relatively narrow predictive power of the sporulation classifier, since this trait is also encoded by specific gene 276 

families. However, unlike cell envelope and metabolic phenotypes, sporulation is found in only a handful of modern bacterial 277 

lineages, which may explain its different behaviour in our analyses. 278 

Despite the broad signal availability for at least some of these traits, our test-time feature removal experiments reveal an 279 

important asymmetry: models tend to exploit a small subset of highly informative features rather than the full distributed signal. 280 

When these features are missing or corrupted at inference time (as they inevitably are in reconstructed ancestral genomes), 281 

performance drops sharply even though alternative informative features exist. This distinction between signal availability and 282 

signal usage has practical consequences for ancestral inference and motivates our noise augmentation strategy. By exposing 283 

models to controlled false-positive and false-negative perturbations during training, we encourage reliance on redundant, 284 

distributed signals rather than fragile feature subsets, substantially improving robustness across the full range of noise levels. 285 

Crucially, however, noise augmentation can only reinforce signal that is already broadly distributed: for GC content, whose 286 

predictive signal is inherently lineage-specific, augmentation improves robustness at high noise but ancient GC content cannot 287 

be inferred. 288 

After noise augmentation, the models stay well calibrated even in the presence of substantial false-positive and false-negative 289 

noise. Their predicted class probabilities remain meaningful on the kind of incomplete, error-prone feature vectors produced 290 

by ancestral genome reconstruction. Because expected gene copy numbers can be inferred at internal nodes by phylogenetic 291 

reconciliation [26], this combination lets us carry prediction — and a calibrated estimate of its confidence — back along the 292 

bacterial tree to its root. We exploited this by tracking model confidence as a function of node age, from extant tips to the last 293 

bacterial common ancestor (LBCA), see Fig. 7(f). For phenotypes underpinned by broadly conserved, genome-wide signal, 294 

confidence decays only slowly toward the root and remains high at the deepest nodes, whereas for lineage-restricted phenotypes 295 

such as GC content it falls to near-random well before the LBCA. The practical consequence is that some phenotypes can be 296 

reconstructed confidently into the Archaean. 297

the root, consistent with and extending recent work linking genomic signatures to early Earth history [14]. These ancestral 300 

predictions are broadly consistent with current hypotheses about early bacterial evolution. The inference of an anaerobic LBCA 301 

agrees with the prevailing view that most bacterial lineages predate the Great Oxidation Event and with the conclusions of 302 

[14]. 303 

The inference that the LBCA and the ancestors of Gracilicutes and Terrabacteria were diderms agrees with a growing body 304 

of work suggesting that the double membrane is an ancestral bacterial feature and that monodermy represents a derived state 305 

[51], [59], [60], [61]. The genes most predictive of didermy in our analysis overlap with the canonical diderm machinery — 306 

lipid A and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis and outer-membrane assembly (Section II-B) — consistent with previous 307 

candidate-gene analyses [62], [63], supporting the biological validity of the learned signal. The classifier further predicts multiple 308 

independent losses of the outer-membrane machinery within Terrabacteria, including on the lineages leading to Patescibacteria 309 

and to Firmicutes, in agreement with recent reconstructions [51], [60], [61]. The best-supported scenario is therefore one 310 

in which the double membrane is ancestral in Bacteria and was lost convergently on multiple independent lineages — a 311 

conclusion that stands in contrast to earlier views of early bacteria as monoderm organisms resembling modern Firmicutes or 312 

Actinobacteria [64], [65], and that highlights the outer membrane as an under-appreciated distinguishing feature between early 313 

Bacteria and Archaea. 314 

Although the precise optimal growth temperature of the LBCA cannot be inferred with high confidence, our analysis is 315 

broadly consistent with previous work suggesting a thermophilic LBCA [66], [67], with optimal temperatures in the 46 –75 ◦C316 

range (Fig. 7). In more recent times, Bacteria adapted to somewhat cooler conditions, with some signal of bimodality and a 317 

peak emerging at 37 ◦C. This might result from adaptation to eukaryote-associated lifestyles - including most recently human 318 

guts - although it may also reflect the nature of how experimental OGT data is collected, with 37 ◦C a frequently-tested value 319 

[68]. 320 

While our results encourage optimism about the detail with which ancient life can be reconstructed given modern data, 321 

several limitations should be noted. Ancestral gene content is reconstructed probabilistically, and expected copy numbers 322 

at deep nodes carry substantial uncertainty that compounds toward the root. Our noise augmentation scheme simulates this 323 

uncertainty but cannot fully capture its phylogenetic covariance structure. Published experimental information on phenotypes 324 

collated in BacDive and related databases is highly valuable but incomplete and unevenly distributed across lineages, which 325 

may bias both training and the interpretation of ancestral predictions. In addition, our pipeline was evaluated on five phenotypes 326 

chosen to span a range of biological and predictive complexity; how well these findings generalize to other traits, particularly 327 

those with more complex polygenic architectures or stronger environmental contingency, remains to be explored. 328 

Beyond ancestral inference, the framework developed here offers a general approach for probing the evolutionary signal 329 

of any phenotype encoded in genomic gene content, and for identifying new gene families that appear to be associated with 330 

phenotypes of interest. The combination of taxonomy-aware evaluation, feature redundancy analysis, and noise-robust training 331 

provides a principled basis for assessing which phenotypes are tractable targets for deep-time reconstruction and which are not 332 

— a question of broad relevance as ancestral genome reconstruction methods continue to improve. An immediate extension 333 

would be to apply the same pipeline to other phenotypes of evolutionary and ecological interest, such as nitrogen fixation, 334 

photosynthesis, or antibiotic resistance, and to use the resulting ancestral predictions as calibration points in molecular clock 335 

analyses, as demonstrated previously [14]. 336

B. Phylogeny-based phenotype prediction 346 

In this section, we describe the algorithm used for phenotype prediction based solely on phylogenetic relatedness. We reused 347 

the same 30 train–test splits generated for the XGBoost predictor at each taxonomic level. In each split, 80% of the genomes 348 

constitute the training set and are treated as annotated reference genoms, while the remaining 20% form the test set whose 349 

phenotypes must be inferred. 350 

For each test genome, we traverse the phylogenetic tree to identify the nearest ancestral clade whose descendant leaves 351 

belong entirely to the training set. The taxonomy-aware splits ensure that such a clade exists for every test genome. The 352 

phenotype of the test genome is then inferred from the phenotypes of the genomes within this closest training clade. For 353 

continuous phenotypes, the prediction is defined as the mean phenotype value across the clade. For binary phenotypes, the 354 

predicted class corresponds to the majority class among the genomes in the clade. We note that at the domain level, phenotypes 355 

of bacterial genomes are inferred as the average (or majority class) of archaeal phenotypes, and vice versa. 356 

For random train–test splits, where such taxonomic separation is not guaranteed, we instead assign the phenotype of the 357 

single closest training genome identified using the pairwise phylogenetic distance matrix derived from the genome tree of the 358 

GTDB, see Fig. 8. 359

D. Noise-based training data augmentation 369 

We augment the train datasets by generating x noisy clones of each true genome Gtrue = {gtrue i }1≤i≤N , where gtrue i370 

denotes the true count of gene i. Noise is introduced by injecting false negative and false positive counts as follows. 371 

• To model false negatives, for each gene count gi, we subtract a Poisson-distributed number of counts proportional to gtrue i372 

gnoisy i = max( gtrue i − Poisson (gtrue i sF N ), 0) , (7) where sF N denotes the false negative rate. 373 

• False positives are then introduced for genes with zero observed counts by adding Poisson-distributed counts: 374 

gnoisy i = gtrue i + Poisson (sF P ), (8) where sF P is the false positive rate. 375 

Together, this procedure simulates gene loss and spurious gene presence, approximating the uncertainty induced by genome 376 

reconstruction errors while preserving the overall structure of the genome. 377 

Rather than fixing the false negative sF N and false positive sF P rates, we consider stochastic noise regimes in which these 378 

rates are independently sampled from predefined probability distributions for each noisy genome. Specifically, we focus on three 379 

distribution families: uniform, exponential, and gamma. Each distribution is parameterized by its mean value, denoted λF N ,380 

and λF P for false negatives and false positives, respectively. Each noise augmentation scheme is thus defined by the choice of 381 

the three parameters (x, λ F N , λ F P ). For each phenotype, we systematically explore combinations of these parameters, retrain 382 

XGBoost models for each configuration, and evaluate performance gains on noisy test datasets with varying noise levels. The 383 

test-time noise is introduced by setting non-zero gene counts to zero at rate rF N and incrementing all counts by one at rate 384 

rF P . We then quantify model robustness across the full test-noise range by computing the area under the MCC-versus-noise 385 

curves, similar to those shown in Fig. 6. Finally, for each phenotype, we select the augmentation scheme that maximizes the 386 

average MCC curve integral, corresponding to the greatest overall robustness improvement on noisy test data. The performance 387 

improvements of the best-performing models for each phenotype are summarized in Fig. 6 (see Section 8 in Supplementary 388 

Materials for additional details on model selection and augmentation configurations). 389

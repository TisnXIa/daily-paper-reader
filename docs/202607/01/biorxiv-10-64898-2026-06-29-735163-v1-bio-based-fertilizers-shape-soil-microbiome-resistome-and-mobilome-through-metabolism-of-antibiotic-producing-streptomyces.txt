# Abstract 19 

Streptomyces are abundant soil inhabitants with extensive secondary metabolism and antibiotic 20 

resistance traits. Yet, their ecological role in shaping soil antibiotic resistome dynamics remains 21 

understudied. Here, we investigated how two different bio -based fertilizers harbouring Streptomyces 22 

shaped soil resistome and mobilome by combining genome analysis of eight Streptomyces isolates to 23 

metagenomic profiling of soils before fertilization, within 48 hours after fertilizer application, and six 24 

weeks after. 25 

26 

Streptomyces genomes showed linkages among antibiotic resistance genes, carbohydrate -active 27 

enzymes, and antibiotic -production -associated biosynthetic gene clusters, connecting resistance and 28 

biosynthesis to broader metabolic strategies. Relationships between carbon degradation and 29 

biosynthesis associated with specific enzyme families, indicating that carbon availability shapes 30 

secondary metabolism. We confirmed experimentally that antibacterial potential varied with carbon 31 

source, suggesting that microbial activity during manufacturing of the bio -based fertilizers may create 32 

localized selection pressures before fertilizers enter the soil. 33 

34 

Fertilization with the studied materials induced modest but consistent shifts in resistome and 35 

mobilome without major changes in dominant taxa or overall bacterial abundances, indicating 36 

functional reorganization within soil communities. Diversity of antibiotic resistance genes and mobile 37 

genetic elements increased, whereas abundance changes were small. Mobile genetic element 38 

composition showed stronger responses that were associated with fertilizer inputs, Streptomyces 39 

abundance, and taxa linked to faecal and resistance sources. 40 

41 

Together, our results show that bio -based fertilizers shape soil resistome primarily through ecological 42 

restructuring of resident soil communities, while carbon -dependent microbial activity within 43 

fertilizers may enrich resistance. These factors should be considered in manufacturing of bio -based 44 

fertilizer as well as in designing agricultural practices. 45

# Introduction 46 

Antibiotic resistance is an ancient and widespread microbial trait that has existed long before 47 

humans discovered antibiotics [1] . Environmental bacteria, particularly antibiotic produc ers ,48 

carry intrinsic antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) that likely served as ancestral sources of 49 

resistance later observed in clinical pathogens [2 –4] . These resistance determinants can 50 

transfer across distantly related taxa via mobile genetic elements (MGEs) and other horizontal 51 

gene transfer mechanisms [5] . Yet , successful transfer depend s on ecological context ,52 

including taxon co -occurrence, microbial density , and environmental conditions that enable 53 

DNA exchange [6,7] . Bio -based fertilizers (BBFs) , manufactured from nutrient -rich side -54 

streams such as manure and various compost -based materials , represent environments 55 

where these conditions are often met .56 

Field studies demonstrate that both manure -derived and plant -based amendments can 57 

disseminate ARGs and MGEs in to soils , altering both their abundance and composition [8,9] .58 

Differences in nutrient regimes, organic matter quality, source streams , and manufacturing 59 

practices associated with BBFs can further shape the structure of ARG and MGE pools 60 

introduced in to or selected within soils [10 –13] . Consequently, BBFs may function not only as 61 

reservoirs of resistance determinants but also as ecological contexts that influence resistome 62 

composition and the structure of associated mobilome pools following fertilization. Here, the 63 

resistome refers to the collective repertoire of ARGs within a microbial community, and the 64 

mobilome comprises the MGEs that can mediate or reflect the movement and rearrangement 65 

of genetic material. 66 

During BBF manufacture, raw materials from nutrient -rich side -streams are gathered and 67 

stored in piles and often undergo compostin g or anaerobic digestion , generating dense ,68 

metabolically active microbial communities [14] . Within these communities, members of the 69 

genus Streptomyces are of particular interest [15] . Streptomyces are prolific producers of 70 

antibiotics and other bioactive secondary metabolites . This ex ceptional biosynthetic capacity 71 

is supported by large , dynamic genomes enriched in biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), and 72 

diverse metabolic pathways that enable adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions 73 

[16] . Many BGCs encode intrinsic resistance determinants t hat prevent self -toxicity , making 74 

Streptomyces an important environmental reservoir of ARGs [17 –19] .75 

Carbon availability is a key regulator of secondary metabolism in Streptomyces , with carbon 76 

catabolite repression (CCR) influen cing both carbon utilization pathways and the expression 77 

of BGC s. Different carbon sources have been shown to exert distinct effects on secondary 78 

metabolite production [20,21] . Moreover, c arbohydrate -active enzymes (CAZymes) are 79 

essential to Streptomyces ecology , as they enabl e the degradation of diverse plant - and soil -80 

derived polysaccharides and support the use of a wide range of carbon substrates .81 

Consequently , variation in CAZyme profile s may affect metabolic versatility , influence 82 

secondary metabolite production, and shape the intrinsic resistance profiles associated with 83 

these metabolic pathways. 84

In the resource -rich , decomposing and microbial rich environments characteristic of BBFs, 85 

sustained antimicrobial production by Streptomyces may impose continuous, yet indirect ,86 

selective pressures on co -occurring microbial populations . Such pressure could potentially 87 

contribut e to elevated resistance in the surrounding microbiome and shap e the broader 88 

resistome and mobilome . Although the roles of secondary metabolism, intrinsic resistance, 89 

and horizontal gene transfer in Streptomyces are well established [16,20 –24] , how these 90 

processes are shaped by carbon -utilization strategies remains insufficiently understood. In 91 

particular, the role of CAZym e-mediated substrate degradation modulating secondary 92 

metabolite production and thereby influencing resistance selection dynamics in surrounding 93 

microbial communities, remains largely unresolved. 94 

To address these knowledge gaps, we integrated metagenomics of two BBFs and agricultural 95 

soils fertilized with them with long -read sequencing of eight Streptomyces isolates and 96 

formulated three interconnected hypotheses that explore potential links between 97 

Streptomyces metabolic traits and community -level gene patterns. First, we hypothesize that 98 

Streptomyces associated with BBFs harbour functionally diverse repertoires of ARGs, BGCs, 99 

CAZymes, and MGEs, and their metabolic potential, particularly carbon degradation capacity, 100 

is linked to traits involved in antibiotic production and resistance. Second, we hypothesize 101 

that a subset of ARGs in Streptomyces are associated with MGEs, indicating traces of previous 102 

transfer event s as well as potential for mobility, and that these ARGs and MGEs are present 103 

in the BBFs as well as in fertilized soils. Finally, we hypothesize that BBFs act not only as 104 

disseminators of microbial taxa and genes, but also as ecological drivers that influence the 105 

structure of soil microbiomes, resistomes, and mobilomes after fertilization. Together, this 106 

framework provides a basis for understanding how carbon -mediated metabolism in 107 

Streptomyces and the use of BBFs may influence microbial community dynamics and 108 

resistance gene patterns on short timescales. 109

# Discussion 454 

Our findings show that bio -based fertilizers (BBFs) and Streptomyce tes communities can 455 

influence soil resistome dynamics through ecological processes that begin before the 456 

fertilizers are applied to soil . During manufacture and storage, bacterial communities in BBFs 457 

remain active, allowing them to develop substrate -specific function s, with Streptomyces 458 

playing a key role in secondary metabolite production and carbon cycling [15] . Consequently ,459 

BBFs enter soil with established microbial community structures and functions .460 

Our eight isolates contained a wide variety of carbohydrate -active enzymes (CAZymes) and 461 

biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) , and the strong similarity in their composition suggest s that 462 

these functions may be interconnected rather than independent . The relationship between 463 

carbon degradation capacity and metabolite potential depended on the specific CAZyme 464 

famil ies and BGC type s, indicating that carbon availability affects biosynthetic pathways in 465 

distinct ways rather than increasing biosynthetic diversity uniformly . Isolates with more 466 

diverse CAZyme profiles may therefore maintain the carbon -derived signals that modulate 467 

antibiotic production and antagonistic interactions [16,20,21] , which aligns with the 468 

phenotypically observed carbon -dependent antibacterial activity . Together , the sustained 469 

metabolite production during storage may impose localized selection pressure of co -470 

occurring microorganism s, thereby shaping resistance -related traits before fertilization .471 

Resistance in Streptomyces appeared closely tied to broader functional trait s. CAZymes, 472 

antibiotic -production -associated BGCs, and resistance genes formed phylogenetically 473 

structured associations , while connections to mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were weak . In 474 

contrast t o our hypothesis, no ARG -MGE co -localization was dete cted . This suggests that 475 

resistance in Streptomyces is built into the intrinsic metabolic and ecological strategies rather 476

than acquired via horizontal gene transfer , which aligns with how Streptomyces genome s477 

evolve primarily  through duplication and rearrangement of BGCs [16,17,19,22,23] . In 478 

addition, only a subset of the isolate -derived ARGs and MGEs w ere detected in metagenomes, 479 

typically at low abundance s. This pattern is consistent with the idea that soil resistomes are 480 

largely shaped by existing gene pools rather than by the introduction of new resistance genes 481 

through fertilization [1,3,4] . The limited detection of isolate -derived genes in fertilizer 482 

metagenomes likely reflects methodological detection limits, emphasizing the 483 

complementary use of isolate genomics and metagenomics across abundance levels. F or 484 

example , DNA extraction biases can skew representation toward more readily lysed taxa, 485 

while library preparation and sequencing steps, may influence detection of low -abundance 486 

genes, contributing to underrepresentation of certain taxa and functions [60 –64] .487 

At the bacterial community scale , fertilization altered resistome and mobilome without major 488 

shifts in dominant taxa or overall bacterial abundance, suggesting a decoupling between 489 

taxonomic composition and functional change. This pattern implies that responses of the 490 

resistome and mobilome are driven by shifts in ecological interactions rather than taxonomic 491 

turnover . Similar patterns have been observed in manure -amended soils [10] , and animal -492 

production settings, where increased ARG -MGE co -occurrence was observed without 493 

detectable shifts in community composition [12] . Such responses reflect the ability of soil 494 

microbiomes to maintain stability while remaining functionally flexible under environmental 495 

perturbation s.496 

In this context , the mobilome was more responsive , showing coherent restructuring and 497 

strong associations with taxonomic (e.g. Streptomyces , and faecal -associated taxa) and 498 

functional (ARG abundance and diversity) gradients. Fertilizer origin further modulated these 499 

patterns : manure -based inputs were associated with MGEs linked to clinically relevant and 500 

opportunistic taxa, whereas compost -based inputs showed a wider range of associations with 501 

environmental and opportunistic groups . Th ese differences suggest that BBF source material s502 

define how the interactions of introduced genetic elements interact with soil communities. 503 

The observed coupling between ARGs and MGEs further supports the idea that these 504 

functional components respond together and MGEs act as a dynamic link that reshap es the 505 

resistome within the community . In contrast to the structured, largely immobile resistance 506 

observed in Streptomyces genomes, these patterns point to community -level processes 507 

shaped by the movement and rearrangement of existing gene s rather than direct transfer of 508 

specific traits [5 –7] . Despite these shifts , the overall change s were modest and mostly 509 

reversible, indicating a degree of resilience in the soil system [10] .510 

Some limitations linked to our approach should be noted. Genomic analyses show potential 511 

functions but not actual activities of the bacterial species. Conclusively linking carbon 512 

metabolism, antibiotic production, and resistance would require integration of genomics with 513 

transcriptomic and metabolomic data. Metagenomics alone cannot fully capture horizontal 514 

gene transfer dynamics. For this, one would need to use approaches such as Hi -C or plasmid -515

Taken together, our results support our hypothesis that Streptomyces and BBFs impact soil 518 

resistomes through metabolically driven ecological processes shaped by the fertilizer 519 

composition and the soil community. Although th e observed effects were modest and short -520 

term, they are not negligible considering the evolutionary potential of the soil resistome ,521 

which may gradually alter resistance dynamics with long -term use of microbiologically active 522 

fertilizers .523

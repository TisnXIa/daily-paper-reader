# Abstract 12 

Orphan genes - genes lacking detectable homologs outside a species - are widespread in 13 microbial genomes and are thought to contribute to their adaptation and molecular innovation. 14 However, not all predicted orphan genes may represent novel functional coding sequences. 15 False positive orphan genes, also called spurious orphan genes, can arise from gene prediction 16 errors. We reason that orphan genes lacking detectable expression are more likely to be 17 spurious. To test this, we combined large-scale metatranscriptomic profiling of the human gut 18 microbiome with machine learning to distinguish expressed orphan genes from spurious ones 19 and to compare them with conserved genes found in multiple species. Using nearly 5,000 20 metatranscriptome libraries, we identified ~218,000 orphan genes supported by expression 21 evidence, while ~330,000 predicted orphan genes lacked detectable expression, and were 22 classified as spurious. We extracted 154 features for sequence, structural, and evolutionary 23 properties for each gene and trained XGBoost classifiers while accounting for genomic 24 representation. The models achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 25 (AUC) of 0.82 in distinguishing expressed orphan genes from spurious orphan genes and an 26 AUC of 0.93 in distinguishing expressed orphan genes from conserved genes. SHAP-based 27 interpretation revealed clear biological signals. Particularly, expressed orphans were present in 28 more genomes than spurious ones and expressed orphan genes were shorter than conserved 29 genes. This work improves orphan gene discovery and suggests that expressed orphan genes 30 differ systematically from conserved genes and spurious orphan genes in sequence composition, 31 structural constraints, and evolutionary signals. 32

# Significance statement 37 

Species-specific genes, also called orphan genes, are abundant in bacterial genomes and have 38 the potential to drive evolutionary innovation. However, the study of orphan genes is 39 confounded by annotation errors that produce spurious gene predictions. Here, we leveraged 40 nearly 5,000 metatranscriptomes from the human gut microbiome and implemented machine 41 learning models to distinguish expressed orphan genes from non-expressed, likely spurious, 42 genes and conserved genes. We show that a substantial fraction of predicted orphan genes lacks 43 expression support and they exhibit distinct sequence and evolutionary signatures from 44 expressed orphans. By integrating expression evidence with sequence-, structure-, and 45 evolution-based features, our work refines orphan gene catalogs. This work improves the 46 reliability of orphan gene discovery and provides new insights into the biological 47 characteristics of orphan genes in human microbial communities. 48 

# 1. Introduction 49 

Orphan genes (OGs), also known as orphans, are defined as having no homologs outside a 50 given species or outside a particular lineage (Dujon 1996; Domazet-Loso and Tautz 2003; Li 51 et al. 2022). OGs are ubiquitous in each newly sequenced genome, including those of bacteria, 52 archaea, and phages (Tautz and Domazet-Lošo 2011). Compared to conserved genes (CGs), 53 which occur in multiple species, OGs are characterized by fewer exons in eukaryotes, shorter 54 lengths, higher Adenine-Thymine (AT) content, higher disorder protein structure, higher 55 nonsynonymous substitution rate, and higher isoelectric points (Daubin and Ochman 2004; 56 Fakhar et al. 2023; Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). 57 58 OGs are thought to play a significant role in evolution and speciation, likely contributing to 59 crucial species- or lineage-specific adaptive processes by facilitating the generation of novel 60 functions (Tautz and Domazet-Lošo 2011; Arendsee et al. 2014; Vakirlis et al. 2020; Fakhar et 61 al. 2023; Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). However, the role of OGs in evolution remains elusive 62 due to their lack of phylogenetic conservation and their origination by diverse of evolutionary 63 processes (Toll-Riera et al. 2009; Tautz and Domazet-Lošo 2011; Fakhar et al. 2023). OGs 64 potentially arise de novo from non-coding genomic regions or alternative reading frames of 65 existing genes, through fast divergence, or by horizontal gene transfer (HGT) (Tautz and 66 Domazet-Lošo 2011; Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). Thus, OGs originate by different 67 mechanisms and are of different age, forming a heterogenous group (Pereira et al. 2025). 68 69 Some experimental studies have shown that proteins derived from random sequence libraries 70 can acquire biologically relevant functions in prokaryotic systems (Babina et al. 2023; Frumkin 71 et al. 2025). This suggests that even short, newly emerged sequences may rapidly become 72 functional, supporting the idea that orphan genes can contribute to evolutionary innovation. 73 Most of our current understanding of OGs and the evolutionary mechanisms has been derived 74 from studies in eukaryotes (Tautz and Domazet-Lošo 2011; Prabh and Rödelsperger 2019). In 75 addition to studies in eukaryotes, recent work has highlighted the importance of orphan genes 76 in non-eukaryotic systems, including viruses, where lineage-specific genes can play key roles 77 in host interaction and adaptation (Nomburg et al. 2024). Additionally, studying prokaryote 78 orphan genes is particularly interesting and relevant for a number of reasons (uz-Zaman and 79 Ochman 2025). Prokaryotes are ubiquitous, ample genome data is available, and they are found 80 in all ecosystems and play crucial roles in various ecological processes, such as nitrogen 81 fixation and organic matter decomposition, which are essential for ecosystem functioning and 82 stability (Whitman et al. 1998; Meng et al. 2022). Moreover, pathogenic prokaryotes can cause 83

Thus, understanding prokaryotic OGs can provide 85 insights into their unique environmental adaptations and mechanisms of pathogenicity, 86 potentially leading to new treatments and preventive measures. 87 88 In a previous study, we constructed an orphan reference dataset of predicted species-specific 89 OGs from the human gut environment — a well-explored habitat accommodating numerous 90 prokaryotic species (Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). A key question is whether this dataset truly 91 contains only novel, functional coding sequences unique to each species. False positive OGs 92 could arise in two ways. First, non-existent genes may be incorrectly annotated as coding 93 sequences because OGs are typically short and often lack evolutionary conservation (Yu and 94 Stoltzfus 2012); we refer to these as spurious OGs (SOGs). Second, genes that are actually 95 conserved may be misclassified as OGs when distant homologs remain undetected, leading to 96 an overestimation of lineage-specific genes (Weisman et al. 2020). In this study, we focus on 97 the first category, SOGs. Our identification of SOGs is based on the expectation that such 98 spurious predictions lack detectable expression. To collect expression evidence for OGs, we 99 used human gut metatranscriptomics data to distinguish expressed OGs (EOGs) from SOGs, 100 defining EOGs as genes with sufficient coverage by mapped reads across multiple samples. 101 Genes without any expression are labelled SOGs and may be artifacts. 102 103 We then classified genes as EOGs and SOGs using machine learning (ML) models. ML offers 104 a powerful tool for classification tasks by effectively learning the relevant features in the data 105 and using them to make accurate predictions. These models, such as random forest (Breiman 106 2001) and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin 2016), can be trained to identify complex relationships 107 between input features and output labels. Sequence analysis has been the primary source for 108 studying genes being CGs or OGs, focusing on features such as gene length, GC content, 109 isoelectric point, and codon adaptation index (Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). Structural 110 properties also differed, particularly protein disorder levels were observed to be higher in OGs 111 (Basile et al. 2017). Recently, accurate protein language models such as ESMFold (Lin et al. 112 2023) have been proposed, greatly advancing the accuracy of protein structure prediction. 113 Because orphan proteins lack homologous sequences, structure prediction methods relying on 114 multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) often perform poorly. In contrast, protein language 115 model–based approaches have been shown to outperform such methods on orphan proteins 116 (Michaud et al. 2022). Here, we used ProstT5 (Heinzinger et al. 2024), which encodes protein 117 sequence information directly from protein sequences without relying on MSAs, to extract so-118 called 3Di token representations of protein structures from amino acid sequences. While this 119 approach enables structure-related features to be derived without relying on homologous 120 sequences, it may also introduce biases, as protein language models such as ProstT5 are trained 121 predominantly on datasets enriched for conserved proteins. Consequently, structural 122 representations for orphan proteins, which typically lack homologs, may be less reliable. In 123 this study, we address the following research questions. First, what proportion of predicted 124 orphan genes in the human gut microbiome are likely to be spurious due to annotation artefacts? 125 Second, which features are most informative for distinguishing EOGs from SOGs and which 126 features distinguish EOGs from CGs? To answer these questions, we developed machine 127 learning models using sequence-, evolution-, and structure-based features, and evaluated their 128 performance and feature contributions. 129

# 2.1 A large proportion of orphan genes are likely spurious 131 In earlier work, we found 631,104 unique species-specific prokaryotic orphan genes from the 132 human gut microbiome (Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). To investigate which of these genes are 133 supported by transcriptional evidence, we retrieved 4,969 prokaryotic human gut 134 metatranscriptomics libraries from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (Leinonen et al. 2011) 135 and mapped them to our orphan reference dataset (Figure 1). For each gene, we calculated 136 expression coverage across all libraries, assuming that expressed genes are likely true genes 137 and non-expressed genes may be spurious. A gene with no coverage in all libraries was 138 classified as a spurious orphan gene (SOG). We found that the number of genes detected in at 139 least one library decreased continuously with the coverage cutoff (Figure S1A) and additionally 140 the number of detected genes decreased with the number of libraries in which each gene was 141 detected as expressed (Figure S1B). In the following, we denote a gene with coverage ≥ 1.2 in 142 at least 2 libraries as an expressed orphan gene (EOG). These thresholds were selected to 143 minimize technical artifacts by requiring both a reliable coverage level and consistent detection 144 across multiple libraries. 145 146 Figure 1 . # 3. Discussion 331 

The dataset of microbial orphan genes analyzed here originates from large-scale predictions of 332 species-specific genes in the human gut microbiome (Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). Various 333 evolutionary processes create orphan genes and bacteria offer large potential to study these due 334 to the ample genomic data that is available at species and strain level (uz-Zaman and Ochman 335 2025). For example, remodeled gene families have been detected using network approaches 336 (Watson et al. 2022), genes originating from divergence have been distinguished using 337 simulations (Tassios et al. 2026), and de novo genes have been traced using omics approaches 338 (uzZaman and Ochman 2025). Nevertheless, most previous studies have treated all predicted 339 orphans as a single category without considering their evolutionary heterogeneity (Pereira et 340 al. 2025). Additional heterogeneity is created by spurious predictions that can arise from 341 annotation errors or non-functional open reading frames, which has likely obscured the 342 functional and evolutionary signals of true orphans. Here, by explicitly distinguishing between 343 SOGs, which show no expression evidence, and EOGs, we aimed to separate non-expressed 344 artifacts from genuine OGs and to identify the features that best distinguish EOGs vs. SOGs, 345 and EOGs vs. CGs. Our analysis of conserved genes shows that a larger proportion of CGs is 346 expressed compared OGs. This suggests that expression provides a useful signal for 347 distinguishing between bona fide and spurious OGs, albeit imperfect for separating more likely 348 functional genes from less likely ones. Our results further show that the apparent importance 349 of evolutionary features is strongly influenced by sampling bias due to the number of genomes 350 representing a species. This pattern is likely explained by the fact that species with more 351 available genomes are typically more abundant in the gut microbiome, and therefore contribute 352 a larger proportion of reads in metatranscriptomic datasets, increasing the likelihood of 353 detecting gene expression (Almeida et al. 2021). Thus, their genomes are better represented in 354 the human gut environment, which could lead to higher expression levels in 355 metatranscriptomics data. To address this, we specifically filtered out species with low 356 genomic representation and restricted the analysis to well-represented species, thereby 357 ensuring that SOGs and EOGs had comparable distributions of genome counts across species. 358 Under this controlled scenario, much of the bias disappeared, highlighting the importance of 359 considering differences between species when studying orphan genes. 360 Here we analyze feature importance using SHAP values, which jointly evaluate contributions 361 of all features. This approach quantifies not only whether a feature is informative, but also its 362 importance relative to other features, which allows us to analyze all features at once. Feature 363 importance analyses highlight the number of genomes a gene is present in as an informative 364 signal for distinguishing SOGs from EOGs. Many SOGs are restricted to only a few genome 365 occurrences, which are consistent with their likely origin as prediction errors or non-functional 366 sequences. This observation can also be framed in the context of pangenome structure. The 367 pangenome of a species encompasses the complete set of genes across all sequenced genomes 368 and is typically divided into categories. The core genome comprises genes present in nearly all 369 strains, encoding essential or highly conserved functions, whereas the accessory genome 370 contains genes restricted to only a subset of strains (Matthews et al. 2024; Vakirlis and 371 Kupczok 2024). In pangenome studies, such low-frequency genes are known to be enriched in 372 annotation errors or assembly artifacts (Tonkin-Hill et al. 2020). SOGs tend to be rare genes 373 and could thus be also enriched in annotation artefacts. Nevertheless, EOGs were determined 374 using expression signal, which might be easier to detect for high-frequency genes; thus, true 375 low-frequency orphan genes could still be classified as SOGs. 376

Our approach is able to detect distinguishing sequence features for classifying EOGs vs. SOGs 377 and EOGs vs. CGs. GC content at the third synonymous codon position (GC3s) followed the 378 order CG > EOG > SOG. Given that GC3s is strongly linked to synonymous codon usage bias 379 and is interpreted as a signature of translational selection acting on coding sequences (Wan et 380 al. 2004; Babbitt et al. 2014), the higher GC3s in EOGs relative to SOGs suggests that EOGs 381 are unlikely to be transcriptional artifacts but are instead subject to selective pressures shaping 382 codon usage, potentially enhancing translational efficiency. This is consistent with the even 383 higher GC3s values of CGs indicating stronger selection in conserved genes. From an 384 evolutionary perspective, the intermediate GC3s levels observed in EOGs suggest that OGs are 385 relatively young coding sequences that are in the process of adapting to the cellular 386 environment and gradually acquiring functional characteristics. In contrast, the lower GC3s 387 values in SOGs are consistent with weak or absent selective constraints on codon usage, as 388 expected for non-functional open reading frames. Beyond GC3s, we observed that several 389 dinucleotides show differential enrichment between EOGs and SOGs, but not between EOGs 390 and CGs. Dinucleotide composition is shaped by genome-wide mutational processes and can 391 serve as a genomic signature that characterize the sequence composition of genes (Kariin and 392 Burge 1995; Chen et al. 2014). Thus, SOGs show sequence compositions that deviate from 393 EOGs consistent with the expectation that SOGs represent spurious ORFs or annotation 394 artifacts. Furthermore, CDS length emerged as one of the most informative features across 395 comparisons. SHAP analysis revealed that: CGs possess longer CDSs than EOGs, while SOGs 396 also tend to have longer CDSs than EOGs. The short length of EOGs is consistent with their 397 potential role as recently emerged genes (Tautz and Domazet-Lošo 2011; Vakirlis and 398 Kupczok 2024). The longer CDSs observed in SOGs, despite lacking expression support, likely 399 result from annotation artifacts. Gene prediction pipelines are biased toward reporting long 400 open reading frames (Choudhary et al. 2020), for example, gene-prediction pipelines such as 401 Prokka (Seemann 2014) and Prodigal (Hyatt et al. 2010) tend to detect long open reading 402 frames, thereby increasing the chance of spurious annotations in sequences without 403 experimental support. Thus, SOGs may appear long in sequence, but this does not equate to 404 functional or structural validity. 405 

De novo genes in eukaryotes are often thought to originate from previously non-coding open 406 reading frames (ORFs), but in prokaryotic genomes, which are highly compact and dense in 407 coding regions, new genes may more commonly arise from alternative reading frames within 408 existing coding sequences (Watson et al. 2022). Thus, some de novo–originated EOGs likely 409 arose from alternative reading frames. Our results have shown that EOGs exhibit different 410 proportions of amino acids compared with CGs and SOGs. Amino acid composition is a major 411 determinant of intrinsic structural disorder (Wilson et al. 2017), and is therefore subject to 412 biophysical and evolutionary constraints. A previous study reported that predicted intrinsic 413 structural disorder is highest in de novo proteins, intermediate in random sequences, and lowest 414 in conserved proteins (Middendorf and Eicholt 2024). Although structural disorder does not 415 appear as an important feature in any of our analyses, the observed differences in amino acid 416 composition, particularly in the content of acidic amino acids, across EOGs, SOGs, and CGs 417 could reflect differences in the strength of selection acting on these genes. CGs encode proteins 418 shaped by long-term selection, whereas EOGs likely represent younger proteins that have 419 begun to acquire functional constraints. In contrast, SOGs, which lack expression support, are 420 expected to be under little or no selective pressure and may therefore exhibit amino acid 421 compositions that are closer to those of random sequences. 422 Interestingly, only few structural features appeared among the top predictors in our SHAP 423 analysis for distinguishing EOGs from SOGs. This likely reflects a methodological limitation 424

Specifically, the structural predictor we used was 425 ProstT5 (Heinzinger et al. 2024), which itself was trained on AlphaFold’s protein sequences 426 and 3Di information, and the 3Di representation was obtained via Foldseek (van Kempen et al. 427 2024). Because AlphaFold relies on multiple sequence alignments, its structural embeddings 428 are not independent of evolutionary conservation information. Consequently, structural 429 features may overlap with the sequence features already used in the model, reducing their 430 apparent contribution to orphan genes prediction. 431 In summary, our analysis shows that a substantial proportion of predicted orphan genes in gut 432 microbiomes are likely spurious, while a smaller subset exhibits consistent expression. By 433 integrating expression evidence with sequence, structural, and evolutionary features, we 434 established a framework for the discrimination between true orphan genes and prediction 435 artifacts. The findings enhance our understanding of orphan genes and provide a foundation 436 for future studies on the origination and functional roles of orphan genes in microbial 437 communities. 438 

# 4. Materials and Methods 439 

# 4.1 Dataset construction 440 

In a previous study, orphan genes (OGs) were identified through a multi-step filtering process 441 (Vakirlis and Kupczok 2024). First, the Unified Human Gastrointestinal Protein catalogue, 442 clustered into protein families at 50% identity (UHGP50 v1.0) (Almeida et al. 2021) was 443 analyzed and protein families present in multiple species were removed, along with those 444 having a match in EggNOG (Buchfink et al. 2021; Hernández-Plaza et al. 2023). Next, 445 DIAMOND searches were performed against the NCBI prokaryotic RefSeq database, 446 removing sequences with significant matches (E-value < 10 -3 ), except for those with >90% 447 coverage and >95% identity, which were likely from the same species. A final BLASTp 448 (Altschul 1997) search against the UHGP50 protein catalogue further filtered out sequences 449 matching proteins from different species (E-value < 10 -5 ). To reduce contamination, OGs found 450 on contigs containing only orphans were removed. The remaining sequences formed our 451 reference dataset of 631,104 species-specific OGs, while the non-orphan families were labelled 452 conserved genes (CGs), totaling 4,104,442. In the following, we focused on highly conserved 453 genes (defined as genes present in >10 species, 26,545 genes in total) rather than all conserved 454 genes because their broad phylogenetic distribution provides stronger evidence of long-term 455 evolutionary conservation, making them a more reliable group to compare to orphan genes. 456 

457 The Snakemake (v7.32.4) (Mölder et al. 2021) workflow management system was used to 458 develop an automated pipeline (Figure 1, https://github.com/cheche0109/OGs-SOGs-CGs). 459 The pipeline includes processing and alignment of metatranscriptomics sequencing data sets 460 against our orphan reference dataset and generating BAM files for further analysis to 461 distinguish EOGs and SOGs. 462 

4.1.1 Search and download of metatranscriptomics data. We analyzed 463 metatranscriptomics data to label expressed genes in our reference dataset as EOGs, and non-464 expressed genes as SOGs. To this end, we searched for libraries containing raw 465 metatranscriptomics data in the sequence read archive (SRA) (Katz et al. 2022) (10 April 2024) 466 database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). To ensure that the 467 included metatranscriptomics data originated from prokaryotic human gut metagenomes, we 468 used the following criteria: (i) human gut metagenome, NOT mouse gut metagenome, (ii) AND 469

metatranscriptomics, AND biomol RNA, (iii) AND prokaryote, NOT virome. The compressed 470 RNA-seq read data (.fastq.gz) of the accessions was downloaded directly using fasterq-dump 471 (v3.1.1) (Leinonen et al. 2011) from the SRA Toolkit. 472 

4.1.2 Read processing. Cutadapt (v4.0) (Martin 2011) with the parameter -q 30 was used 473 to trim low-quality bases from the compressed sequencing reads downloaded from the SRA 474 database. Trimmed reads were mapped to our orphan reference genes with CoverM (v0.7.0) 475 (Aroney et al. 2025) using the command coverm make --discard-unmapped -p bwa-mem2 ,476 generating BAM files directly converted from the SAM outputs. Reads with a mapping quality 477 score of 0 were then removed using the command samtools view -hbq 1 to eliminate low-478 confidence alignments and ensure accurate coverage estimation. 479 480 

4.1.3 Labeling EOGs and SOGs. We used CoverM with the command coverm contig --481 

min-read-percent-identity 95 --min-read-aligned-percent 75 --min-covered-fraction 0.6 --482 

contig-end-exclusion 0 to calculate read coverage per gene from the bam files, without 483 excluding any base pairs from the beginning or end of each gene. To ensure that only highly 484 similar reads are mapped, we require at least 95% identity to the reference gene and to exclude 485 short, ambiguous matches, we require a minimum of 75% of the read length aligned to the gene. 486 Finally, to ensure that the detected expression is biologically meaningful rather than resulting 487 from fragmented or low-confidence mappings, we retained only genes with at least 60% of 488 their length covered by mapped reads. Genes that did not meet the defined thresholds for any 489 library were classified as SOGs. For the remaining genes, we explored the influence of 490 threshold settings on coverage and the number of libraries in which a gene was detected. Genes 491 exceeding the coverage threshold and present in at least 2 libraries were designated as EOGs. 492 493 

4.1.4 Expression analysis of CGs. To evaluate whether the absence of detectable 494 expression is a reasonable criterion for identifying potentially SOGs, we performed a parallel 495 expression analysis on conserved genes (CGs). To reduce bias caused by uneven genomic 496 representation across species, we restricted the analysis to species with 497 Number_of_genomes_in_species ≥ 700, consistent with the filtering strategy used in our 498 orphan gene analyses. We then estimated low, medium, and high genomic representation levels 499 using the 25%, 50%, and 75% quantiles of Number_of_genomes_in_species among the 500 eligible species. Based on these thresholds and the species phylogeny, we selected three 501 phylogenetically distant representative species with different genome representation levels: 502 GUT_GENOME001602 (Holdemanella sp002299315), GUT_GENOME239670 503 (Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum ), and GUT_GENOME140299 ( Prevotella copri ). 504 For each selected species, we extracted CGs belonging to that species by retaining genes 505 present in more than 10 species across the UHGP-50 dataset. Expression analysis was 506 performed using the same Snakemake pipeline and the same set of 4,969 human gut 507 metatranscriptomic libraries described above. Read processing, mapping, and coverage 508 estimation were conducted identically to the procedure used for OGs. A CG was considered 509 expressed if it showed detectable coverage in the metatranscriptomic data according to the 510 same criteria applied to OGs. 511

# 4.3 Machine learning 556 

To classify SOGs versus EOGs, and EOGs versus CGs, we developed machine learning models 557 based on XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin 2016). For each comparison, we constructed a balanced 558 dataset by randomly downsampling the majority class to achieve equal representation of both 559 classes. Additionally, prior to model training, z-score normalization was applied using 560 parameters (mean and standard deviation) estimated from the training set only, which were 561 then used to normalize the test data. 562 Classifiers were trained on Fseq , Fstruct , Fevo , and Fall . Model selection and hyperparameter 563 tuning were performed using a nested cross-validation strategy: an inner 4-fold cross-validation 564 for hyperparameter optimization, and an outer 5-fold stratified cross-validation to assess 565 generalization performance. The hyperparameter grid search explored the following parameter 566 ranges: n_estimators: [100, 200], max_depth: [3, 5, 7], learning_rate: [0.005, 0.01, 0.1], 567 subsample: [0.8, 1.0], colsample_bytree: [0.8, 1.0]. 568 Model performance was primarily evaluated using the area under the receiver operating 569 characteristic curve (AUC). For each comparison, we report the mean AUC ± standard 570 deviation (SD) across inner folds. SD reflects variability among the folds. For interpretability, 571 we extracted feature importance from the trained models and applied SHAP (SHapley Additive 572 exPlanations) (Lundberg and Lee 2017) to quantify the contribution of each feature to the 573 classification outcome. 574

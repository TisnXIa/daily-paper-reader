Abstract 

Plant-associated microbiomes contribute to plant health and resilience, yet the extent to which 1

host traits shape microbiome assembly remains poorly understood despite increasing interest in 2

leveraging them for crop performance. Here, we investigated whether drought-response 3

phenotypes are associated with reproducible patterns of microbiome assembly across peanut 4

genotypes under field conditions. The cultivars represented three drought-response categories: 5

water-savers with tighter stomatal regulation, water-spenders with deeper root systems, and 6

drought-sensitive genotypes. Bacterial and fungal communities were characterized from bulk 7

soil, rhizosphere, and root endosphere compartments of six non-stressed peanut cultivars. Both 8

host genotype and drought-response phenotype were associated with microbiome composition, 9

with phenotype-associated patterns remaining detectable across multiple genetic backgrounds. 10 

Unexpectedly, the strongest phenotype-associated differences occurred in bulk soil communities, 11 

suggesting plant-mediated effects extending beyond the immediate root zone. Community 12 

differences were driven primarily by shifts in the relative abundance of existing taxa rather than 13 

turnover of distinct microbial lineages. Fungal communities responded more strongly to host 14 

phenotype than bacterial communities, with water-spender genotypes supporting greater fungal 15 

diversity and uniquely enriched taxa in the rhizosphere and endosphere. Neutral community 16 

modeling indicated stronger deterministic filtering of fungi than bacteria. Together, these 17 

findings demonstrate that drought-response phenotypes shape reproducible microbiome variation 18 

before stress exposure. 19

Introduction 32 

33 

Peanut is one of the most widely cultivated legumes globally (Wagner, 2021). They are a crucial 34 

part of agricultural ecosystems in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia and Africa, where they 35 

largely contribute to food security and soil fertility (due to biological mechanisms such as 36 

nitrogen-fixation) (Wagner, 2021). A large percentage of peanut production occurs in semi-arid 37 

or seasonal rainfall zone, where drought episodes in regions reliant on these rain-fed agricultural 38 

systems cause devastating yield loss (Oyserman et al., 2022). Due to this, drought has been 39 

identified as a dominant threat to the peanut industry where it contributes to roughly 50 million 40 

USD in losses annually (Pokhrel et al., 2025). As the severity and duration of droughts are 41 

expected to increase alongside rapidly changing environmental conditions, new strategies are 42 

being developed to increase crop resilience to drought and other abiotic stressors alongside other 43 

mitigation efforts like enhancing water use efficiency and irrigation redesigns. 44 

45 

Plant hosts exhibit intimate relationships with complex networks of bacteria, archaea, fungi, and 46 

viruses that are collectively referred to as the plant microbiome. While it is well known that 47 

microorganisms help to maintain fundamental aspects of all life, the plant microbiome has 48 

gained increased attention for its role in modulating plant health (Turner et al., 2013). Plant 49 

microbes are known to assist in nutrient acquisition, protection against biotic and abiotic 50 

stressors, and overall growth promotion of the host (Yusuf et al. 2025). Different areas of a plant, 51 

or “plant compartments”, provide specialized environments for microorganisms that are uniquely 52 

enriched depending on the requirements of the host (Rossmann et al., 2017) . Bulk soil plant 53 

compartment refers to the area of soil around a plant and this area acts as a pool of potential 54 

microbiota for other areas of the plant (Ling et al., 2022). Microbial community members from 55 

this pool are selectively recruited for colonization into the root structure (often referred to as the 56 

rhizosphere) and are known to interact synergistically with the host to contribute to plant growth, 57 

mediation of phytohormone production, and priming of plant immune defenses (Kumar et al., 58 

2018). Beyond the rhizosphere, the root endosphere refers to the space within the root tissue of a 59 

plant that is colonized by a highly specific group of microbiota, either by horizontal or vertical 60 

transmission (Lengrand et al., 2024). In terms of plant-mediated microbiome assembly, selection 61 

of and colonization by specific microbes moves in an increasingly selective spatial and 62 

biochemical gradient from bulk soil to root endosphere (Kalumbilo et al., 2026). A combination 63 

of factors such as plant species, genotypes, soil types, abiotic stress, season, cultural practices, 64 

and predation can determine how plant microbial communities are assembled among specialized 65 

compartments (Guo et al., 2023). 66 

67 

There is increasing evidence that host genotype shapes the composition and assembly of the 68 

plant-associated microbiome, with downstream consequences for host performance under both 69 

biotic and abiotic stress. Model systems like Arabidopsis have shown that bacteria belonging to 70 

phylum Actinobacteria differentially colonize roots in response to cues derived from 71 

metabolically active host cells (Bulgarelli et al., 2012). In crop systems, similar genotype-72 

dependent effects have been observed. In wheat, disease resistant cultivars were consistently 73 

associated with beneficial early-colonizing rhizosphere taxa, contributing to a distinct metabolite 74 

profile that supported viral disease suppression (C. Wu et al., 2025). In maize, host genotype 75 

explained a significant amount of variation in rhizosphere microbiota even after controlling for 76 

environmental and technical factors (Peiffer et al., 2013). These genotype effects are an indirect 77

byproduct of genetically determined phenotypic traits that modify root exudate-mediated 78

81 

As previously mentioned, a central mechanistic driver of microbiome assembly is genotype-to-82 

phenotype expression of root exudate profiles, including primary and secondary metabolites such 83 

as carbohydrates, amino acids, phenolics, flavonoids, and auxins (L. Wu et al., 2023; Yang et al., 84 

2024). These compounds establish chemical gradients that drive microbial movement toward or 85 

away from host root tissues, and colonization at the root-soil interface (Jing et al., 2023). While a 86 

host’s genotype determines biosynthetic capacity for these metabolites, it is ultimately the host 87 

phenotype, shaped by developmental state and environmental context, that defines the quantity 88 

and composition of exudates, and consequently the microbial communities that assemble (Taiz et 89 

al., 2015). Beyond exudates, phenotypic traits such as root architecture, and immune signaling 90 

further act as filters that drive microbial assembly of symbiotic microbes, not only in rhizosphere 91 

communities but also the subset of microbes that successfully transition into the endosphere 92 

(Araujo et al., 2025). This hierarchical selection results in the enrichment of specific microbial 93 

communities with the host that can functionally integrate with host physiology and support 94 

overall host health (Brachi et al., 2022; Wagner, 2021). Thus, these microbiomes are recognized 95 

not merely as passive assemblages but as active contributors to host fitness, providing 96 

microbiome-mediated protection through mechanisms such as pathogen suppression, immune 97 

priming, and enhanced tolerance to abiotic stresses (González & Elena, 2021; Singh et al., 2023). 98 

Evidence for this functional linkage is emerging across host systems. For example, in oat ( Avena 99 

sativa ) and wheat ( Triticum aestivum ), host genotype influences microbiome assembly under 100 

water stress, likely through differential stress-induced shifts in metabolite profiles among 101 

different host genotypes that selectively enrich drought-adaptive microbes (Guo et al., 2023). 102 

These recruited microbiomes can, in turn, enhance host resilience by modulating stress-103 

responsive pathways, improving nutrient acquisition, and stabilizing root function under stress 104 

(Fadiji et al., 2023; Hawkes et al., 2020; Pérez-Izquierdo et al., 2019). Therefore, we deduce that 105 

abiotic or biotic stress tolerance in plants can be viewed as emergent property of plant-106 

microbiome holobiont. 107 

108 

Auburn University’s peanut breeding program has shifted from selection solely for high yield 109 

toward integrating physiological traits associated with drought tolerance (Zhang et al., 2022). 110 

This transition has been guided by analogous efforts in other crops, for example, improved water 111 

use efficiency (WUE) has enhanced drought resilience in wheat, while increased biological 112 

nitrogen fixation (BNF) has supported yield stability in soybean under water-limited conditions 113 

(Condon et al., 2002, 2004; Sinclair et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2022). Besides agronomical traits, 114 

evaluation of peanut drought tolerance at Auburn University has extended to the traits that 115 

include denser root systems that set deeper into the soil, transpiration efficiency (TE) while 116 

(sustaining photosynthetic capabilities), improved biological nitrogen fixation (BNF), harvest 117 

index, and overall biomass accumulation (Zhang, 2021). Deeper root systems and improved TE 118 

are identified as the most studied traits of drought tolerance in peanut. This is due to their 119 

relevance in addressing water availability which remain one of the largest abiotic stressors 120 

influencing peanut yield (Zhang, 2021). 121

Variation in TE among peanut genotypes is largely mediated through stomatal regulation (Zhang 123 

et al., 2022). Under drought conditions, tighter stomatal control reduces transpiration but also 124 

limits carbon-di-oxide diffusion to photosynthetic sites (Tardieu et al., 2018). Genotypes that 125 

adopt this conservative water-use strategy are classified as “isohydric” or “water-saver” 126 

genotypes (Zhang et al., 2022). Physiological mechanisms of water-saver genotypes are 127 

associated with chemical and hydraulic signaling which are thought to be influenced by gradients 128 

of the phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA) (Tardieu et al., 2018). Notably, root-associated 129 

microbiomes are known to play a crucial role in driving change in root exudate and 130 

phytohormone production suggesting a potential microbial contribution to water-saver 131 

phenotypes (Sharma et al., 2025; Tardieu et al., 2018). 132 

133 

However, the effectiveness of water-saver strategies is context dependent, with the most benefits 134 

seen in dry seasonal environments with longer periods of intense drought. In regions such as the 135 

Southeastern United States, where drought is typically intermittent and rainfall remains possible, 136 

selecting genotypes with highly regulated stomatal control may lead to decreased yield output 137 

(Zhang et al., 2022). In these environments, these “water-spender” genotypes that utilize deeper 138 

root systems with dense root architecture may be more advantageous to combat issues of water 139 

availability by i) reaching larger areas of sporadically moist soil and ii) harboring the required 140 

biomass to retain moisture (Zhang et al., 2022). Given the critical role of the rhizosphere 141 

microbiome in regulating root function, including phytohormone modulation, nutrient 142 

acquisition, and stress priming, understanding microbial community composition in water-143 

spender genotypes is essential for evaluating their functional potential under variable drought 144 

conditions. Knowing the crucial role that rhizosphere microbiota has on the functionality and 145 

efficiency of root systems, investigation into the microbial community that is present in water-146 

spender peanut genotypes are particularly necessary. Water-saver and water-spender phenotypes 147 

represent contrasting drought-adaptive strategies that provide a useful framework for 148 

investigating the biological mechanisms underlying drought resilience. 149 

150 

We therefore hypothesized that peanut genotypes exhibiting distinct drought response strategies 151 

would harbor unique root-associated microbial communities. Because microbiome composition 152 

can be influenced by host genetic background, we included two genotypes within each drought-153 

response category (water-saver, water-spender, and drought sensitive) to minimize the influence 154 

of any single genotype on observed community patterns. The water-saver genotypes (Line 8 and 155 

AU-16-28) share a common parental lineage but originated from different breeding crosses, 156 

whereas the water-spender genotypes (AU-NPL-17 and PI-502120) represent more genetically 157 

distinct backgrounds with PI-502120 being a drought tolerant Peruvian landrace accession. We 158 

reasoned that microbial taxa or community features consistently enriched across genotypes 159 

exhibiting the same drought-response phenotype would provide preliminary evidence for 160 

phenotype-associated microbial assembly, whereas taxa restricted to individual genotypes would 161 

more likely reflect genotype-specific effects. This framework enabled us to evaluate whether 162 

microbiome assembly patterns are linked to drought-response categories rather than individual 163 

host genotypes. As resistant plant breeding moves toward integrated forms of plant resistance 164 

(genetically resistant genotypes paired with sustainable, non-chemical pest and pathogen 165 

controls), it is becoming mandatory to acknowledge the plant-associated microbiome’s role in 166 

determining the success of resistance traits. 167

Methods 169 

170 

In this study, six genotypes of Line 8, AU-NPL 17, AU16-28, PI502120, PI390428, and AP-3 171 

were planted in Auburn University Wiregrass Research and Education Station (AAES) at 172 

Headland, AL. Six peanut genotypes were selected from three unique phenotypic classifications 173 

of drought tolerance (water-saver, water-spender, and sensitive) from Auburn University’s peanut 174 

breeding program (Table 1). The experiment plots were two-row plot of 6.10 meters long and 91 175 

cm space between row and the experiment design was randomized complete block design with 3 176 

replications. The seeds were planted on May 22, 2024. Plants were sampled 177 

in early October 2024, 135 days after planting, which marked the final harvest period of the 178 

growing season. From each plot, three plants were randomly selected for sampling from the first 179 

genotype; these randomly selected locations determined sampling for the other five genotypes 180 

(Fig. S3). The samples present in this study are from 6 different genotypes spanning across 3 181 

different host genotype categories watered under rain-fed and irrigated systems. Each sample 182 

was further separated by plant compartment, bulk soil, rhizosphere soil, and root endosphere 183 

tissue for microbiome profiling. In total, there were 56 samples sequenced for this study 184 

including both bacterial and fungal amplicon sequencing. There are positive controls in the form 185 

of Zymo community standards and Zymo DNA samples ( ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community 186 

DNA Standard , n.d.) and negative controls in the form of buffer extractions. 187

Sample Preparation and DNA Extraction 191 

To collect samples for each compartment, peanut plants were carefully loosened and uprooted 192 

from the soil and the intact root system was cut at the soil line and stored in sealable plastic bags 193 

at 4℃ for transport. To collect bulk soil, samples were taken from the remaining soil that 194 

separated from the plant while lifting it from the ground. This soil was roughly 4 inches deep and 195 

was collected in 5ml Eppendorf tubes to be stored at 4℃ until DNA extraction. For the 196 

separation of rhizosphere soil from the intact root tissue, a sample of root cutting was placed in a 197 

50mL falcon tube and 30-45mL of 10mM NaCl solution was added to completely cover the root 198 

tissue. Root cuttings were sonicated using the VEVOR Digital Ultrasonic Cleaner (Model: MH-199 

040S) for 5 minutes and vortexed. If any rhizosphere soil remained attached to the root tissue, 200 

another NaCl wash was performed, and the resulting pellets were combined prior to DNA 201 

extraction. After cleaning the root tissue fully, it was removed and placed in a Ziploc bag 202 

between Kimwipes to dry overnight. The remaining NaCl solution was centrifuged at 12,000 rcf 203 

for 10 min and, after decanting the supernatant, the resulting rhizosphere pellet was stored at -20 204 

℃ until DNA extraction. To perform the extractions, total microbial DNA was extracted from 205

Data Preparation 233 

After receiving raw FASTQ files back from the MARS sequencing facility, raw paired-end 234 

Illumina reads were downloaded and input into Alabama ASAX HPC supercomputer for data 235 

processing. Adapter trimming was performed and primers were removed with BBduk (Bushnell, 236 

2014) and bases with a Phred quality score of <20 were removed. Before quality filtering and 237 

assessments were performed, the bulk soil, rhizosphere, and root endosphere reads were 238 

separated for easier processing in HPC. Quality checks were performed using FastQC (Andrews, 239 

n.d.) where the quality of bases was checked and singletons were removed and validated which 240 

also assessed successful removal of primer sequences. Error models were generated and quality 241 

was assessed using DADA2 v1.40.0 (Callahan et al., 2016). Prior to ASV inference, the bulk 242 

soil, rhizosphere, and root endosphere samples were re-merged and processed together until later 243 

downstream statistical analyses of plant compartments. ASV inference was performed using 244 

DADA2; paired-end reads were imported and denoised, dereplicated, and re-replicated. The 245 

reads were then merged and chimeric sequences were removed. After ASV inference, taxonomic 246 

classification was assigned using the DECIPHER package and DADA2. For the bacterial dataset, 247 

the SILVA SSU r138 (2019) database was used as the taxonomic reference. For the fungal 248 

dataset, the UNITE sh_general_release_dynamic (19.02.2025) dataset was used. Negative 249 

(buffer) controls from all extraction protocols were sequenced to assess potential contamination 250 

and were excluded from downstream analyses after the lack of amplified microbial reads was 251

Alpha diversity (diversity within a sample) of the bacterial and fungal communities was 264 

calculated from amplicon sequence variant (ASV) tables using the estimate_richness function in 265 

phyloseq package v1.56.0 in R (McMurdie & Holmes, 2013). These diversity metrics were 266 

assessed through Shannon Index (H) and Chao 1 richness using the phyloseq package. Beta 267 

diversity (dissimilarity between samples) was assessed through the vegan package v2.7.5 268 

(Oksanen et al., 2026). Beta diversity was assessed using both phylogenetic and non-269 

phylogenetic distance metrics to capture the phylogenetic nuance of any potential community 270 

variation. Phylogenetic beta diversity was calculated using unweighted UniFrac distances, which 271 

takes phylogenetic information between taxa into account while incorporating presence–absence 272 

scores (Fukuyama et al., 2012). In addition, non-phylogenetic compositional dissimilarities were 273 

calculated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity to assess abundance-based diversity dissimilarities 274 

(IMPACTT investigators, 2022). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was performed with the 275 

vegan package to visualize patterns of community dissimilarity patterns seen in beta diversity 276 

analyses. To assess how specific host variables affected microbial assembly patterns, a 277 

constrained principal coordinates analysis (CAP) was performed using unweighted Unifrac and 278 

Bray-Curtis distance metrics. The ordination was computed by using the model formula: 279 

Host_Phenotype + Host_Genotype + Replicate. Prior to ordination and beta-diversity analyses, 280 

phylogenetic trees associated with each dataset were assessed to ensure consistent rooting. For 281 

each rarefied phyloseq object that was separated earlier in the analysis (bulk soil, rhizosphere, 282 

and endosphere compartment samples), the phylogenetic tree was midpoint-rooted using 283 

phyloseq package. All further statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted in R, and a 284 

fixed random seed was set prior to the phyloseq rooting step to ensure reproducibility. 285

genotype, and plant compartments were evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of 293 

variance (PERMANOVA), 9999 permutations) which was conducted using the vegan package in 294 

R (Anderson, 2001). To confirm the validity of significant PERMANOVA results, homogeneity 295

Differential abundance analyses were performed separately for bulk soil, rhizosphere, and root 309 

endosphere microbial communities using the DESeq2 v1.52.0 package in R (Love et al., 2014). 310 

Unrarefied ASV count tables derived from phyloseq objects were used as input and host 311 

phenotype was used as the explanatory variable. In this study, DESeq2 was used to as a model to 312 

calculate the overall difference in taxa enrichment between host phenotype groups using 313 

generalized linear models with Wald significance tests. P-values were adjusted for multiple 314 

testing using standard Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Due to 315 

sparsity of taxa in the root endosphere because of sequencing quality control, size factors were 316 

estimated using the “poscounts” to exclude taxa that only contained zero reads. For all 317 

compartments and phenotype groupings, taxa with adjusted p-values below 0.05 were considered 318 

significantly differentially abundant (Love et al., 2014). The Sloan neutral community model 319 

(NCM) was used to evaluate the potential importance of stochastic processes to microbial 320 

community assembly across all three plant compartments (Sloan et al., 2006). 321

Discussion 656 

657 

Drought-response phenotype is associated with reproducible microbiome variation across 658 

peanut genotypes 659 

Host genotype is a recognized determinant of plant microbiome assembly, although its effects are 660 

intertwined with environmental conditions and host functional traits (Bulgarelli et al. 2013; 661 

Walters et al. 2018; Bergelson et al. 2019). In this study, we sought to determine whether peanut 662 

genotypes exhibiting contrasting drought-response strategies harbor distinct microbial 663 

communities and whether these patterns are detectable across multiple genetic backgrounds. 664 

Consistent with our hypothesis, both drought-response phenotype and host genotype were 665 

associated with variation in microbial community structure, particularly within the bulk soil 666 

compartment. Importantly, phenotype-associated patterns remained detectable despite underlying 667 

genetic variation among genotypes, suggesting that host traits linked to drought response 668 

contribute to microbiome assembly beyond individual genotype effects. Several studies have 669 

demonstrated that plant-associated microbiomes are influenced not only by host taxonomic 670 

identity but also by host functional traits (Fitzpatrick et al. 2018; Trivedi et al. 2020; Deng et al. 671 

2021). Our findings extend these observations to peanut by showing that microbial community 672 

differences were associated with water-saver, water-spender, and drought-sensitive host 673 

categories under non-stress conditions. Our findings suggest that drought-response phenotypes 674 

act as distinct ecological niches capable of supporting different microbial assemblages. Notably, 675 

these associations were detected under field conditions, and across multiple genetic backgrounds, 676 

indicating that host traits linked to drought adaptation contribute to microbiome assembly under 677 

agronomically realistic conditions. 678

Phenotype-associated signals persist despite host genetic background effects 680 

A central challenge in plant microbiome studies is disentangling host phenotype effects from 681 

underlying genetic background. To address this issue, we included two genotypes within each 682 

drought-response category and explicitly evaluated genotype and phenotype effects. Separate 683 

PERMANOVA analyses indicated that both factors significantly influenced bulk soil bacterial 684 

and fungal community composition, with genotype often explaining a larger proportion of 685 

variation. However, nested PERMANOVA provided additional insight. The nested 686 

PERMANOVA revealed a significant phenotype effect but no significant genotype-within-687 

phenotype effect, suggesting that bacterial community differences observed in bulk soil were 688 

associated with drought-response categories across genetic backgrounds. While the limited 689 

number of genotypes representing each phenotype category reduced the power to detect 690 

genotype-within-phenotype effects, these results indicate that microbial community differences 691 

were more consistently associated with drought-response categories. Similar observations have 692 

been reported in maize, rice, Arabidopsis , and sorghum, where shared host functional 693 

characteristics can shape microbial community assembly across genetically distinct accessions 694 

(Edwards et al. 2015; Walters et al. 2018; Wagner et al. 2016; Deng et al. 695 

2021). While additional genotypes would be needed to fully disentangle phenotype and genetic 696

Bulk soil exhibited the strongest phenotype-associated microbial patterns 720 

One of the more unexpected findings was that the strongest phenotype- and genotype-associated 721 

microbial patterns occurred in the bulk soil compartment. Classical models of plant microbiome 722 

assembly predict stronger host effects in the rhizosphere and endosphere habitats because these 723 

compartments experience increasing levels of host filtering (Bulgarelli et al. 2013; Compant et 724 

al. 2019). However, both bacterial and fungal communities exhibited their strongest phenotype-725 

and genotype-associated shifts in bulk soil, whereas host-associated effects were generally 726 

weaker in rhizosphere and endosphere communities. 727 

728 

Bulk soil functions as a highly diverse “reservoir” of recruitable microbes for the rhizosphere 729 

and root endosphere compartments. Consequently, shifts within this compartment can influence 730 

downstream colonization process closer to the root. A potential explanation for the strong 731 

signature observed in the bulk soil is that long-term interactions between plants and soil generate 732 

plant-mediated legacy effects that extend beyond the immediate root zone. Differences in root 733 

growth and rhizodeposition can progressively alter bulk soil microbial communities over time 734 

(de Vries et al. 2020). The stronger phenotype-associates signal in bulk soil may therefore reflect 735 

cumulative effects of contrasting drought-response strategies on soil microbial habitats rather 736 

than direct root selection alone. Furthermore, Bray-Curtis distances generally produced stronger 737 

phenotypic group separation than unweighted UniFrac distances across both bacterial and fungal 738 

communities. This pattern suggests that differences among drought-response categories were 739 

driven primarily by shifts in relative abundance of existing taxa rather than wholesale turnover of 740 

phylogenetically distinct lineages. Such abundance-driven filtering is consistent with ecological 741

Conclusion 804 

805 

This study demonstrates that peanut drought-response phenotypes are associated with 806 

reproducible patterns of bacterial and fungal community assembly across multiple host genetic 807 

backgrounds, even in the absence of drought stress under field conditions. Although host 808 

genotype contributed substantially to microbiome variation, phenotype-associated signal 809 

remained evident, particularly within bulk-soil communities and root-associated fungal 810 

assemblages. These findings suggest that plant functional traits linked to drought adaptations 811 

such as root architecture, water-use strategies, carbon allocation, and potentially root exudation 812 

patterns, shape microbiome assembly prior to the onset of stress. Stronger abundance-based 813 

responses relative to phylogenetic turnover further suggest that drought-response phenotypes 814 

primarily influence the relative abundance of existing microbial taxa rather than recruiting 815 

entirely distinct microbial lineages. 816 

817 

From a breeding perspective, these results highlight the potential for drought-adaptive 818 

phenotypes to influence plant physiology and composition of associated microbial communities. 819 

As efforts increasingly integrate host genetics and microbiome-based approaches to improve 820 

crop resilience, understanding the mechanisms underlying phenotype-associated microbiome 821 

assembly will become increasingly important. Future studies combining host genomics, root 822 

phenotyping, exudate metabolomics, and microbiome manipulation experiments will be 823 

necessary to determine whether the microbial signatures identified here are consequences of 824 

drought-adaptive traits or active contribution to drought resilience in peanut. Screening of 825 

differentially enriched fungal taxa for root exudate modulation and assessing the biological and 826 

chemical changes to the host environment will establish further insight into the role that these 827 

taxa might play in enhancing the overall drought-resistance mechanisms of specialized peanut 828 

genotypes. The findings from this work highlight the potential for integrating microbiome-829 

informed approaches into future drought-resilience breeding programs. 830

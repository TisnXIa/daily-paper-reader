impractical , creating a need for reliabl e software that can reproducibly assign viral contigs 23

dataset s, open -source softwar e, novel approaches , and recommendations to benchmark 30 

computational taxonom ic classification of viru ses , and support or ganiz ing the many vir uses 31

# Introduction 34 

Viruses dominate Earth's biosphere in both abundance and genetic diversity, yet no single 35 

method can completely resolve their taxonomy. Taxonomy provides far more than names: it 36 

anchors inferences about host range, replication strategies, virulence potential, and 37 

ecological roles. This information is essential for interpreting virom ics data , tracking 38 

emerging pathogens, designing phage therapies , and engineering viral vectors. There is 39 

consensus that viruses are so divers e that no single taxonomic method can be used to 40 

classify them all (Simmonds et al., 2023) . Since its inception , the International Committee 41 

for the Taxonomy of Viruses ( ICTV ) has relied on the expertise of the global community of 42 

virologists to classify viruse s into a ranked taxonomy (Gorbalenya, Krupovic, et al., 2020) .43 

This has generated a patchwork of methods and criteria that, ideally, capture the features 44 

of different viral li neages into meaning ful taxa that reflect the biology and evolutionary 45 

relationships of viruses . This information is formalized in taxonomy proposals , written by 46 

expert groups , evaluat ed and ratified by the ICTV (Siddell et al., 2023) . These documents 47 

provide evidence on how viruses within each taxon shall be classified, ideally based on 48 

specific demarcation criteria , and are available via the ICTV website at 49 

https://ictv.global/files/proposals/approved  (Black et al., 2026) .50 

Over the past decades, the exponential increase in DNA and RNA sequencing capacity and 51 

the rel ated discovery of new vir us es has brought new challenges. An important source of 52 

new viral genome sequenc es is metagenomic s, i.e., the direct sequencing of genomic 53 

material from complex samples , also referred to as viromics when viruses are targeted 54 

specifically  (Mokili et al., 2012; Simmonds et al., 2017) . Viromics involves sampling, sample 55 

preparation, and assembly , which may result in incomplete or inaccurate sequences  (Cook 56 

et al., 2024; Roux et al., 2019) . Th e resulting viral sequ ences can be broadly organized in to 57 

tens of millions of viral operational taxonomic units . Given the diverse methods that are 58 

currently used to classify viruses, it is unclear wh ich approaches can be applied to 59 

metagenome -derived sequences. While viromics is rapidly expanding our view of the 60 

virosphere, researchers turn to taxonomy for structur ing the resulting data (Dutilh et al., 61

## Creation of a virus contig dataset for the taxonomy challenge 97 

The dataset used for the ICTV C omputational Virus Taxonomy Challenge was assembled 98 

from three separate sources . First, To verify whether the classification pipelines could 99 

correctly identify officially recognized taxa, we generated a dataset of 15,900 virus contigs 100 

representing 12,9 53 species from the ICTV Virus Metadata Resource (VMR38v3; Figure 1A). 101 

This total includes 617 contigs from non -viral mobile genetic elements that are within the 102 

remit of the ICTV (viroids, viriforms, and satellites). Sec ond, to assess the performance of 103 

the pipeline s in assign ing novel viruses, we collected 840 previously uncharacterized virus 104 

contigs and invited ICTV Study Group member s to suggest their classification (Figure 1B, see 105 

Methods) . Except for two sequences associated with established caudoviricete genera 106 

(0. 2%) , these novel sequences wer e not ass ign ed t o established genera or species, but 107 

experts could assign them at the family or higher rank . The distribution across realms and 108 

kingdoms of these two datasets is shown in Figure 1C. Third, to challenge the various 109 

pipelines with unknown viruses , we included 3,151 predicted viral contig s from assembled 110 

metagenom ic sequences available in the MGnify database (Richardson et al., 2023) . The 111 

sequences were edited , as de novo sequencing and assembly commonly results in contigs 112 

with sequencing errors , or in fragmented or c ircularly permuted contigs (Figure 1 ABD ). This 113 

resulted in a dataset of 59, 673 contig s, tailored to assess different strengths and 114 

weaknesses of the contributed pipeline s. Each entry was assigned a random label, and the 115 

dataset was made available via GitHub (Supplementary Data 1) . Participants were blinded 116 

to the sequence origins, classifications, and editing status for the duration of the challenge. 117

## Description of the entries 141 

By the deadline for submission to the challenge (1st January 202 5), seventeen teams of 142 

bioinformaticians and virologists had entered a total of thirty -four separate sets of 143 

taxonomic classifica tions and made their pipelines publicly available via GitHub (Table 1). 144 

One parameter of the challenge was that participants use the Master Species List 39 version 145 

4 (MSL39v4, available at https://ictv.global/msl ) for assigning taxonomic labels , and a n146 

initial screen revealed that some of the entries had systematic encoding errors consisting of 147 

illegal string s for the taxa names at various ranks . Thus, we provide d a validation script and 148 

gave all teams the opportunity to identify and fix any issue s in their pipelines . We also 149 

encouraged the teams to improve the documentation of the ir tools in the form of dedicated 150 

“readme ” files on the respective GitHub repositorie s ( see also the Supplementary Methods 151 

contain ing extensive description s of each pip eline) . These files were a required part of the 152 

challenge and used to assess reproducibility . The final c lassifications from all teams for all 153 

contigs can be found in Supplementary Data 2.154 

Most final submission s resolved the issue of incorrect names within the ranks 155 

(Supplementary Table 2). The o ne clear outlier was CAT -ncbi , a pipeline consist ing of contig 156 

annotation tool CAT (von Meijenfeldt et al., 2019) with its default NCBI database . Although 157 

NCBI implement s ICTV taxonomy on all viruses in the database (Schoch et al., 2020) , non -158 

viral sequences are far more abundant , and includ e integrated proviruses that will be 159 

assigned to the taxonomy of their host . While CAT was proven to be efficient at classifying 160 

bacteria (Meyer et al., 2021) , in the ICTV taxonomy challenge , CAT -ncbi assigned 31.5 % of 161 

the viral sequences to non -vir al taxa , highlighting the difficulty of annotating truly un charted 162 

sequences , which might include both viral and non -viral contig s (Meyer et al., 2021) . The 163 

remaining pipelines assigned valid taxa to the ranks used to assign the sequences, and most 164 

pipelines were error -free with five pipelines having further issues (incorrect fraction :165 

1.2%±2.6 %) . A separate analysis of the comb inations of names across ranks (i.e., lineages) 166 

revealed that inconsistent lineage strings were common in ten pipeline s, when evaluated 167 

based on the MSL39v4 (incorrect fraction :15.0%±21. 6%, Supplementary Table 2). 168

## pipeline configurations 257 

One of the aims of the ICTV Computational Virus Taxonomy Challenge was to identify the 258 

strengths and weaknesses of different pipelines in the face of de novo assembled contig 259 

sequences such as may be obtained from metagenomics . To this end, sequences were 260 

edited in different ways, including the introduction of single nucleotide replacements to 261 

reflect sequencing errors or substitution mutations , fragmentation of viral genome 262 

sequences into shorter contigs, reverse complement ation , and circular permut ation of the 263 

viral sequences to mimic results from de novo assembly (Figure 1 AB D). This was used to 264 

assess how the classification accuracies of all the pipelines are impacted b y these 265 

transformations (Figure 3A , Supplementary Figure 1 , Supplementary Table 4 ). A general 266 

emerging trend was a decrease in classification accuracy as transformations and random 267 

substitutions were introduced , but these effects varied among pipelines . Some pipelines ,268 

including Metabuli , Vclust, VCAT , geNomad, and VISTA , appear ed to be resilient to such 269 

changes , wh ereas for PhaBOX and Vanjari, accuracy decreased with increasing substitution 270 

rate . Vanjari wa s strongly impacted by reverse complemen tation , an issue that has been 271 

addressed by the Vanjari team (Supplementary Methods) , and PhaBOX 2 was recently 272 

rel eased that addres s some of these limi tat ions (Shang et al., 2026) .273 

Several pipelines had high accuracy scores for genus and species classifications in the 274 

Expert dataset. As only two sequences were assigned below the family rank, these high 275 

scores reflect the fact that these pipelines did not assign most sequences at these ranks. 276

# Discussion 478 

## Classifying the virosphere 479 

Thi s first ICTV Computational Virus Taxonomy Challenge asked a n important question for 480 

modern virology : to what extent can automated, high - throughput computational tools 481 

assign unannotated viral sequences to the fifteen ranks in the ICTV virus taxonomy ? The 482 

dataset was compos ed of sequences derived from reference genomes of classified viruses 483 

(VMR dataset ), sequences of previously unclassified viruses (Expert dataset), and 484 

hypothetical vir us sequences predicted from metagenomic data (MGnify contigs) . The VMR 485 

dataset generally had high assignment rates , suggesting that most tools perform well in 486 

assign ing known viruses , wh ereas the Expert and MGnify datasets proved to be more 487 

challenging . Together, the dataset enabled a rigorous benchmarking of the strengths and 488 

limitations of current bioinformatic approaches .489 

The submitted pipelines varied g reatly on the evaluated metrics of accuracy , false discovery 490 

rate , and computational performance (time/memory) . The ability of fast tools with low o r491 

configurable memory requirement s, such as Vclust, Sourmash, Metabuli, and VCAT , to 492 

accurately assign known virus sequences , even when fragmented or mutated (VMR dataset), 493 

suggests immediate utility in clinical and environmental surveillance pipelines or rapid 494 

detection of known pathogens. Pipelines such as geNomad, MMSeqs2 -2, Metabuli, PhaB OX ,495 

PhaGCN Cluster , vConTACT2, and VCAT maintained accuracy for the Expert dataset , albeit 496 

with variable sensitivity , supporting their use for characterizing sequences from unexplored 497 

viromes . As these viruses await official classification at lower taxonomic ranks , the 498 

sequences from the Expert dataset were more accurately assign ed at high er ranks starting 499 

at the family rank . VirTaxon uses a hybrid approach that integrates alignment -based and 500 

deep -learning methods, resulting in high accuracy at the family and order ranks within the 501 

Expert dataset. 502 

The assignment gap between known and novel viruses remains substantial. While a 503 

significant fraction of unknown sequences from the MGnify dataset could be consistently 504 

placed into established families, many also remained unassigned . The se sequences may 505

represent deep -branching taxa whose affiliation is unresolved by the current ICTV 506 

framework. Also , the sequences in the MGnify dataset were predicted to be virus -derived, 507 

but they may not be. In metagenomic datasets, a common source of mis assignment 508 

consists of human, bacterial, and other host -derived sequence s, whic h may be spuriously 509 

interpret ed as viral . An important caveat in the design of this first Virus Taxonomy Challenge 510 

was that we assumed that all sequences were derived from viruses, which made it possible 511 

for tools such as VCAT to utilize a small, targeted reference database consisting only of 512 

sequences belonging to ICTV -classified viruses. When a full database is used, 513 

contaminating sequences from hosts or other cellular organisms may be more readily 514 

detected, but it a lso complicates the classification of virus sequences, which may be 515 

spuriously a nnotated. This is illustrated by the CAT -ncbi results , since this pipeline use d the 516 

full NCBI non -redundant protein database and did not attempt to filter out non -viral hits .517 

## Technical considerations 518 

More often than expected, pipeline results included illegal labels (e.g., “ Flaviviridae -like”, 519 

“Cluster 19”) and invalid combinations of labels along the taxonomic lineage (e.g., a genus 520 

assigned to the wrong family) . Such anomalies have a direct negative impact on accuracy 521 

but are easy to prevent, as they can be automatically detected . Another limitation that 522 

became apparent during the evaluation of the pipelines is relat ed to the progressing nature 523 

of virus taxonomy , and its yearly assessment by experts . Taxon names at different ranks may 524 

change, e.g. , they may be abolished (removed entirely), renamed (given a new name), 525 

demoted (moved to a lower rank), promoted (moved to a higher rank) , split into two or more 526 

taxa, or merged (Simmonds et al., 2025) . Ideally, a classification pipeline would allow user s527 

to specify which MSL version to use , e.g. , by implementing a command line parameter such 528 

as “--msl_version 39v 4” or “ --msl_version latest” , which would make it more sustainably 529 

applicable . Currently, VCAT is the only pipeline that enable s users to choose between 530 

MLS39 or MSL40 with the “--dbversion ” option. Automatically updating the reference 531 

database would benefit greatly from a single access point for download ing all ICTV -532 

classified sequences (i.e. , those listed in the VMR or MSL) , which is possible in the recently 533 

published VirJenD B database (Saghaei et al., 2026) .534

A critical challenge fo r automated virus classification is th e inconsistency , and sometimes 535 

even the absence , of species and genus demarcation criteria , even for ICTV -ratified taxa 536 

(Box 1AB) . One of the premises of th is Taxonomy Challenge was that viruses are so diverse 537 

that no single taxonomic method can be used to classify them all (Simmonds et al., 2023) .538 

However, only few of the pipelines were biased for specific families or realms , even though 539 

they used very different computational methodologies (Extended Methods) . This holds 540 

promise for computational virus taxonom ic classification and suggests that diverse virus 541 

sequences may be confidently assigned with a single approach . Some ICTV Subcommittees 542 

and Expert Groups have already adopted genome -based criteria that standardize 543 

demarcation of low -rank taxa , supported by evolutionary analyses  (Chiumenti et al., 2021; 544 

Deng et al., 2022; Turner et al., 2021; Van Doorslaer, 2022) . As the virus taxonomy remains 545 

a living project that depends on the contributions of exper ts around the world who feed it 546 

with new insights, we hope that the findings described above , including any inconsistencies 547 

between the pipeline assignments and the ICTV taxonomy , will inspire these experts to dig 548 

deeper an d unravel the underlying causes and patterns , further improv ing the taxonom y.549 

## Outlook 550 

In t he ICTV Taxonomy Challenge , the focus was to assess the assignm ent of unlabeled virus 551 

sequences into the established ICTV taxonomy using computational methods. F uture 552 

studies might also assess the potential of computational tools to suggest novel taxa at all 553 

ranks , or even the need for additional ranks. An important direction for future computational 554 

research is thus to shift from assign ing sequences to established tax a to propos ing and 555 

delineat ing novel taxa. Integration of reproducible, data -driven computational methods is 556 

essential to transition the virus taxonomy into a sustainable platform that can scale with the 557 

rate of virus discovery. As the availability of complete genome sequences is a main criterion 558 

for approval of a taxonomy proposal by the ICTV, completeness evaluation would be a 559 

critical aspect of such tools. 560 

An important finding of our benchmarking study is the observation that for most tools, 561 

viruses were more accurately classified in large families with many reference sequences 562

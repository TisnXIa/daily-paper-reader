Abstract 20 

Environmental change exposes ecosystems, including host-associated microbiomes, to 21 

stressors that occur repeatedly and in sequence, yet it remains unclear whether prior stressor 22 

history conditions host-microbiome responses to later perturbation. We used adult zebrafish 23 

(Danio rerio ) to test whether sequential exposure to antibiotics, heat stress, the intestinal 24 

nematode Pseudocapillaria tomentosa , or pairwise stressor combinations altered gut 25 

microbiome structure, intestinal host gene expression, and host health outcomes. Across eight 26 

exposure regimes, prior stressor history and parasite exposure were associated with gut 27 

microbiome composition, while increasing prior stressor history was associated with reduced 28 

gut microbial diversity and convergence in community composition. Host intestinal 29 

transcriptional responses to parasite exposure were historically contingent, with parasite-30 

associated differential gene expression varying non-linearly across prior stressor histories. 31 

Cumulative mortality increased with prior stressor history, whereas infection prevalence among 32 

surviving hosts decreased. Integrating microbial abundance, host gene expression, mortality, 33 

and neutral-community modeling identified Cetobacterium , Culicoidibacter , Flavobacterium ,34 

and Shewanella as candidate host-linked taxa associated with host response and survival. 35 

Collectively, these findings indicate that prior environmental stressor history shapes vertebrate 36 

host-microbiome responses to future perturbation and highlight specific gut microbial members 37 

as potential biomarkers or functional targets for follow-up studies. 38

Introduction 40 

The Anthropocene is accelerating environmental change, exposing ecosystems and host-41 

associated communities to stressors that increasingly co-occur and recur [1–3]. Because 42 

stressors can interact synergistically, antagonistically, or additively, multi-stressor responses 43 

are often difficult to predict from single-stressor studies, and empirical syntheses show that 44 

stressor suites frequently depart from additivity [4–6]. Climate-linked drivers are expected to 45 

increase the repetition and sequencing of perturbations, including in host-microbiome systems 46 

[7, 8]. Yet most multi-stressor work emphasizes concurrent exposures, leaving the sequence 47 

and accumulation of prior perturbations, and how they shape host-associated microbiomes 48 

and host responses to a later biotic challenge, largely untested [9]. Resolving this is central to 49 

predicting the resilience of host-associated microbial ecosystems to anthropogenic stress [10]. 50 

Ecological theory offers a framework. Historical contingency predicts that the order and timing 51 

of past events shape community structure and function [11, 12], and priority effects can amplify 52 

these legacies when early perturbations alter the establishment of later organisms through 53 

niche preemption or modification [13, 14], steering communities toward divergent ecological 54 

states [15]. Host-associated microbiomes are a tractable system for testing how assembly 55 

history shapes community and host responses under environmental change [16, 17], and 56 

across hosts both stressor combination and cumulative stress history can shape host fitness 57 

and microbiome-associated phenotypes [18, 19]. Building on this theory and evidence, we 58 

hypothesize that historical stressors act as assembly filters that modify host-microbiome 59 

structure and function and thereby condition responses to future disturbance. 60 

We tested this in the zebrafish ( Danio rerio ), a vertebrate model well suited to controlled, 61 

longitudinal, multifactorial designs that manipulate stressor order and whose gut microbiota are 62 

experimentally accessible and environmentally responsive [20, 21]. Prior work links priority 63 

effects and stressor-driven filtering to microbiome assembly and host-relevant outcomes in this 64 

system [22], and work from our group shows that abiotic and biotic factors, including warmer 65 

rearing temperatures and intestinal nematode exposure, shift gut microbiota composition and 66 

assembly and associate with divergent host phenotypes [23–25]. This foundation enables a 67 

direct test of whether successive stressor exposure conditions the host-microbiome response 68 

to a future perturbation. 69 

Here, we asked whether successive stressor history conditions zebrafish host-microbiome 70 

responses to a later perturbation, with three predictions: that prior antibiotic and thermal 71 

exposure act as assembly filters, producing differences in gut community composition, 72 

diversity, and dispersion across stressor histories; that distinct histories alter the intestinal 73 

transcriptional response to a subsequent parasite challenge; and that cumulative mortality and 74 

infection prevalence among survivors both increase with prior stressor history. We reared 720 75 

adult zebrafish across eight regimes spanning all combinations of antibiotic exposure, heat 76 

stress, and challenge with the intestinal nematode Pseudocapillaria tomentosa , applied 77

Gut microbiota community displays historically contingent sensitivity in response to 95 

parasite exposure 96 

We first characterized the gut microbiota at day 60 across the eight exposure regimes and 97 

prior stressor history levels (Figure 1; Table 1). Differential abundance analysis (MaAsLin3) 98 

identified 12 genera that differed from the control regime (A- T- P-) in at least one regime and 99 

11 genera associated with a linear prior-stressor-history contrast (Benjamini-Hochberg q < 0.1; 100 

Tables S3.9.2, S3.9.7). Core zebrafish gut taxa were consistently present, with the most 101 

prevalent genera including Aeromonas , Cetobacterium , Pseudomonas , Culicoidibacter ,102 

Shewanella , and an unclassified Paracoccaceae genus (Figure 2A). Gut microbial diversity 103 

tracked prior stressor history more than parasite exposure. In factorial models, temperature 104 

was associated with Shannon and Simpson diversity, and an antibiotic-by-temperature 105 

interaction with observed richness (Table S1.1.1). Across the prior-stressor-history gradient, 106 

Simpson diversity declined significantly, Shannon showed a consistent but non-significant 107 

decline, and richness showed no trend (Table S1.5.1; Figure 2B). Parasite exposure did not 108 

significantly alter any diversity metric within a stressor-history level after correction (Table 109 

S1.2.1). Community composition was shaped by both factors. PERMANOVA associated prior 110 

stressor history and parasite exposure with composition under both Bray-Curtis and Canberra 111 

distances, with a significant history-by-parasite interaction only for Canberra (Table S2.9.1; 112 

Figure 2C). Beta dispersion also differed significantly across stressor-history levels (Table 113 

S2.3.1; Figure 2D). Because dispersion heterogeneity can itself drive PERMANOVA, we 114 

interpret prior stressor history as associated with both community location and dispersion, 115 

parasite exposure as associated with compositional shifts, and the history-dependent parasite 116 

effect as metric-dependent. Together, gut microbiota sensitivity to parasite exposure was 117 

shaped by prior stressor history. 118

Host gene expression shows non-additive response to parasite exposure 122 

We next profiled intestinal host transcription across regimes at day 60. Because 123 

Pseudocapillaria tomentosa burrows into the intestinal lining, we expected parasite exposure 124 

to elevate differentially expressed gene (DEG) counts, and predicted that this response would 125 

scale linearly with prior stressor history. As expected, parasite-exposed fish showed far more 126 

DEGs than unexposed fish relative to controls (averaging 5,338 versus 78 DEGs; Benjamini-127 

Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05; Figure 3; Table S4.3.1). Counter to prediction, however, DEG 128 

counts did not scale linearly: the response peaked at one prior stressor rather than two, 129 

whether quantified per regime (one-stressor parasite regimes averaged 6,283 DEGs versus 130 

4,964 at two prior stressors; Table S4.3.1) or by pooled stressor-history group (9,188 DEGs at 131 

one versus 4,440 and 5,871 at zero and two; Table S4.2.1). Thus prior stressor exposure 132 

modifies the host transcriptional response to infection non-additively, with the largest response 133 

at intermediate stressor history. 134

Mortality increases with cumulative stressor exposure independent of parasite 137 

exposure 138 

To test whether these patterns extended to host health, we examined mortality and infection at 139 

day 60. Because cohousing and intermediate terminal sampling preclude continuous mortality 140 

tracking, we estimated final mortality from the expected minus surviving fish per regime (about 141 

45 fish expected per regime). Cumulative mortality rose from 18.9% at zero prior stressors to 142 

31.1% at one and 55.6% at two, a nearly three-fold increase (Figure 4A), and a linear trend 143 

test confirmed that mortality increased significantly with prior stressor history (binomial GLMM 144 

with tank as a random effect; Table S5.13.1). This relationship held independent of parasite 145 

exposure, which had no significant main or interaction effect on mortality (Table S5.14.1). 146 

Infection prevalence among survivors did not increase with stressor history (83.8%, 59.7%, 147 

and 60.0% across zero to two prior stressors; Figure 4B; Table S5.8.1), and neither the linear 148 

trend nor pairwise contrasts were significant (Tables S5.5.1, S5.4.1). Mortality therefore 149 

increased with cumulative stressor history while infection prevalence did not, an unexpected 150 

dissociation we revisit below. 151

Culicoidibacter, Shewanella, Flavobacterium, and Cetobacterium abundance associates 155 

with host gene expression and mortality 156 

Building on these mortality patterns, we tested associations between genus abundance and 157 

tank-level final mortality (MaAsLin3 with tank as a random effect). Four genera, Shewanella ,158 

Culicoidibacter , Flavobacterium , and Cetobacterium , showed significant negative abundance-159 

mortality associations (Figure 5A; Tables S3.8.1, S3.9.4), indicating that higher abundance of 160 

these taxa tracked lower final mortality. 161 

We then asked whether these four genera also associated with host intestinal gene 162 

expression. Using a two-stage procedure (Spearman screening followed by nonparametric 163 

partial correlations adjusted for total worm burden, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control; 164 

Table S6.6.1), each of the four carried at least one significant gene edge. Their profiles 165 

differed: Culicoidibacter had few but the strongest edges (mean absolute partial correlation 166 

0.744, nearly all positive), Flavobacterium the most edges (83, nearly all negative), and 167 

Shewanella and Cetobacterium intermediate numbers of comparatively strong edges (Figure 168 

5B; Tables S6.5.1–S6.5.4). No host gene was shared among the four (Table S6.4.1). These 169 

genera were therefore consistently associated with both host gene expression and final 170 

mortality. 171 

To identify host functional themes linked to each genus, we tested KEGG pathway and Gene 172 

Ontology over-representation on each genus’s significant partial-correlation gene list (Figure 173 

5C; Tables S7.36.1–S7.39.1 for KEGG; Tables S7.17.1, S7.24.1, and S7.31.1 for GO). The 174 

four genera nominated largely distinct programs: Flavobacterium -linked genes were enriched 175 

for proteasome function, Culicoidibacter -linked genes for xenobiotic and drug metabolism via 176 

cytochrome P450, cofactor biosynthesis, and related metabolic pathways, and Shewanella -177 

and Cetobacterium -linked genes for structural and signaling themes such as motor proteins, 178 

cytoskeleton, and integrin signaling. Despite non-overlapping input gene sets, each genus 179 

mapped to partitioned host functional themes rather than a shared program, consistent with 180 

distinct host-microbe relationships among the four taxa. 181

Culicoidibacter, Shewanella, Flavobacterium, and Cetobacterium genera appear below 185

Finally, we asked whether the abundances of these four genera reflected neutral processes 187 

(random dispersal, drift) or deterministic selection. We fit a Sloan neutral model to genus 188 

occurrence frequency versus metacommunity relative abundance at day 60 (Figure 6; Tables 189 

S8.6.2, S8.7.2, S8.8.2, and S8.9.2). All four focal genera fell below the neutral prediction, 190 

indicating that neutral processes alone cannot explain their distributions and that each 191 

occurred less frequently than expected for its abundance, consistent with being selected 192 

against. Together with their associations with mortality and host gene expression, these 193 

deviations provide a further line of evidence that Culicoidibacter , Shewanella , Flavobacterium ,194 

and Cetobacterium track biologically meaningful host-microbiome variation. Collectively, the 195 

gut microbiota responded to perturbation in a manner contingent on prior stress history, and 196 

these taxa may help mediate host outcomes within that historical context. 197

Discussion 200 

Ecosystems and the organisms that inhabit them face increasing environmental stressors that 201 

challenge their resiliency. Ecological theories of historical contingency and priority effects 202 

predict that an ecosystem’s prior stressor exposure, by altering community structure and 203 

function, shapes its response to future perturbations [12, 14], yet how strongly this applies to 204 

vertebrate host-microbiome systems remains unexplored [9, 29]. Here, we asked whether 205 

successive abiotic stressors (antibiotics and heat) restructure the zebrafish gut microbiome 206 

and condition host intestinal transcription and health outcomes to a subsequent biotic stressor 207 

(intestinal parasites). 208 

Prior multi-stressor research spans many environments and host-associated systems [5, 30– 209 

33]; our study extends it by asking how multiple successive historical stressors shape a 210 

vertebrate host-microbiome system’s response to a later perturbation. We found that 211 

cumulative stressor history shapes the endpoint structure of zebrafish gut communities, 212 

consistent with priority effects and historical contingency, in which the order and timing of 213 

colonization influence community structure and function. Each prior stressor may act as an 214 

assembly and successional filter that narrows the set of viable community configurations (the 215 

species pool) able to withstand later stressors. Consistent with host traits contributing to this 216 

filtering, Stagaman et al. found that adaptive immunity filters the zebrafish gut microbiota by 217 

reducing neutral assembly (e.g., dispersal) and increasing interindividual differences, though 218 

such effects can be sensitive to shared-environment transmission [34]. Our observation that 219 

gut diversity decreases and composition converges with more prior stressors supports this 220 

view, indicating that microbiota structuring reflects historical exposure to successive stressors, 221 

not only the number of co-occurring ones. 222 

How strongly parasite exposure registers at the community level depended on prior stressor 223 

history. Based on our earlier work with P. tomentosa [24, 35], we expected parasite exposure 224 

to produce larger differences in diversity and composition, especially in fish with more prior 225 

stressors. Instead, fish with fewer prior stressors showed greater interindividual heterogeneity, 226 

regardless of parasite exposure. This contrasts with the Anna Karenina Principle (AKP) [36], 227 

under which stressed hosts should be more variable, and with our prior work in which 228 

temperature acted as a stronger selective force and parasite exposure added stochasticity, 229 

with microbiota diverging toward multiple temperature-dependent stable states rather than an 230 

AKP-like dysbiosis [24]. The pattern instead fits a homeorhetic framework [37], in which 231 

stability reflects change along trajectories rather than return to a single static state. Two factors 232 

may explain the discrepancy with our expectations: parasite exposure here was chronic rather 233 

than single-dose, and, lacking intermediate sampling, endpoint patterns may be biased toward 234 

survivors. Thus prior stressor history conditions how parasite exposure is expressed at the 235 

community level, with endpoint structure reflecting both the final perturbation and the 236 

preceding sequence of stressors. 237

Host outcomes also tracked cumulative stress history, indicating organism-level consequences 238 

of sequential perturbation. As predicted, mortality rose with prior stressor history, consistent 239 

with work showing that multiple stressors alter host fitness [38–40] and that microbiome 240 

context shapes responses to combined stressors [19, 41]. In Daphnia , for example, the 241 

interaction between a cyanobacterial stressor and an oomycete parasite depended on 242 

microbiome inoculum type, with an antagonistic survival effect only in hosts carrying a 243 

laboratory-derived microbiome [19]. We similarly observed a non-significant antagonistic 244 

relationship in which parasite-exposed fish had lower mortality than unexposed fish of similar 245 

stressor history, though this differs from Godwin et al., who found heat stress exacerbates 246 

mortality in parasite-exposed salmon [38]. The hygiene hypothesis offers one interpretive 247 

frame, whereby limited exposure to immune-stimulating organisms can promote dysregulated 248 

immunity and vulnerability to later challenges [42, 43]. Together these results suggest multi-249 

stressor survival effects are context-dependent and non-additive, not predictable from single-250 

stressor responses. Contrary to our third prediction, infection prevalence among survivors 251 

decreased with prior stressor history. We interpret this as selective mortality: fish most 252 

vulnerable to cumulative stress and parasite exposure likely died before final sampling, leaving 253 

a more resilient surviving subset and producing an inverse mortality-infection relationship. This 254 

implies a host-level filtering effect analogous to the microbiome filtering above, in which 255 

successive stressors constrain not only which microbiota configurations persist but also which 256 

hosts survive to be assessed. 257 

Host intestinal transcription was likewise historically contingent and non-additive. Parasite-258 

associated differential gene expression (DEG) peaked in fish with one prior stressor and was 259 

lower at zero or two, so the response did not scale linearly with cumulative stress. Among 260 

parasite-unexposed fish, transcriptional divergence from the reference regime instead 261 

increased with prior stressor number, indicating that cumulative stress alone progressively 262 

altered the intestinal state, and our joint model identified genes with significant parasite-by-263 

stress-history interactions, supporting a host response that depends on prior context. Because 264 

gut microbiota and host intestinal gene expression are tightly linked through immune, 265 

metabolic, and barrier processes, and microbiome state can influence transcriptional plasticity 266 

and stress tolerance [41, 44], the non-additive response we observed may partly reflect 267 

differences in host-microbiome state across stress-history backgrounds. However, sampling 268 

transcription only at the endpoint means we cannot resolve expression trajectories or separate 269 

direct effects of cumulative stress, microbiome-associated shifts, selective mortality, and their 270 

interactions. Overall, prior stress history appears to coordinate shifts in both microbiome 271 

structure and host response, prompting us to ask which microbial taxa link these patterns to 272 

host outcomes. 273

Methods 334 

Ethical approval 335 

All animal procedures were approved by the Oregon State University Institutional Animal Care 336 

and Use Committee (IACUC protocol 2022-0280) and conducted in accordance with 337 

institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory zebrafish. 338 

Experimental design and stressor history 339 

We tested whether sequential environmental stressor history conditions host-microbiome 340 

responses to a later biotic challenge in adult zebrafish ( Danio rerio , 5D strain). Seven hundred 341 

twenty fish were distributed across 24 tanks (three replicate tanks per treatment) in a fully 342 

factorial design crossing antibiotic exposure (A+ or A-), heat stress (T+ or T-), and intestinal 343 

nematode exposure ( Pseudocapillaria tomentosa ; P+ or P-), yielding eight exposure regimes 344 

(A±T±P±). Stressors were applied sequentially over 60 days rather than concurrently, so that 345 

regimes differed in the number of prior stressors experienced before the final parasite phase 346 

(0, 1, or 2 prior stressors; Table 1). 347 

Antibiotic-exposed fish received medicated gelatin feed containing streptomycin, ciprofloxacin, 348 

and ampicillin (50 μg g ⁻¹ body weight per day) from days 0-14. Heat-stressed fish experienced 349 

a ramp from 28 °C to 35 °C between days 14-18, maintenance at 35 °C through day 25, and 350 

return to 28 °C by day 29. Parasite-exposed fish received continuous exposure to P. 351 

tomentosa eggs via flow-through effluent from infected donor tanks from days 29-60, following 352 

established Sharpton and Kent Lab helminth challenge procedures [24, 25, 28]. Control fish 353 

(A-T-P-) experienced matched handling without antibiotics, without heat ramp, and without 354 

parasite effluent. 355 

Animal husbandry 356 

Fish were reared under standard Sharpton and Kent Lab zebrafish husbandry (Supplementary 357 

Methods): recirculating aquaculture, 14:10 h light:dark cycle, and diet consistent across tanks 358 

unless otherwise noted for antibiotic delivery. Tanks were cohoused by exposure regime; 359 

individual fish were not uniquely tagged. This cohousing structure motivated endpoint-only 360 

mortality estimation and tank-level inferential models (see below). 361 

Sampling and host phenotyping 362 

Fecal and intestinal microbiota samples were collected at days 0, 14, 29, and 60 (three fish per 363 

tank per time point when available). At day 60, all remaining survivors were sampled, yielding 364 

235 fish with sufficient 16S data after quality filtering. Intestinal tissue for RNA sequencing was 365 

collected from a subset of day-60 fish with paired microbiome data (Supplementary Methods). 366 

Cumulative mortality was assessed at day 60 only. Because intermediate sampling removed 367 

fish from tanks and fish were cohoused without individual IDs, daily mortality could not be 368

16S rRNA gene sequencing and microbiome processing 388 

Gut microbiome profiling followed our published zebrafish 16S workflow (Sieler et al. 2023, 389 

2025; see Supplementary Methods for PCR, sequencing platform, DADA2 parameters, 390 

taxonomy assignment, and filtering). Briefly, V4-region amplicons were processed to amplicon 391 

sequence variants (ASVs), assigned with the SILVA reference, and aggregated to genus level 392 

for downstream analyses. Analyses presented in the main text focus on day-60 samples 393 

unless noted. 394 

RNA sequencing and differential gene expression 395 

Intestinal RNA was extracted from day-60 samples and processed with standard Illumina 396 

library preparation and paired-end sequencing (Supplementary Methods). Salmon 397 

quantification yielded gene-level counts. We conducted two complementary DESeq2 designs 398 

on filtered genes: (1) per exposure-regime contrasts versus the A-T-P- reference (eight 399 

regimes; main-text Figure 3 spine), and (2) a pooled-by-prior-stressor-history model grouping 400 

parasite-exposed fish by history level (0, 1, or 2 prior stressors) to test history-dependent 401 

transcriptional responses (Table S4.2.1). Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) 402 

were declared at Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P < 0.05. The A-T-P- reference regime was 403 

omitted from bar-chart display because it is the contrast baseline. 404

Microbiome community analyses 405 

Alpha diversity 406 

Genus-level Shannon diversity [57], inverse Simpson diversity [58], and observed genus 407 

richness were analyzed with beta regression GLMMs (glmmTMB [59]; logit link) including 408 

random intercepts for tank: y ~ HistoryLevelNum × Parasite + (1 | Tank.ID) for stress-history 409 

gradients, and factorial Antibiotics × Temperature × Parasite models with tank random effects 410 

for exposure-regime effects. Type-III Wald tests and emmeans contrasts were used for fixed 411 

effects [60, 61]; pairwise parasite contrasts within each history level received Benjamini-412 

Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction across strata [62, 63]. 413 

Compositional variation and dispersion 414 

Bray-Curtis [64] and Canberra [65] dissimilarities among genus-relative-abundance profiles 415 

were tested with PERMANOVA (distance ~ HistoryLevelNum × Parasite; 999 permutations, 416 

seed 42) and betadisper ANOVAs on group dispersions [66, 67]. Main-text models treat fish as 417 

exchangeable units under permutation (no tank stratum). 418 

Differential abundance 419 

Genus-level differential abundance at day 60 was estimated with MaAsLin3 [68] (TSS 420 

normalization, log transform) using tank random-effect models for primary manuscript tables: 421 

exposure-regime contrasts versus A-T-P-, linear prior-stressor-history trends, and parasite 422 

effects (~ Treatment + (1 | Tank.ID) or ~ HistoryLevelNum + (1 | Tank.ID) as appropriate). Cited 423 

main-text results use tank models (e.g., Tables S3.8.1, S3.9.2, S3.9.4, S3.9.7). Sensitivity 424 

models without tank random effects are retained in supplements where noted. 425 

Host-microbiome integration 426 

Taxon-mortality associations 427 

Tank-level final percent mortality was regressed on genus log ₂ abundance with MaAsLin3 [68] 428 

including (1 | Tank.ID) to identify taxa whose abundance tracked mortality (Table S3.8.1). 429 

Partial correlation networks 430 

We tested associations between genus relative abundance and host intestinal gene 431 

expression using a two-stage procedure on day-60 samples with paired RNA and 16S data. 432 

First, Spearman correlations were screened across exposure-regime pairwise contrasts versus 433 

A-T-P-; second, retained gene-taxon pairs were tested with nonparametric partial correlations 434 

(nptest::np.cor.test) adjusting for total worm count as a continuous covariate, as previously 435 

described [25]. Partial-correlation P values were FDR-controlled at 0.10 for network edges 436 

(Table S6.6.1). Worm burden was set to zero for unexposed (P-) RNA samples with missing 437 

burden (structural zeros); mortality and infection models instead use parasite exposure as a 438 

binary indicator and P+-only subsets for burden summaries, respectively (see Sampling and 439 

host phenotyping). 440

Functional enrichment 441 

For each focal genus with significant partial-correlation edges, host genes passing the partial-442 

correlation FDR threshold were tested for over-representation of KEGG pathways and Gene 443 

Ontology terms (biological process, molecular function, cellular component) using 444 

clusterProfiler (org.Dr.eg.db; organism dre). Enrichment used the submitted gene list per 445 

genus without an explicit genome-wide expression universe (Supplementary Methods); 446 

interpretation focuses on ranked terms (Figure 5C; Tables S7.36.1–S7.39.1 for KEGG; Tables 447 

S7.17.1, S7.24.1, and S7.31.1 for GO). 448 

Sloan neutral community model 449 

At day 60 we fit a Sloan neutral model relating genus occurrence frequency to metacommunity 450 

mean relative abundance [69, 70], with bootstrap confidence intervals for the neutral 451 

expectation (1,000 resamples, seed 42). Focal genera were compared to the model prediction 452 

to classify occurrence above or below neutral expectation (Figure 6; Tables S8.6.2, S8.7.2, 453 

S8.8.2, and S8.9.2). 454 

Significance testing and reporting 455 

Unless stated otherwise, two-sided tests were evaluated at α = 0.05. Multiple testing was 456 

controlled within analysis families by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR: alpha-diversity Wald and 457 

pairwise contrasts; PERMANOVA and betadisper follow-up tests where noted; MaAsLin3 458 

qval_individual < 0.05 for primary taxon screens (joint q < 0.1 reported for multi-feature 459 

MaAsLin summaries in supplements); DESeq2 adjusted P < 0.05 for DEGs; partial-correlation 460 

FDR < 0.10 for network edges (Spearman screen FDR < 0.05); enrichment adjusted P < 0.10 461 

for reporting ranked pathways. Main-text Results report effect direction and magnitude; test 462 

statistics, degrees of freedom, and exact P values appear in cited supplementary tables. 463 

Mortality trend contrast 464 

Tank-level mortality was modeled as cbind(dead, alive) ~ HistoryLevelNum + (1 | Tank.ID) 465 

(main trend) and cbind(dead, alive) ~ HistoryLevelNum × Parasite + (1 | Tank.ID) (joint test). 466 

The reported odds ratio for the history gradient is from a linear polynomial contrast on the log-467 

odds scale (emmeans::contrast(…, “poly”)) testing monotonic increase across history levels 0 468 

→ 1 → 2. This OR is not equivalent to a simple two-level marginal odds ratio between extreme 469 

groups on the response scale (Table S5.13.1). 470

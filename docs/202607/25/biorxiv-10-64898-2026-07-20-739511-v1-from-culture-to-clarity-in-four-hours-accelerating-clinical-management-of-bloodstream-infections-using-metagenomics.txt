# Abstract 19 

Background 20 

Metagenomic next -generation sequencing (mNGS ) has the potential to transform clinical 21 

diagnostics for bloodstream infections (BSIs). However, its clinical utility is currently limited by 22 

several challenges, including the extraction of DNA from blood cultures. Our aim was to develop, 23 

evaluate, and optimize an in -house method for host depletion and bacterial DNA extraction from 24 

positive blood cultures to enable rapid mNGS -based pathogen detection and antimicrobial 25 

resistance profiling, informing clinical management of BSIs. 26 

Methods 27 

151 clinical blood cultures (115 positive and 36 negative) were processed for DNA extraction 28 

using an in -house -developed SEPSINN method for host depletion and bacterial DNA extraction. 29 

mNGS on the MinION was performed, and the results for pathogen identification and 30 

antimicrobial susceptibility predictions were compared with the routine clinical workflow. 31 

SEPSINN was also evaluated against a commercial DNA extraction method to assess its 32 

effectiveness in depleting host DNA and recovering bacterial DNA. 33 

Results 34 

The SEPSINN method achieved up to 1000 -fold depletion of host DNA and outperformed the 35 

commercial DNA extraction method.  At the sample level,  mNGS  achieved 100% accuracy, 36 

specificity, and sensitivity, identifying at least one pathogen in all 115 positive blood cultures. At 37 

the pathogen level, mNGS showed 98% accuracy (120/123), specificity, and sensitivity.  For 38 

antimicrobial susceptibility predictions, mNGS achieved an accuracy of 95% (1382/1451), a 39

# Background 51 

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) and sepsis are  a global health problem , responsible for millions of 52 

deaths each year  [1,2] . In BSIs, bacteria are the  predominant  causative  agent s, accounting for 53 

around 90% of infections, while fungi, viruses, and other microorganisms  account  for roughly 10% 54 

of cases  [3] . Early diagnosis and  appropriate antibiotic administration are crucial for  reducing 55 

mortality . Current diagnostics rely on blood culture, followed by matrix -assisted laser 56 

desorption/ionization time -of -flight mass spectrometry (MALDI -TOF MS) for pathogen 57 

identification and automated or manual antibiotic susceptibility testing  [4] . However, these 58 

methods require 48 to 72 hours, which is crucial for septic patients , as every hour's delay in 59 

antimicrobial treatment decreases survival  by 7.6%  [5,6] . Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) 60

underscor ing the importance of rapid diagnostics. 62 

Molecular diagnostic methods , such as  multiplex  PCR , offer  a promising alternative 63 

because they are fast and can provide results within 1 –4 hours  after  a positive blood culture  [7] .64 

However,  multiplex PCR panels are  limited to identifying only certain species and resistance 65 

determinants  [8,9] . Metagenomic next -generation sequencing (mNGS)  can overcome the 66 

limitations of multiplex PCR panels  by identifying  pathogens  and  resistance markers in a sample 67 

without relying on predefined targets  [10,11] . Previous studies have  demonstrated  the potential of 68 

mNGS  to  detect pathogens and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in blood culture  [12 –16] .69 

Govender  et al.  reported 97% sensitivity and 94% specificity for  pathogen  identification using 70 

mNGS compared  with  routine matrix -assisted laser desorption ionization time -of -flight (MALDI -71 

TOF) diagnostics  [12] . Harris et al. have  reported  94%  accuracy  in  species identification using 72 

nanopore sequencing compared with routine diagnostics  [16] .73 

While mNGS can rapidly and accurately identify pathogens and detect ARGs in blood 74 

cultures, it faces some challenges. One of the main challenges is extracting pure  microbial  DNA, 75 

as blood cultures contain a high abundance of host cells and contaminants, including  sodium 76 

polyanethol sulfonate (SPS) , hemoglobin,  and  heme  [17 –19] . Therefore, there is an urgent need to 77 

develop a method that depletes host cells, removes inhibitors, and extracts high -quality bacterial 78 

DNA for mNGS -based workflows. Previous studies have employed selective host cell lysis with 79 

detergents such as saponin, followed by digestion with endonucleases to remove host DNA and 80 

extract bacterial DNA  [20 –23] . However, these studies were focused on clinical samples from 81 

respiratory and urinary tract infections and spiked blood cultures. In our previous study, we 82

# Methods 95 

Sample  collection and routine  microbiological  testing 96 

This study involved the collection of 151 blood cultures at the University Hospital of North 97 

Norway (UNN). It included 115 consecutive positive blood cultures from adults and 36 randomly 98 

chosen negative blood cultures. The routine diagnostic laboratory used aerobic and anaerobic 99 

BACT/ALERT ® Culture Media Bottles (bioMérieux, Marcy -l’Étoile, France) for culturing, 100 

followed by incubation in the BACT/ALERT ® VIRTUO ® system. The microbiology laboratory at 101 

UNN performed two different MALDI  analyses . The first MALDI, referred to as " initial MALDI ", 102 

is performed directly from positive blood cultures according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 103 

second MALDI, hereafter referred to as " definite MALDI ", is subsequently performed on purified 104

Host depletion and microbial DNA extraction 110 

Host depletion and microbial DNA extraction were performed using an in -house -developed 111 

method called "SEPSINN". 1 -2 mL of each blood culture sample was processed for host depletion 112 

as reported , with some modifications  [24] . The blood culture samples were centrifuged at 13000g 113 

for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded while the pellet s were processed further. 600  μL  of 114 

8% saponin and 600  μL  of HL -SAN buffer were added to obtain a final  saponin  concentration of 115 

4% , followed by the addition of 250 U of HL -SAN endonuclease  (25  U/μL, ArcticZymes 116 

Technologies, Tromsø, Norway) . The sample was gently mixed and incubated at 37  °C for 15 min 117 

with shaking at 900 revolutions per minute. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged to 118 

remove debris, and the pellet was washed with phosphate -buffered saline. Lysis of  bacterial cells 119 

was performed using a bead -beating step with lysis buffer, followed by magnetic bead -based 120 

extraction with  the  NAxtra ™ 2.0 Aquaculture NA extraction kit  (Lybe Scientific AS, Trondheim, 121 

Norway) .122

cultures ( E. coli  and  P. aeruginosa ) with and without host depletion. qPCR was performed on 125

At the sample level, true positives (TP) are those that are flagged positive by both  definite MALDI 171 

and mNGS . Similarly, true negatives (TN) were samples that were flagged negative by both. False 172 

positives (FP) were samples that were negative by  definite MALDI , but mNGS showed as positive. 173 

False negatives (FN) are samples detected as positive by  definite MALDI  but negative by mNGS .174 

A similar classification was performed at the pathogen level, with detection or non -detection 175 

considered for each pathogen rather than each sample. Similarly, for the AST predictions, any 176 

pathogen -antibiotic combination identified as susceptible (S) by both mNGS and the routine 177 

method was classified as TN, whereas those identified as resistant (R) by both methods were 178 

classified as TP. FPs were antibiotic -pathogen combinations for which mNGS showed resistance ,179 

but  the routine method showed susceptibility, and FNs were those for which the routine method 180 

showed resistance while mNGS flagged them as susceptible. 181 

To assess SEPSINN performance relative to the BiOstic kit, a Kruskal -Wallis H -test  [34] 182 

was performed on the qPCR Ct values. A post hoc analysis  – Dunn´s test  [35]  was also conducted 183 

on this data set to compare the extraction methods. The analysis was performed using GraphPad 184 

Prism (Version 10.6.1), with a  p < 0.05  considered statistically significant. 185

# Discussion 329 

In this study , we have shown that our  in -house -developed  host  depletion and DNA  extraction 330 

method  (SEPSINN) , when  combined with  real -time  nanopore sequencing  and data analysis ,331 

achieved  high accuracy  in identifying  pathogens and  profiling  their resistance  within 332 

approximately  four  hours  of  positive blood cultures.  Moreover, u sing  SEPSINN , the  pathogen and 333 

ARGs were detected  approximately one day  after  receipt of the  blood  sample  in the laboratory .334 

The method performed on par with  clinical routine diagnosis, which used MALDI (purified 335 

colonies) for pathogen I D and  disk diffusio n for AST testing . However, the  SEPSINN  mNGS 336 

method outperformed the  initial MALDI , which  wa s performed on positive blood cultures  without 337 

isolating pure bacterial colonies.  Furthermore, we used an unselective sampling process, ensuring 338 

a continuous supply of samples from clinical routine without selection bias. 339 

Blood culture is considered the gold  standard for diagnosing BSI s [36,37] . However,  isolati ng 340 

bacterial DNA from blood cultures is challenging because human cells outnumber  microbial cells ,341 

have  a larger genome size , and  contain  about  1500  times  more DNA  per cell  [38 –40] . To enhance 342 

bacterial growth and neutralize the effects of antibiotics, blood culture bottles are supplemented 343 

with various additives, such as sodium polyanetholsulfonate (SPS), gelatin, resins, and 344 

charcoal [18] . The se additives , specifically SPS, which is usually co -purified with the extracted 345 

DNA, are strong inhibitors of downstream molecular -based processes such as PCR and  mNGS 346 

[19,41] . Most previous  metagenomic  studies  have  used  commercially available kits for DNA 347 

extraction from  blood culture  [12,14,16,42 –47] . Specifically,  the BiOstic bacteremia kit is most 348

While the BiOstic  kit performed well, it lacks host DNA depletion, which is important for 350 

increasing microbial sequencing data and streamlining analysis. Host DNA depletion will be 351 

especially beneficial when the microbial load is low, particularly in polymicrobial samples where 352 

one species is less abundant than the others. Depleting host DNA is also essential for obtaining 353 

sufficient microbial mNGS data for precise AMR predictions  [49] . The SEPSINN method 354 

successfully isolated bacterial DNA from blood cultures while efficiently depleting host DNA and 355 

removing inhibitors.  Up to  100 0-fold depletion of host DNA was observed relative to non -depleted 356 

samples.  Previous studies, including ours, have reported greater fold depletion with saponin - and 357 

nuclease -based methods; however, those studies used spiked  samples , whereas this study used 358 

blood cultures from patients with BSI  [20,23,24] .359 

The  SEPSINN  mNGS  method  detected  pathogens at the species level. All  monomicrobial 360 

infections , including fungal infections,  were  identified  to the species level. Among the seven 361 

polymicrobial samples, all pathogens were detected in four, and one pathogen was detected in the 362 

remaining three.  Previous stud ies have  shown th at  nanopore sequencing can detect monomicrobial 363 

samples  at  the genus level, whereas  polymicrobial samples are challenging to identify 364 

[16,43,45,47,50] . However, most of these studies focused only on bacterial species identification; 365 

in the current study, all pathogens, including fungi, were detected. In addition, the mNGS method 366 

identified five additional pathogens compared with the  definite MADI . The identification of these 367 

additional pathogens was confirmed with qPCR using species -specific primers. A recent study 368 

reported similar results, showing that a sequencing -based method can detect additional fastidious 369 

and unculturable microbes  [12,23] .370

An important aspect of using  an mNGS  method  in  clinical diagnostics  is  its ability to predict 371 

antimicrobial sus ceptibility.  For AST predictions , our  method achieved  95% accuracy, 97% 372 

specificity,  and  88% sensitivity  across  1451 antibiotic combinations . These results showed  great er 373 

concordance  with  the phenotypic AST  than  those reported in previously published studies . Harris 374 

et al .[16]  achieved  87% accuracy, Azami et al. [43]  76%, and Gu et al. [42]  80% . A recent  study by 375 

Govender et al . reported  88% sensitivity and 93%  specificity  for AST predictions  [12] . Most of 376 

these previous studies have used the R9.4 flow cells from ONT, which rely on older chemistry and 377 

outdated basecalling and demultiplexing algorithms. However, the current study used the latest 378 

R10 chemistry, which offers a lower error rate, higher  Q score and  accuracy, and updated machine -379 

learning -based algorithms for basecalling  [51,52] . Additionally,  the current study  predicted AST 380 

using  a diverse sa mple set co mprising  aerobic and anaerobic bacteri a.  However, most  previous 381 

studies  have focused  on specific species for resistance prediction  [10,11], while others also 382 

perform whole -genome amplification before sequencing, which can introduce bias in  AMR 383 

prediction  [16,53] . Most  false -negative results were observed with amoxicillin ,384 

amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ampicillin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and 385 

piperacillin/tazobactam . False -negative results for these antibiotics in  E. coli  hav e also  been 386 

observed  previously  [23,47] . Resistance to these antibiotics usually results from multiple genetic 387 

elements, including  chromosomal mutations,  overexpression of efflux pumps , and plasmid -388 

mediated  resistance  [54] . It is difficult to detect these mutations or overexpressed efflux pumps 389 

and correlate them with the phenotypic AST, making the prediction challenging . Similarly, most 390 

false -positive  predictions were  observed  with cephalosporins for  E. coli  and  K. pneumoniae , and 391 

with amoxicillin/clavulanic acid for  S. aureus . The percentage of the bacterial genome sequenced 392 

for each species is also important for accurate AST prediction  [55] . Our method generally achieved 393

Conclusions 427 

The results  demonstrate  the effectiveness of our  in -house  developed host depletion  and  DNA 428 

extraction method ( SEPSINN ), enhanced by nanopore sequencing and real -time data analysis, for 429 

the rapid diagnosis of  BSI s in clinical settings. Overall, the method  achieved 98% pathogen 430 

identification accuracy and 95%  antibiotic susceptibility prediction accuracy, with host depletion 431 

up to 1000 -fold . This approach achieves an overall accuracy exceeding 90%, which is typically 432 

essential for implementing new diagnostic techniques in clinical microbiology  [59,60] .433 

Furthermore, these  mNGS techniques can provide deeper insights into the mechanisms of 434 

resistance.  The method took approximately 4 hours for pathogen detection and antimicrobial 435 

resistance profiling  after culture positivity . Since sepsis is time -critical, achieving a turnaround 436 

time of approximately 24 hours from sample receipt in the laboratory, and potentially even faster 437 

for aerobic blood cultures, could prevent millions of unnecessary antibiotic doses  of empirical 438 

broad -spectrum antibiotics each year before initiating personalized, targeted therapy against an 439

Abstract 15 

Soil microorganisms are crucial for plant survival and productivity, but factors governing 16 

rhizosphere recruitment across diverse regions remain unclear. This study investigated the 17 

rhizosphere microbiome of barley, using elite cultivars across seven location-year trials to 18 

evaluate the effects of environmental factors and crop genotype on bacterial and fungal 19 

community composition. Three locations were in the US northern Great Plains, and Hawai’i was 20 

used as a contrasting environment. A greenhouse reciprocal transplant study determined the 21 

relative contributions of soil physicochemical factors and soil inoculum to rhizosphere 22 

community structure. Using 16S and ITS2 amplicon sequencing, the study characterized 23 

bacterial and fungal microbiomes and assessed the contribution of environment, soil chemistry, 24 

and barley genetics to microbial community assembly. In locations within the adapted range of 25 

barley, Actinobacteriota was the dominant phylum, while Proteobacteria was dominant in 26 

Hawai’i. Variance partitioning showed that 73% of bacterial and 80% of fungal genera were 27 

associated with location-year effects while 53% of bacterial and 36% of fungal genera were 28 

responsive to soil factors. Enrichment analysis found 21.6% of bacterial and 51.4% of fungal 29 

ASVs were unique to specific barley genetic subpopulations. Results from the reciprocal 30 

transplant study validated field observations by demonstrating that 20.7% of the variation in 31 

community structure was explained by soil while 18.2% was explained by inoculum source. 32 

These findings demonstrate that environmental variation is the dominant constraint on 33 

rhizosphere community composition but within these constraints, barley genotype drives 34 

recruitment of distinct bacterial and fungal taxa. 35

1. Introduction 41 

Soil microorganisms are integral to plant productivity, contributing to nutrient cycling and stress 42 

protection (Liu et al., 2023a; Wall et al., 2015). Soil hosts plant-associated microbes, including 43 

bacteria and fungi, which interact directly and indirectly with plant roots (Chepsergon and 44 

Moleleki, 2023). These microbes enhance crop performance by improving nutrient availability 45 

and suppressing plant-pathogens (Liu et al., 2021b; Zhao et al., 2021), and thus influence the 46 

fitness of plant hosts within a given environment. Despite these impacts, the interplay of 47 

stochasticity, environmental factors, and plant genetics in shaping communities and crop 48 

productivity and sustainability remain poorly understood. 49 

The ability of a plant to recruit microbes is limited by the available soil community, 50 

which is shaped by the environment (Coller et al., 2019). Global soil surveys have revealed 51 

broad patterns of microbial composition with communities generally consisting of a few 52 

dominant groups and many rarer groups, and plants selectively recruit a subset of soil microbes 53 

to the rhizosphere, creating a distinct and less diverse community in the rhizosphere (Santoyo, 54 

2022; Walker et al., 2003). Plant genetics govern this recruitment through the production of root 55 

exudates, which induce community-level changes to filter and select specific taxa (Agler et al., 56 

2016; Banerjee et al., 2018; Berry and Widder, 2014; Bulgarelli et al., 2013). Species-specific 57 

differences in rhizosphere community recruitment have been observed, even at relatively narrow 58 

plant phylogenetic scales (Erlandson et al., 2018; Grayston et al., 1998). Recent studies have 59 

identified specific plant genes impacting the rhizosphere microbiome through molecular 60 

signaling and root exudate production (Liu et al., 2023b), and studies in rice, maize, sorghum, 61 

and switchgrass have identified plant genetic loci associated with recruitment of beneficial 62

Crop genotypes are shaped by breeding programs that prioritize traits such as yield, grain 65 

quality, and disease and herbicide resistance (Smith et al., 2020). While breeding programs favor 66 

broad adaptation, genotype performance often depends on local temperature, photoperiod, soil 67 

fertility, and other environmental constraints, so varieties can respond differently when grown 68 

outside this adapted range (Ewing et al., 2023; Lawn et al., 1995; Teixeira et al., 2015). Breeding 69 

programs focus on target production environments to ensure crop productivity within a range of 70 

environmental conditions which has resulted in widely adapted genotypes, but this adaptation is 71 

often limited to regions with similar climate conditions, soil fertility, and yield potential 72 

(Ceccarelli, 1989; Smith et al., 2020). 73 

Current evidence suggests that rhizosphere assembly reflects both deterministic processes, 74 

such as environmental and host genetics, and stochastic processes, but their relative importance 75 

remains uncertain (Dini-Andreote et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2025; Mo et al., 2024; Rain-Franco et 76 

al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024). This distinction is important because deterministic 77 

processes may structure dominant, generalist taxa, while stochasticity may be more important for 78 

rare, specialist taxa that may have large impacts on plant fitness (Li et al., 2025; Riddley et al., 79 

2025). 80 

To gain insight into the role these processes play in shaping microbial communities, we 81 

performed a common garden experiment with a population of 232 barley ( Hordeum vulgare L.) 82 

genotypes grown across contrasting soil and climatic environments, including a location outside 83 

the adapted range of the barley genotypes. We hypothesized that i) environmental conditions 84 

would be the dominant driver of barley rhizosphere microbiome structure while plant genotype 85

2.3. Greenhouse reciprocal transplant experiment 145 

To disentangle the relative contribution of soil physicochemical factors and resident soil 146 

microbiota in shaping the rhizosphere microbiome, we conducted a reciprocal transplant 147 

greenhouse experiment using contrasting soils and inoculum. This complementary experiment 148 

was performed to evaluate the contribution of soil properties and soil microbial inoculum in 149 

shaping the resulting microbial community under uniform temperature and moisture conditions. 150 

A subset of ten barley lines was selected to compare those that were well adapted or un-adapted 151 

based on their relative yield performance in the 2021 swMT and cntrMT field studies (Ewing et 152 

al., 2023). Three lines each were selected based on performance at a single location (cntrMT or 153 

swMT), and four lines were selected that performed consistently across both locations. 154

The selected barley lines were grown under four soil conditions consisting of soil and 155 

inoculant from swMT or cntrMT. Each pot contained 80% by volume of pasteurized field soil 156 

from either swMT or cntrMT to represent base soil. Soil was pasteurized by 3 repetitions of 157 

steaming at 160F for 1.5 hours. Each repetition was conducted 24 hours apart. Ten percent by 158 

volume was non-pasteurized soil which served as the microbial inoculum. This resulted in four 159 

treatments; swMT soil with smMT inoculum, swMT soil with cntrMT inoculum, cntrMT soil 160 

with cntrMT inoculum, and cntrMT soil with swMT inoculum. The final ten percent of each pot 161 

by volume contained pasteurized sand to increase water infiltration and pore space for roots. 162 

Each line and soil combination was replicated six times and pots were watered every two days. 163 

Twice a week during watering, Peters Professional 20-20-20 fertilizer G99290 (ICL, St. Louis, 164 

MO) was added to the water at a concentration of 200 ppm nitrogen. Three replicates were 165 

removed at stem elongation to collect rhizosphere samples, and three replicates were grown to 166 

maturity and dry weight of the above ground biomass was measured. Rhizosphere extractions, 167 

DNA purification, and sequencing were performed as described above in Section 2.2. Only 168 

bacterial sequencing was performed because soil mixing and the short duration of the experiment 169 

was expected to disrupt mycorrhizal associations and confound interpretation of the fungal data 170 

(Gorzelak et al., 2015). 171

2.5. Statistical Analysis 187 

The effect of location, year, genotype (barley line), and interactions between these factors, on 188 

rhizosphere community composition was assessed using permutational multivariate analysis 189 

(PERMANOVA) with 9999 permutations on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix. Differences in 190 

community composition between location-years was assessed visually using constrained analysis 191 

of principal coordinates with Bray-Curtis dissimilarities using the vegan R package (Oksanen et 192 

al., 2018) with the soil parameters nitrate, potassium, organic matter, and pH as constraints. 193

principal coordinates (CAP) using the weighted unifrac distances. Distances were constrained by 195 

base soil and inoculant, and the model was tested using PERMANOVA. A Random Forest 196 

model, implemented in the microeco (Liu et al., 2021a) R package, was used to identify taxa that 197 

were responsive to each base soil and inoculum combination (Breiman, 2001). To infer putative 198 

bacterial functions in the greenhouse study, predictive functional profiling of the bacterial 199 

community was performed using Functional Annotation of Prokaryotic Taxa (FAPROTAX) 200

2.6. Variance partitioning 209 

Variance partitioning was used to calculate the proportion of total variance (termed “variance 210 

scores”) attributed to potential explanatory factors for rhizosphere taxa as previously described in 211 

(Meier et al., 2021; Tedersoo et al., 2014). Variance partitioning was performed at the ASV level 212 

with center-log-transformed relative abundances to estimate the contribution of environment 213 

(location-year) and soil physical and chemical parameters to changes in microbiome composition 214 

in rhizosphere. We used a 5% variance partition threshold to filter meaningful factors as 215 

previously described (Meier et al., 2021). Mixed effects models were used to assess fixed 216 

quantitative effects such as soil chemistry (NO 3 -N, OM, K, and pH) while random effects 217 

included location-year, genotype, and block nested within location-year. The final linear model 218 

was 219

2.7. Ecotypes and community assembly 225 

To explore the effect of environmental factors in shaping specific components of the 226 

rhizosphere communities, we defined four ecological strategies of abundant, rare, generalist, and 227 

specialist following the method of Riddley et., al. (Riddley et al., 2025). Abundant taxa were 228 

defined as ASVs with a mean relative abundance across all samples > 0.1% which was within 229 

the outlier distribution (Fig. S2) as commonly used in other studies (Liao et al., 2017; Liu et al., 230 

2015). Rare taxa were defined as ASVs below the median of the mean relative abundance of all 231 

samples (Liao et al., 2017; Riddley et al., 2025). 232

Discussion 396 

Rhizosphere microorganisms play a fundamental role in plant nutrient acquisition, abiotic stress 397 

protection, and pathogen suppression. Understanding the drivers of recruitment and the role of 398 

abiotic and biotic factors in shaping community assembly is important for future efforts to 399 

enhance crop productivity and sustainability by identifying new targets for crop breeding and 400 

improving sustainable management strategies. By leveraging a multi-environment field study 401 

and a controlled greenhouse reciprocal transplant experiment, we disentangled the contributions 402 

of location, soil properties, the resident microbiota, and barley genotype to rhizosphere assembly. 403 

Environment exerted a greater influence on rhizosphere communities than host genotype 404 

which supports hypothesis (i). Across barley growing regions of Montana and South Dakota, 405 

bacterial communities were broadly consistent and were predominantly Actinobacteriota and 406 

Proteobacteria which is consistent with other studies (Maestre et al., 2015; Riddley et al., 2025) 407

while fungal communities were dominated by the phylum Ascomycota, consistent with global 408 

soil surveys (Egidi et al., 2019). Importantly, by including a contrasting environment outside the 409 

adapted range of barley, we demonstrated an extreme case of environmental filtering and a limit 410 

to plant recruitment of soil microorganisms, supporting hypothesis (ii) that a novel environment 411 

yields a divergent rhizosphere community. 412 

Variance partitioning analysis further quantified this hierarchy of drivers. Location-year 413 

consistently explained the largest share of variation in microbial community composition across 414 

taxa, whereas specific soil physicochemical factors (e.g. pH, nutrients) had smaller, though 415 

measurable, contribution. The strong location-year effect may have been due in part to drought 416 

conditions in the Montana locations. In swMT in 2021, May – August precipitation was 17% 417 

below the 100-year average while in cntrMT, May – August precipitation was 24% and 50% 418 

below the 100-year average in 2021 and 2022, respectively Table S1). Community assembly 419 

metrics reinforced the predominance of broad-scale stochastic processes. The phylogenetic 420 

turnover analysis ( βNTI and RC metrics) indicated that over 90% of community differences 421 

across samples were attributable to neutral or stochastic assembly (random drift and dispersal), 422 

with only a small proportion of instances showing evidence of deterministic selection. This is 423 

consistent with other studies which have shown stochastic processes dominate (Chen et al., 2026; 424 

Ma et al., 2025). For fungal communities, there was a slightly higher deterministic signal than 425 

for bacteria, yet in both cases stochastic processes were dominant. These results suggest that 426 

broad-scale climate and edaphic conditions that vary annually play a greater role in shaping 427 

community structure than deterministic soil chemistry filtering. Soil microbial communities are 428 

sensitive to changes in temperature and moisture, and these factors also alter host plant 429 

physiology (Meier et al., 2021) which may have an indirect effect on microbial recruitment. One 430

Despite the predominant influence of environmental conditions and stochastic assembly 434 

processes, we observed evidence of host genotype filtering on specific taxa, consistent with our 435 

hypothesis (i). Multiple ASVs belonging to the phyla Actinobacteriota and Proteobacteria were 436 

significantly (p < 0.05) enriched in genotype cluster 1 and depleted in cluster 3 while 437 

Bacteriodota and Nitrospirota were depleted (Fig. 4 B). Enrichment analysis identified only one 438 

fungal phylum, Basidiomycota, which was depleted in cluster 1 (Fig. 4 D). However, these 439 

genotype-associated patterns were not uniform across all environments, nor did they manifest as 440 

broad shifts in whole-community composition, as evidenced by the negligible genotype effect in 441 

overall β-diversity analyses. The significance of these genotype-specific ASVs must be 442 

interpreted cautiously since they may reflect low-abundance taxa that are strongly influenced by 443 

chance colonization events. Overall, this finding provides additional support for stochastic 444 

processes being the primary driver of community assembly. Within this context, barley genetics 445 

appear to fine-tune the microbiome by altering the prevalence of specific taxa rather than 446 

changing the overall community structure. Our findings align with a growing body of evidence 447 

that plant host effects are context-dependent and genotype-driven microbial associations often 448 

vary by environment and may be reproducible only under certain conditions. 449 

A strength of this research is the combination of field trials with a greenhouse reciprocal 450 

transplant experiment, which allowed for a more mechanistic understanding of how abiotic and 451 

biotic soil components influence rhizosphere assembly. While soil microorganisms are often 452 

considered part of the environment (Blouin et al., 2026), our study provides experimental 453

recruitment within that framework. One limitation of this study is that functional predictions 471 

were constrained by the genus level or higher taxonomic resolution of the sequencing data. 472 

Microbial function can vary even at the species or strain level and predictive functional profiling 473 

cannot resolve strain level variations or identify functions if the pathway annotations are poor 474 

(Langille et al., 2013). While this approach is useful for hypothesis generation, metagenomic or 475 

metatranscriptomic studies are needed to validate predicted functions. 476

Conclusions 478 

By explicitly assessing environment, soil, and plant genetic factors together, our study presents a 479 

hierarchical model of rhizosphere assembly in barley. Broad-scale environmental factors emerge 480 

as the top-tier drivers, defining the baseline community composition and diversity. Within this 481 

environmental context, the rhizosphere community is further shaped by local soil chemical 482 

properties and the existing soil microbial community. Finally, plant genotype introduces a fine-483 

scale filter by altering the relative abundance of select microbial taxa without drastically 484 

changing the overall community structure. These insights confirm our initial hypotheses and 485 

refine our understanding of how adaptation to local conditions is reflected in the crop 486 

microbiome. Barley largely recruits a broadly similar microbiota across adapted environments, 487 

whereas in a climatically extreme, poorly adapted site, environmental filtering dominates to yield 488 

a novel community. Meanwhile, plant genetic diversity in barley still has consequential effects 489 

on specific microbes, which may have functional relevance. 490 

The predominantly stochastic nature of community assembly we observed demonstrates 491 

that microbiome outcomes in field conditions can be variable, posing challenges for harnessing 492 

these interactions reliably. However, the detection of genotype-associated microbial differences 493 

within each environment suggests that targeted breeding or management to favor certain 494 

beneficial taxa must be pursued with recognition of strong environmental context. The primary 495 

constraint to achieving these goals is a lack of a mechanistic understanding of specific plant-496 

microbe interactions and future research is needed to build on these findings by integrating 497 

barley genomics and functional microbial traits to identify mechanisms underlying these 498 

interactions. 499

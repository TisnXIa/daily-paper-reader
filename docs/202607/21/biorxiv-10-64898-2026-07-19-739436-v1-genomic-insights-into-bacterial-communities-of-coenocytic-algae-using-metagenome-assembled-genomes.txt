Abstract 12 Coenocytic algae are organisms that undergo karyokinesis without cytokinesis, resulting in 13 multinucleated cells. Most research on bacterial communities in coenocytic algae has used 16S 14 rRNA sequencing, primarily focusing on the order Bryopsidales of green coenocytic algae. 15 Recent studies have analyzed metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from algal hosts across 16 multiple taxa, such as Chlorophyta, Phaeophyta, and Rhodophyta, revealing more about bacterial 17 biosynthetic machinery and potential symbiotic relationships. Given the cosmopolitan 18 distribution of coenocytic algae, such as Bryopsis , Caulerpa , Codium , and the yellow-green alga 19 

Vaucheria , and their unique morphology, there is a need to better understand their associated 20 bacterial communities. To address this, filaments of Vaucheria bursata LB2067 were sequenced 21 using the Illumina NovaSeq instrument, and all publicly available short- and long-read datasets 22 from coenocytic algae were screened for MAG recovery. All recovered MAGs from both 23 Bryopsidales and Vaucheriales showed a high dominance of Pseudomonadota at the phylum 24 level, but no consistent patterns at the order or family levels. High completeness of specific 25 KEGG pathways, such as bidirectional polyphosphate metabolism and riboflavin biosynthesis, 26 was prevalent in MAGs from coenocytic orders compared to non-coenocytic ones. Notably, N-27 acetylglutaminylglutamine amide (NAGGN) biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were found only 28 in MAGs from coenocytic orders, whereas polysaccharide utilization loci (PULs) were present in 29 all MAGs analyzed. These results indicate that bacterial communities associated with coenocytic 30 algae are complex, with diverse survival strategies adapted to challenging and variable 31 environments. 32

A microbiome is a microbial community that 36 occupies a well-defined habitat or host and performs distinct physicochemical functions (Vila 37 Duplá 2025). This is significant in 40 algae, given their symbiotic relationship with the bacterial community in the microbiota (Van 41 Der Loos et al., 2019). 42 The majority of research on the composition of algal bacterial communities has focused on 43 sequencing specific regions of the 16S rRNA gene (Singh & Reddy, 2014). This approach is 44 widely used because the 16S rRNA gene is highly conserved, omnipresent in bacteria, and 45 evolves slowly, making it ideal for taxonomic identification. However, if diversity is not present 46 in the 16S rRNA region, identification fails (Lamoureux et al., 2022). A key limitation is that 47 16S rRNA sequencing is only for taxonomic prediction and diversity analysis (Mathieu et al., 48 2023). It cannot detail metabolic interactions with algae and bacteria or reveal all interactions or 49 pathways. 50 In contrast to 16S rRNA sequencing, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of the algae host 51 enables the recovery of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). This approach has proven to 52 be a robust method for comprehending both the metabolic interactions and taxonomic 53 identification of algal microbiomes (Meziti et al., 2021; Mirete et al., 2025). Such metabolic 54 exchanges include algal carbon cycling, where heterotrophic bacteria, such as those in the 55 phylum Bacteroidota, possess numerous polysaccharide-degrading enzymes and polysaccharide 56 utilization loci (PULs) (Martens et al., 2011; Lu et al., 2023). Enzymes encoded in these gene 57 clusters enable the microbiome to degrade host polysaccharides such as alginate, fucoidan, agar, 58 and carrageenan (Gu et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2022; Grondin 59 et al., 2017). This degradation provides the bacterial community with essential building blocks 60 for the production of metabolites, such as vitamin B12 (Balabanova et al., 2021). In contrast to 61 PULs, Biosynthetic Gene Clusters (BGCs) are groups of genes that encode different enzymatic 62 machinery required to produce specific metabolites (Covington et al., 2021). BGCs are critical 63 for many organisms, particularly bacteria, as these secondary metabolites underpin essential 64 pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and ecological mediators (Blin et al., 2023, 2025). Identification 65 of BGCs and PULs enables an analysis of metabolic capabilities that cannot be determined from 66 16S rRNA alone. 67 In recent years, macroalgal bacterial communities have been recovered using metagenome-68 assembled genomes (MAGs) from diverse taxa, including Phaeophyceae, Rhodophyceae, and 69 Chlorophyceae, from the genera Nereocystis, Saccharina, Pyropia, and Ulva , respectively. 70 Metabolic reconstruction of these MAGs has revealed their core functional potential, particularly 71 in carbohydrate metabolism and the degradation of host structural polysaccharides, as indicated 72 by their high abundance (Lu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022; Weigel et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 73 2024). Additionally, these studies suggest that macroalgal-associated bacteria have the capacity 74 to produce essential bioactive compounds and secondary metabolites, via BGCs involved in the 75 synthesis of vitamins and phytohormones such as auxin, as well as enzymes like ACC deaminase 76 that may help regulate host stress responses (Lu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022; Weigel et al., 77 2022). 78 79 Bacterial communities associated with yellow-green algae (Xanthophyceae), a distinct algal 80 group characterized by xanthophyll pigments, remain poorly explored. Xanthophyceae exhibit an 81 exceptional range of morphologies, including unicellular forms, coenocytic filaments, simple 82 multicellular thalli, and complex multicellular thalli. Apart from this work, most studies of 89 bacterial communities associated with coenocytic algae have relied primarily on 16S rRNA 90 sequencing. 91 92 Coenocytic algae represent a unique morphological organization in which repeated nuclear 93 divisions (karyokinesis) occur without cytokinesis, resulting in a multinucleate organism 94 composed of a continuous cytoplasm lacking internal cross-walls. This body plan has evolved 95 independently in several algal lineages, including the green algal genera Caulerpa and Bryopsis 96 and the yellow-green algal genus Vaucheria , all of which have broad, cosmopolitan distributions. 97 Because coenocytic algae possess a continuous cellular architecture distinct from both 98 unicellular and multicellular forms, they provide an excellent model for investigating how algal 99 morphology influences the composition, spatial organization, and metabolic interactions of 100 associated bacterial communities. Analysis of bacterial communities of coenocytic algae has 101 been dominated by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing in Caulerpa , Bryopsis, and Vaucheria ,102 revealing bacterial communities composed of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Planctomycetes 103 (Aires et al., 2015, 2013; Delbridge et al., 2004; Kopprio et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2019; 104 Roth  Schulze et al., 2018; Devine et al., 2012; Ochiai et al., 2025; Van Duijnhoven et al., 105 2024). 108 To date, the bacterial community composition of Vaucheria has only been reported for V. litorea 109 using 16S rRNA sequencing (Devine et al., 2012). A deeper understanding of both the 110 taxonomic composition and metabolic interactions of bacterial communities associated with 111 

Vaucheria species could provide valuable insights into the relationship between algal 112 morphology and bacterial community composition. Vaucheria belongs to the class 113 Xanthophyceae and is taxonomically distinct from coenocytic green algae such as Bryopsis and 114 

Caulerpa (Chlorophyta). This study aims to investigate potential associations between bacterial 115 communities and coenocytic algal morphology through a comprehensive analysis of recovered 116 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), with a particular focus on identifying conserved 117 metabolic functions across the surfaces of these giant single-celled algae. To this end, vegetative 118 filaments of Vaucheria bursata LB2067 were sequenced using the Illumina sequencing platform. 119 In addition, recovered MAGs were compared with those obtained from 11 publicly available 120 whole-genome sequencing (WGS) datasets from various other coenocytic algae. 121

Culture conditions, DNA extraction, and sequencing 130 

V. bursata LB2067 cultures were obtained from the UTEX Culture Collection of Algae at UT-131 Austin (Austin, USA). The cultures were maintained under a 16:8 light/dark photocycle at 25 °C. 132 Isolation of genomic DNA from filaments was performed using the NucleoSpin Plant II kit 133 (Macherey-Nagel), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified genomic DNA was 134 used for library construction following a standard Illumina library preparation protocol. Briefly, 135 DNA was fragmented and subjected to end repair, adenylation of 3 ′ ends, adapter ligation, and 136 PCR amplification to generate indexed sequencing libraries. Library concentration and insert-137 size distribution were evaluated prior to sequencing. Indexed libraries were pooled and 138 sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument to produce 150-bp paired-end reads. 139 

Metagenome assembly and binning 140 Different strategies for Metagenome-Assembled Genome (MAG) recovery were performed 141 according to the data availability for each alga: short-read (SR), long-read (LR), or hybrid 142 (SR+LR). For datasets consisting solely of Illumina short reads (SR), the metaWRAP pipeline 143 (v1.3) was used (Uritskiy et al., 2018), specifically the Read_QC, Assembly (with MegaHit), 144 Binning, Bin_Refinement, and Reassembly_Bin modules. Bins with a total size exceeding 10 145 MB were removed to filter out potential eukaryotic contamination or assembly artifacts. All 146 software packgages were used with default parameters unless otherwise specified. Host DNA 147 was not removed prior to binning to support a separate, ongoing study. For datasets consisting 148 only of long-read (LR) data (PacBio or Nanopore), the following pipeline was used: Raw long 149 reads were assembled using Flye (v2.9.6) (Freire et al., 2022) with the --meta option. Depending 150 on the sequencing platform, the --pacbio-raw, --pacbio-hifi, or --nano-raw flag was applied. Raw 151 long reads were mapped to the resulting assemblies using minimap2 (v2.30) (Li 2018) with the 152 appropriate preset (map-pb, map-hifi, or map-ont). Coverage depth across contigs was calculated 153 using the jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths script from MetaBAT2 (v2.18) (Kang et al., 2019). 154 Both MaxBin2 (v2.2.7) (Wu et al., 2016) and MetaBAT2 were implemented for the binning 155 process. The final bin set was produced by de-replicating and consolidating bins from both 156 MaxBin2 and MetaBAT2 using DAS Tool (v1.1.7) (Sieber et al., 2018) (Figure S1). 157 158 In cases where datasets were available for both LR and SR (i.e., hybrid), the processes described 159 above were performed independently for each data type. To identify potentially identical bins 160 between the SR and LR bin sets, FastANI (v1.34) (Jain et al., 2018) was implemented with a 161 95% threshold, and the resulting pairs were processed in parallel. The raw reads were mapped 162 using minimap2 (v2.30) for LR and Bowtie2 (v2.5.4) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) for SR to 163 the bins identified as identical by FastANI. The corresponding mapping files were converted to 164 BAM format, and mapped FASTQ files were extracted using SAMtools (v1.22.1) (Li et al., 165 2009). Reassembly was performed using Unicycler (v0.5.1) (Wick et al., 2017), and the quality 166 of the reassembled bins was compared against the original bin pairs in terms of contamination, 167 completeness, genome size, N50, and contig count using CheckM2 (v1.1.0) (Chklovski et al. ,168 2023). The highest quality bins were selected for the final bin set (Figure S1). 169 170

MAG quality assessment, de-replication, and taxonomic classification 171 The quality of the final MAGs was determined using CheckM2 (v1.1.0) to estimate genome 172 completeness and contamination. Based on this predicted quality from CheckM2, MAGs were 173 classified as near complete, high quality (HQ), good quality (GQ) and low quality (LQ) 174 (Almeida et al., 2019). For HQ classification, MAGs with equal to or greater than 90% 175 completion and equal to or less than 5% contamination were considered. MAGs classified as GQ 176 had completion of equal or greater than 50% and equal or less than 5% contamination. In 177 addition, LQ classification was considered by MAGs with equal or less than 50% of completion 178 and equal or less than 10% contamination. MAGs with a completeness of at least more or equal 179 to 90% and contamination of at most less than 5%, with only 1 contig, were classified as near 180 complete. MAGs with a contamination level of 10% or higher were removed from the 181 subsequent analysis, regardless of completeness; only 1 MAG was removed. De-replication of 182 MAGs was performed to remove replicated genomes from the dataset by implementing Galah 183 (v0.4.2) (https://github.com/wwood/galah) for clustering. The CheckM2 output was then used to 184 manually select the best representative genome from each cluster based on contamination, 185 completeness, genome size, N50, and contig count. 186 187 Taxonomic classifications were conducted primarily using GTDB-Tk (2.6.1+galaxy0) (Genome 188 Taxonomy Database Toolkit) (Chaumeil et al., 2022) using the database version 224. Using 189 classifications derived from GTDB-Tk, taxonomic lineages were assigned. Barrnap (v0.9) 190 (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap) was used to identify all rRNA sequences present in 191 MAGs. QIIME 2 (v2025.10) (Bolyen et al., 2019) was used with the SILVA 138.2 database 192 (Quast et al., 2012) for taxonomic prediction of the extracted 16S sequences for validation of 193 genus and species of GTDB-tk results. For this, only SILVA similarity scores of equal to or 194 greater than 94.5% and 98.7% were used for genus and species, respectively. 195

Comparison with Non-Coenocytic Algae 223 MAGs from Ulva sp. and Saccharina sp. of the orders Ulavales and Laminariales were used for 224 comparative analysis of BGCs, PULs and KEGG pathways analysis, with the MAGs recovered 225 from coenocytic algae. These MAGs were obtained from reported microbiome analysis of co-226 located green, brown, and red algae (Lu et al., 2023). The MAGs from non-coenocytic algae 227 were subjected to the same methodology as the MAGs from coenocytic algae in BGCs, PULs, 228 and KEGG pathways. Statistical comparisons of pathway completeness between coenocytic and 229 non-coenocytic MAGs were conducted in R (v4.5.0). Only pathways detected in at least 50% of 230 MAGs in both groups were included in the analysis. For each pathway, a one-tailed Mann– 231 Whitney U test (alternative hypothesis: coenocytic > control) was performed, and p-values were 232 adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (adjusted p < 0.05). Effect 233 sizes were calculated as rank-biserial correlations ( r) and classified as negligible (| r| < 0.1), small 234 (0.1 ≤ |r| < 0.3), medium (0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5), or large (| r| ≥ 0.5). Data processing and visualization 235 were performed using the R packages dplyr (v1.2.0), tidyr (v1.3.2), ggplot2 (v4.0.2), and rstatix 236 (v0.7.3). 237

BGCs distribution from recovered MAGs of coenocytic order 315 A comparison of the distribution of identified biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) shows that 316 MAGs recovered from the Bryopsidales contained a substantially higher number of BGCs than 317 those from the Vaucheriales, Ulvales, and Laminariales (Figure 5A). Notably, MAGs associated 318 with Ulvales and Vaucheriales exhibited a high BGC count relative to the small number of algal 319 hosts from which they were recovered, one and two, respectively (Figure 5A). Regarding BGC 320 types, the majority were conserved between coenocytic and non-coenocytic algae, particularly 321 terpenes, terpene precursors, and T1PKS (Figure 5B & Table S6). The N-322 acetylglutaminylglutamine amide (NAGGN) BGC type was detected only in MAGs from 323 coenocytic algae and was absent from non-coenocytic hosts. Its distribution among the 324 coenocytic taxa was not uniform, as NAGGN was not detected in Avrainvillea , Caulerpa , and 325 

Codium species. The MAGs with NAGGN were assigned almost entirely to the Pseudomonadota 326 and were distributed across several bacterial orders rather than a single lineage. 327 328 The MAGs from the non-coenocytic algae, specifically Ulva sp., uniquely possessed T2PKS and 329 PUFA clusters. In contrast, the MAGs from coenocytic algae were characterized by the exclusive 330 presence of Butyrolactone and NAGGN clusters. Butyrolactone was specific to the MAGs from 331 Bryopsidales, species Bryopsis KO-2023 (Table S5). The NAGGN BGC’s were more widely 332 distributed among MAGs from coenocytic algae, with the highest prevalence in Ostreobium 333 (33%) and Derbesia (32%), followed by Bryopsis (16%), and Vaucheria and Penicillus (8%). 334 Despite the evolutionary distance between Vaucheriales and Bryopsidales, both shared the usage 335 of NAGGN clusters. However, the MAGs from Vaucheriales were distinct, exclusively 336 harboring clusters such as phosphonate-like, NRP-metallophore-NRPS-T1PKS, and various 337 complex hybrid PKS/NRPS combinations. 338

Taxonomy classification and quality 350 The majority of MAGs recovered from the WGS reads of the coenocytic algae were classified from 351 high to good quality with a low level of contamination and high completeness (Figure 1 G & Table 352 S2). This high yield of MAGs demonstrates the viability of recovering bacterial community from 353 host tissues, aligning with recent successes in both targeted coenocytic algal studies (Ochiai et al., 354 2025) and broad scale macroalgal metagenomic surveys (Lu et al., 2023). The predominance of 355 MAGs recovered from SR was classified as good quality and high quality, in contrast to LR, which 356 were classified predominantly as high quality and near complete (Figure S3). Both sequencing 357 methods yielded LQ MAGs; the LR assemblies showed less fragmentation, with fewer contigs and 358 higher N50 values than the SR assemblies (Figure S3). This result is consistent with Orellana et al., 359 (2023), who reported that short-read sequencing often recovers a higher total number of genomes due 360 to greater sequencing depth, but long-read approaches yield a significantly higher proportion of high-361 quality MAGs. They reported that LR MAGs were composed of approximately 10 times fewer 362 contigs and possessed N50 values more than 11 times higher than SR MAGs. Our results show that 363 the workflow utilized for MAGs recovery was able to recover HQ MAGs from both SR and LR of 364 WGS. 365 The distribution of taxa in both coenocytic orders, Bryopsidales and Vaucheriales, showed a direct 366 similarity at the phylum level, characterized by a consistently high percentage of Pseudomonadota 367 and Bacteroidota. In Bryopsidales, these two phyla constitute 72.9% of the recovered MAGs (44.7% 368 Pseudomonadota and 28.2% Bacteroidota), while in the Vaucheriales, it accounts for 84.4% of the 369 community (64.4% Pseudomonadota and 20.0% Bacteroidota) (Figure 2A). These findings align with 370 previous research on Bryopsidales in which the community of Caulerpa lentillifera was dominated 371 by Pseudomonadota (syn. Proteobacteria) at 52.1% and Bacteroidota (syn. Bacteroidetes) at 13.5% 372 (Liang et al., 2019). The dominance of Pseudomonadota observed in the V. bursata MAGs aligns 373 with previous 16S rRNA sequencing of V. litorea , which reported an 88.4% relative abundance of 374 this phylum. However, V. bursata exhibited a notably higher proportion of Bacteroidota (20.0%) 375 compared to the 7.3% observed in V. litorea (Devine et al., 2012). This could indicate a difference in 376 species recruitment, though this variance may also be influenced by the differences between WGS 377 binning and 16S rRNA sequencing. The co-dominance of Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota is not 378 unique to coenocytic algae but has been reported across diverse algal lineages, such as in the red alga 379 

Pyropia haitanensis , comprising approximately 65% of the sequenced microbiome (Wang et al., 380 2022). Furthermore, a major comparative across Ulva sp., Grateloupia sp ., Gelidium sp ., and 381 

Saccharina sp . found that core genera belonging to Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota dominated the 382

Metabolic KEGG pathways distribution 414 An evaluation of KEGG metabolic pathways across the MAGs recovered from coenocytic and non-415 coenocytic algae reveals that core bacterial metabolism remains highly conserved (Figure 3). 416 Specifically, pathways for glycine, cysteine, and lysine biosynthesis, as well as glycolysis and the 417 TCA Cycle, show high completeness across all MAGs (Figure 4). Among the pathways significantly 418 enriched in coenocytic-associated MAGs, the Sec-SRP protein secretion system showed the strongest 419 signal (Table S8), suggesting that bacteria associated with coenocytic algae may possess enhanced 420 secretory capacity, potentially reflecting a more intimate interaction with the host environment. 421 Conversely, bidirectional polyphosphate (polyP) metabolism and riboflavin biosynthesis were 422 significantly more complete in non-coenocytic controls (Mann-Whitney U, padj < 0.05), although 423 both pathways remained highly prevalent across coenocytic MAGs, with completeness values 424 reaching 90–100% in Vaucheriales-associated MAGs. BGC’s & PULs distribution 460 The BGCs identified in MAGs across the coenocytic and non-coenocytic orders (Bryopsidales, 461 Vaucheriales, Ulvales, and Laminariales) were dominated by terpenes, terpene precursors, and 462 T1PKS (Figure 5B). MAGs from Bryopsidales exhibited the highest number of BGCs, exceeding 463 1,500 and substantially more than any other order (Figure 5A), followed by Ulvales (~500) and 464 Vaucheriales (>200). For Vaucheriales MAGs, this abundance highlights a high density of BGC 465 types within the bacterial community, given that the dataset contains only 29 non-redundant MAGs. 466 MAGs from the analyzed algae of the Bryopsidales and Vaucheriales contain the NAGGN BGC 467 type, in contrast to MAGs from their non-coenocytic counterparts (Figure 5B). Its distribution among 468 the coenocytic taxa was not detected in Avrainvillea , Caulerpa , or Codium species. The MAGs 469 possessing NAGGN BGC were assigned to the Pseudomonadota, spanning several orders including 470 the Rhizobiales, Pseudomonadales, Caulobacterales, Nevskiales, Rhodospirillales, and 471 Acidimicrobiales. 488 A comprehensive analysis of recovered MAGs from SR and LR of coenocytic algae from the 489 Vaucheriales and Bryopsidales orders showed high dominance at the phylum level of 490 Pseudomonadota. Conversely, in order and family, there is no specific pattern in predicted taxonomy 491 by GTDB-tk. Moreover, this lack of pattern in taxonomic distribution could also be attributable to 492 environmental factors previously observed in the algal sequencing procedure. KEGG pathways in 493 MAGs from Bryopsidales and Vaucheriales showed high completeness in bidirectional 494 polyphosphate metabolism and riboflavin biosynthesis; however, these pathways were significantly 495 more complete in MAGs recovered from non-coenocytic algae (Figure 4A). Conversely, pathways 496 significantly enriched in coenocytic-associated MAGs included protein secretion (Sec-SRP), motility 497 (Flagellum, Chemotaxis), and nutrient provisioning pathways (Thiamin biosynthesis, MEP-DOXP, 498 phosphate transporters), suggesting that the coenocytic morphology could selectively favor bacteria 499 with enhanced host-interaction and biosynthetic capacities. In MAGs from Vaucheriales, their 500 specific pathway prevalence for PHB synthesis that is reported to be a survival mechanism for carbon 501 reserve in low nutrient environments. In addition, the high number of MAGs with 83% completeness 502 of PHB synthesis shows that the Vaucheria species could be a potential source for extracting bacteria 503 with this PHB capability, given the nuance of PHB in bioeconomic applications (Verlinden et al., 504 2007). The NAGGN BGCs cluster is specific and only present in MAGs from algae of the coenocytic 505 order. NAGGN was reported to be associated with a survival mechanism for osmotic pressure. The 506 high completeness of specific KEGG pathways and NAGGN BGCs within MAGs of the coenocytic 507 algae suggests a bacterial community equipped with various survival mechanisms adapted to diverse 508 and challenging environments. Given this observation of the adaptation of survival mechanisms, it 509 can be hypothesized that it may be due to the cosmopolitan distribution of the various coenocytic 510 algae from which MAGs were recovered. 511 This study represents an initial step toward elucidating the complexity of the microbiome associated 512 with coenocytic algae from the orders Bryopsidales and Vaucheriales, as well as exploring potential 513 associations with host coenocytic morphology. 514

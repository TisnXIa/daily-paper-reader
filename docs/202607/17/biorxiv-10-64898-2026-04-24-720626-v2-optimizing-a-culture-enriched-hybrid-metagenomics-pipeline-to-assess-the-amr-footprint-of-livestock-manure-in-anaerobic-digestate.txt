ABSTRACT 13 

The role of environmental samples from livestock production systems, including manure and 14 

anaerobic digestate, as reservoirs of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) is likely 15 

underestimated because conventional metagenomic approaches can overlook low-abundance 16 

ARGs and often lack the resolution needed to reliably associate these genes with their microbial 17 

hosts and linked mobile genetic elements (MGEs). Here, we evaluated whether culture-enriched 18 

metagenomics (CEMG), with and without antibiotic selection, enhances ARG detection in 19 

anaerobic digestate and improves the resolution of ARG-MGE-host associations using hybrid 20 

short- and long-read metagenomic assembly. Culture enrichment substantially increased ARG 21 

recovery, mean ARG abundance rose from 15.4 counts per million (CPM) in culture-independent 22 

direct metagenomes from fresh digestate (FD) to 124 CPM in CEMG without antibiotics and 23 

160.0 CPM in antibiotic-selective CEMG, corresponding to an approximately 10.4-fold increase 24 

over FD. In FD, only 9 unique ARGs were detected, whereas enrichment recovered 112, 25 

including ARGs of clinical importance such as glycopeptide resistance, β-lactamase genes of the 26 

CTX-M, OXA, and TEM families, and the cfr 23S rRNA methyltransferase conferring cross-27 

resistance to multiple antibiotic classes. Oxygen availability was the strongest factor structuring 28 

enriched community compositions and ARG profiles, with aerobic and anaerobic communities 29 

forming distinct clusters. Antibiotic selection induced targeted, class-specific shifts in ARG 30 

profiles, with ARGs associated with tetracycline resistance consistently enriched across 31 

treatments. Hybrid metagenomic assembly resolved the genomic context of 784 ARGs, of which 32 

59.3% were co-localized with at least one class of mobile genetic element (MGE), 33 

predominantly plasmids, insertion sequences, and integrative and conjugative/mobilizable 34 

elements (ICEs/IMEs). Biocide and metal resistance genes frequently co-occurred with ARGs on 35 

the same contigs, highlighting the potential for co-selection. Together, these findings demonstrate 36 

that antibiotic-selective culture enrichment enhances resistome surveillance by improving 37 

detection of low-abundance ARGs, while hybrid assembly provides critical genomic context for 38 

assessing their mobility and host associations. 39

INTRODUCTION 50 

Antimicrobials are increasingly recognized as emerging environmental contaminants in diverse 51 

ecosystems, including soil and aquatic environments, because of their role in driving the 52 

emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) (1). A substantial proportion of 53 

these contaminants originates from human and veterinary antimicrobial use (2). On average, over 54 

100 mg of antimicrobials were used globally to produce each kilogram of animal biomass in 55 

2016 (3). Notably, some of the antimicrobial classes used in animals, including macrolides, 56 

fluoroquinolones, and cephalosporins, are designated as critically important for treating 57 

infectious diseases in humans (4). Antimicrobial use in food-producing animals selects for 58 

resistant bacteria and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) within the gastrointestinal tract. 59 

Because many antimicrobials are only partially metabolized or absorbed in the gut, a substantial 60 

proportion is excreted in urine and feces alongside resistant bacteria and ARGs. Consequently, 61 

manure serves as a reservoir of resistant bacteria, ARGs, and antimicrobial residues that can 62 

further select and co-select for AMR in environmental microbial communities (5). Digestate, the 63 

byproduct of anaerobic digestion of manure, is widely used as a fertilizer in integrated livestock 64 

and cropping systems. Although anaerobic digestion reduces pathogen abundance and the overall 65 

load of antimicrobial resistance genes, the resulting digestate may still retain residual resistance 66 

genes and associated elements that can be disseminated into the environment following land 67 

application (5, 6). Characterizing the resistome of these reservoirs requires sensitive, high 68 

throughput approaches capable of detecting diverse resistance determinants. Direct culture-69 

independent metagenomics, which enables sequencing of environmental DNA without the need 70 

for cultivation (7), has become a widely adopted strategy, providing broader and more 71 

comprehensive ARG profiling in complex environmental samples than targeted approaches such 72

Despite this practical advantage, direct metagenomics has important limitations for resistome 76 

surveillance. Its effectiveness is constrained by the sequencing platform, community complexity, 77 

and reference-database quality, and it frequently fails to detect underrepresented or low-78 

abundance microbial taxa (13, 14), yielding incomplete representations of ARGs and MGEs in 79 

complex environments such as wastewater, manure, and digestate (15, 16). This blind spot is 80 

consequential because low-abundance microbial taxa can act as reservoirs of clinically relevant 81 

ARGs (17), which can be mobilized and transferred to abundant or potentially pathogenic 82 

community members through horizontal gene transfer (18). Moreover, these ARG-carrying taxa 83 

may expand when conditions change, for example, following land application of manure or 84 

digestate. Methods that systematically under-detect rare populations therefore risk 85 

underestimating both the diversity and the dissemination potential of the resistome. Recovering 86 

this rare fraction by direct metagenomics typically demands ultra-high sequencing depth at 87 

substantial cost (19). Hybridization-based bait capture can enrich low-abundance ARGs, but it 88 

introduces selection biases and depends on careful probe design and accurate on- and off-target 89 

discrimination. In addition, when combined with short-read sequencing, it still limits recovery of 90 

the genomic context needed to resolve ARG-MGE associations (20). 91 

Selective culture enrichment integrated with direct metagenomics provides a complementary 92 

strategy that extends the scope of resistome characterization. Rather than replacing direct 93 

metagenomics, this approach targets the cultivable fraction of the resistome, enhancing the 94 

detection and recovery of rare, resistant populations that may be underrepresented or overlooked 95 

in culture-independent analyses. This strategy has enhanced resistome characterization across 96 

environments ranging from the human gut (17) and cystic fibrosis sputum (9) to drinking water 97 

(21), wastewater (19) and ocean sediments (22). By favoring the growth of targeted groups such 98 

as antimicrobial-resistant bacteria (19) and capturing previously uncultured or poorly 99 

characterized microbes (22, 23), enrichment increases the sensitivity of downstream analyses 100 

(14), a particular advantage in AMR research, where ARGs constitute less than 1% of bacterial 101 

DNA (10) and only about 0.05% of metagenomic reads (24). 102

Improved ARG detection alone, however, does not resolve the critical question of which 103 

organisms carry these determinants. Illumina short-read sequencing has limited ability to link 104 

ARGs to their microbial hosts and can be affected by PCR amplification bias (25). Moreover, 105 

short-read assembly into contigs can further compromise ARG recovery, as assemblies often 106 

fragment across repetitive and conserved regions flanking ARGs (18) and fail to resolve the 107 

complex, mosaic architectures of MGEs (26, 27), the very genetic structures that determine the 108 

potential for ARG mobility and dissemination (18). Third-generation long-read platforms, such 109 

as Oxford Nanopore and PacBio, generate reads spanning tens of thousands of bases that can 110 

cover full-length ARGs and their flanking regions, aiding host inference and MGE resolution 111 

(25). Although their per-base accuracy has improved substantially with recent chemistries, 112 

combining long-read and short-read data remains advantageous, as the high base-level accuracy 113 

and depth of short reads complement the contiguity of long reads (26). Integrating this hybrid 114 

sequencing strategy with culture enrichment can further enhance host identification and resolve 115 

the genomic context of ARGs, particularly those embedded within or associated with MGEs. 116 

Digestate is an anoxic environment dominated by obligate and facultative anaerobes (28, 29), so 117 

recovering its cultivable resistome requires anaerobic cultivation, while parallel aerobic 118 

enrichment makes it possible to assess how oxygen availability shapes which resistance 119 

determinants are recovered. Building on this rationale, our study had two objectives: (i) to 120 

compare direct culture-independent metagenomics and culture-enriched metagenomics (CEMG) 121 

for their ability to characterize the resistome and microbial community structure of anaerobic 122 

digestate, while optimizing enrichment conditions across oxygen regimes, antimicrobial classes, 123 

and concentrations to enhance ARG recovery; and (ii) to integrate hybrid short- and long-read 124 

assembly into this workflow to resolve the genomic context of ARGs, including their 125 

associations with MGEs and bacterial hosts. 126

enriched communities (CEMG-NoAB and CEMG-AB) diverged markedly from FD 143

While reference-based analysis captured broad shifts in resistome composition following culture 283 

enrichment, it provided limited insight into microbial host associations and genomic context of 284 

ARGs, particularly linkages to MGEs. Hybrid assembly of short- and long-read data helped 285 

resolve these associations. Overall, hybrid assembly identified a total of 784 ARGs across 651 286 

unique contigs, representing 100 unique ARGs. Of these, 59.3% (465/784) were co-localized 287 

with at least one MGE class across 353 ARG-MGE bearing contigs (Table S7). Plasmids were 288 

the most prevalent MGEs, identified on 230 contigs (65.2%), followed by insertion sequences 289 

(116 contigs, 32.9%), ICEs and IMEs (77 contigs, 21.8%), integrase/resolvase signatures (59 290 

contigs, 16.7%), and phage or viral sequences (15 contigs, 4.2%) (Fig. 5A). Co-occurrence of 291 

multiple MGE classes on single contigs was frequently observed. Seventy contigs carried both 292 

insertion sequence (IS) elements and plasmid signatures, while 20 contigs exhibited triple co-293 

occurrence of IS elements, integrase/resolvase genes, and plasmid classifications. These 294 

composite genetic architectures suggest mosaic MGE structures and indicate layered potential 295 

for horizontal transfer of ARGs within these genomic contexts (Fig. 5B). Plasmids accounted for 296 

the highest total ARG-MGE associations, spanning 65 unique ARGs across all major drug 297 

classes, while insertion sequences flanked 29 unique ARGs within 2 kb, with aminoglycoside, 298 

phenicol, lincosamide, and β-lactam resistance genes most frequently represented. ICEs and 299 

IMEs were associated with 20 unique ARGs, with tetracycline resistance genes ( tet (M), tet (W), 300

Discussion 351 

Our results suggest that culture-enriched metagenomics not only improves the detectability of 352 

ARGs but also actively reshapes the observed resistome by selectively amplifying specific 353 

microbial populations, consistent with previous reports (9, 30). Unenriched digestate samples 354 

showed a comparatively homogeneous community structure, substantial representation of poorly 355 

resolved taxa affiliated with phyla characteristic of stable anaerobic digestion communities (28, 356 

29). By contrast, enrichment reduced the dominance of this baseline community and favored 357 

bacterial groups better adapted to laboratory cultivation. This pattern reinforces an important 358 

conceptual distinction: direct metagenomics is better suited to describing the in-situ community 359 

within the digester (31), whereas culture enrichment preferentially captures the cultivable 360 

fraction that may be more relevant to post-digestion survival, dissemination, and hazard 361 

assessment. 362 

The enrichment-driven expansion of species-level diversity, particularly the recovery of many 363 

species uniquely detected in antibiotic-supplemented cultures, is especially noteworthy from a 364 

surveillance perspective. Previous studies have also reported increased detection of clinically 365 

relevant ARGs and taxa following selective enrichment (19). The appearance of taxa such as E. 366 

dispar, E. casseliflavus, E. hormaechei, C. amalonaticus, and A. insolitus following antibiotic 367 

enrichment, organisms with recognized clinical and environmental relevance (32–35), suggests 368 

that these populations persist in digestate at low abundance or in a suppressed state, becoming 369 

detectable only under selective antibiotic cultivation. From a One Health perspective, this 370

supports the idea that direct shotgun sequencing alone can underestimate the diversity of viable, 371 

resistant, and potentially transmissible bacteria present in environmental samples like digestate. 372 

This enrichment-driven shift was also reflected in a 10-fold increase in overall ARG abundance, 373 

consistent with observations from wastewater systems (19). Importantly, this shift was not 374 

limited to a greater ARG burden but also involved a broadening of the ARG drug-class profile. 375 

Fresh digestate was dominated by ARGs associated with MLS resistance, whereas enriched 376 

communities displayed a broader profile across tetracycline, β-lactam, aminoglycoside, phenicol, 377 

rifamycin, glycopeptide, and other resistance classes. Although a broad range of ARG classes 378 

have been reported in manure and digestate (31, 36), the strong dominance of MLS resistance in 379 

our unenriched digestate likely obscured less abundant resistance determinants that became more 380 

detectable following culture enrichment. Notably, several clinically important ARG families 381 

were recovered exclusively in enriched samples, including genes associated with glycopeptide 382 

resistance, β-lactamase genes of the CTX-M, OXA, and TEM families, some variants of which 383 

are known to confer extended-spectrum β-lactamase activity, though variant-level confirmation 384 

was not performed in the current study. The cfr 23S rRNA methyltransferase is particularly 385 

notable given its cross-class activity spanning phenicols, lincosamides, oxazolidinones, 386 

pleuromutilins, and streptogramin, meaning a single gene simultaneously undermines multiple 387 

antibiotic classes used in both human and veterinary medicine. These findings align with a 388 

growing body of evidence that the cultivable, low-abundance fraction of environmental 389 

resistomes carries disproportionate clinical relevance relative to its abundance (37–39). 390 

The strong representation of tetracycline resistance following multiple enrichment treatments is 391 

also of importance. Tetracycline resistance genes are widely distributed in livestock-associated 392 

bacteria and are often associated with MGEs, which may facilitate persistence even in the 393 

absence of tetracycline (39–41). The fact that tetracycline resistance increased across several 394 

antibiotic enrichments, rather than just with tetracycline, likely reflects co-selection and co-395 

maintenance of multidrug resistance determinants in the same bacterial population or linked 396 

mobile elements (42). A similar interpretation may explain the increases in cephalosporin, 397 

aminoglycoside, phenicol, glycopeptide, and diaminopyrimidine resistance observed under non-398 

cognate antibiotic treatments. 399

The generally weak effect of increasing antibiotic concentration on community diversity and 400 

ARG richness suggests that, once resistant populations are selected, further concentration 401 

increases do not broaden recovery and may instead narrow the cultured community. This 402 

supports the use of the size-adjusted lowest MIC for broad AMR surveillance, as higher 403 

concentrations may reduce the ecological representativeness of enriched communities. 404 

The predominance of plasmid- and ICE/IME-associated ARGs across all major drug classes has 405 

direct implications for post-digestion dissemination risk. MGEs are the primary vehicle for 406 

cross-species ARG transfer, so, even if anaerobic digestion reduces the abundance of resistant 407 

organisms, the persistence of ARGs on conjugative and replicative elements sustains horizontal 408 

transfer potential upon land application of digestate - independent of the organisms that 409 

originally carried them (43). The co-localization of ARGs with biocide and metal resistance 410 

genes on the same contigs further supports co-selection as a plausible mechanism maintaining 411 

AMR in post-digestion environments. The high relative abundance of zinc and arsenic resistance 412 

genes is consistent with the widespread use of zinc-based compounds as feed additives in food 413 

animal production (44), and with previous reports of zinc co-selecting for antibiotic resistance 414 

through shared genetic linkage on MGEs (45). Because antibiotic and heavy metal resistance 415 

determinants are frequently co-located on the same mobile element, metal selection pressure in 416 

manure-amended soils may indirectly sustain antibiotic resistance gene pools long after 417 

antimicrobial use ceases. 418 

A key strength of this study is the ability to link ARGs and their MGE contexts to putative 419 

bacterial hosts at the contig level, a resolution that read-based approaches cannot provide. 420 

Particularly significant is the broad host distribution of tetracycline resistance ARGs across 421 

phylogenetically diverse genera - spanning both Gram-positive commensals such as 422 

Streptococcus and Vagococcus and Gram-negative opportunists such as Proteus , Citrobacter , and 423 

Escherichia - which, combined with frequent ICE/IME co-localization, suggests that conjugative 424 

dissemination of tetracycline resistance is not confined to a single host lineage but is distributed 425 

across the enriched community. This observation reinforces the broader point that culture 426 

enrichment can surface clinically relevant ARG-MGE-host combinations that remain invisible in 427 

bulk metagenomic profiles, and that the cultivable fraction recovered under selective conditions 428 

may disproportionately represent the transmissible, high-risk segment of the environmental 429 

resistome. 430

The distribution of ARG-MGE-host combinations also differed markedly between aerobic and 431 

anaerobic enrichments, indicating that oxygen availability is a primary determinant of which 432 

resistance determinants are recovered. Previous studies have also shown that oxygen exposure 433 

can restructure microbial communities and alter ARG profiles (46). This suggests that, in 434 

digestate-derived enrichment systems, redox conditions may act as a first-order ecological 435 

determinant that governs which bacterial groups proliferate and, consequently, which resistance 436 

mechanisms are detectable. Aerobic enrichment favored communities dominated by Alcaligenes 437 

and related taxa, whereas anaerobic enrichment selected for Proteus , Streptococcus , Vagococcus ,438 

and Fermentimonas . These shifts likely reflect major physiological differences in respiratory 439 

capacity, substrate use, and competitive fitness under oxic versus anoxic conditions (47), with 440 

corresponding changes in the resistome driven by shifts in host community structure. 441 

Incorporating both aerobic and anaerobic enrichment conditions may therefore improve AMR 442 

surveillance, as some ARGs and their associated hosts may remain at low abundance under 443 

anaerobic conditions but become more detectable under aerobic conditions, enabling 444 

identification of clinically relevant taxa that may proliferate following land application of 445 

manure or digestate. 446 

While these findings provide important insights into the structure and mobilization potential of 447 

the resistome, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, not 448 

all microorganisms present in digestate can grow under the culture conditions used in this study 449 

(48–50). Consequently, taxa that are unculturable under these conditions would remain 450 

undetected regardless of their abundance in the source material. Secondly, the use of a single 451 

culture medium likely constrained the diversity of organisms recovered, whereas employing a 452 

broader range of selective and non-selective media could capture a wider spectrum of microbial 453 

diversity (49). Thirdly, the observation that a substantial fraction of ARG instances lacked 454 

detectable MGE associations raises an important interpretive caution: the absence of detectable 455 

linkage in assembly-based analyses does not confirm chromosomal localization, as contig 456 

fragmentation and database gaps can obscure genuine mobile contexts. This is particularly 457 

relevant for glycopeptide resistance ARGs, which were absent from MGE-associated contigs 458 

despite being detected at the read level, leaving their genomic context unresolved (51). Finally, 459 

although the present workflow identifies ARG-MGE-host linkages and co-occurrence patterns 460

Conclusions 463 

Overall, this study demonstrates that culture-enriched metagenomics can substantially enhance 464 

AMR surveillance resolution in livestock digestate. By selectively amplifying viable microbial 465 

populations, CEMG enabled the recovery of low-abundance ARG-carrying taxa, expanded the 466 

detectable diversity of ARGs, and facilitated the identification of ARG-MGE-host co-localization 467 

patterns and ARG-metal co-selection signals that were largely obscured in direct metagenomic 468 

profiles. These findings indicate that assessments based solely on bulk community metagenomes 469 

may underestimate the diversity and mobilization potential of ARGs present in digestate. 470 

Integrating enrichment-based approaches with direct metagenomic analyses may therefore 471 

provide a more comprehensive framework for evaluating the environmental dissemination risk of 472 

AMR associated with livestock-derived anaerobic digestate. 473

Sample collection 475 

Digestate was obtained directly from a continuously stirred (120 rpm), mesophilic (35°C) 476 

anaerobic digester (CSTR; New Brunswick Scientific BioFlo 110). The digester was operated in 477 

our laboratory for more than six years as a 14 L fed-batch system, with a working volume of 8 L 478 

and a solids retention time of 30 days. During the study period, the digester was in a stable 479

a pH of 7.43. The reactor was fed with manure collected from a family-owned commercial dairy 481

approximately three times per week (e.g., Monday, Wednesday, and Friday), with 0.622 L of 483 

digestate removed and replaced with an equal volume of fresh manure, maintaining an 8 L 484 

working volume and a 30-day hydraulic retention time. 485

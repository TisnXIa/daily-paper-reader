ABSTRACT 14 

Background: Urinary tract infections (UTIs) rank among the most common infections globally, 15 with many linked to indwelling urinary catheters. Our prior culture-based longitudinal evaluation 16 of long-term catheterized nursing home residents revealed persistent asymptomatic colonization 17 by pathogens and demonstrated that CAUTI onset was not necessarily due to new pathogen 18 acquisition. In this study, we optimized metagenomics methods to examine the ecological 19 structure underlying persistent colonization and the transition to infection. 20 

Results: We present a comprehensive longitudinal metagenomic analysis of catheterized urine 21 specimens, revealing colonization dynamics of 69 microbial species across 198 samples from 9 22 individuals. Descriptive ecological metrics were combined with Bayesian mixed-effects models 23 that accounted for repeated within-participant sampling to identify clusters of co-occurring 24 species, determine the impact of perturbations such as antibiotic exposure and catheter 25 changes on community structure, and identify taxa predictive of infection sign and symptom 26 onset. Longitudinal specimens clustered into three main ecological phenotypes: 1) moderate 27 diversity, unstable communities (3 participants); 2) high diversity, stable communities that 28 resisted disruption even after multiple catheter changes (3 participants); and 3) low diversity, 29 pathogen-dominated communities (3 participants). Catheter changes alone did not significantly 30 disrupt community composition, while antibiotic exposures induced major shifts often followed 31 by re-colonization with the same genera within subsequent weeks. Six clusters of species were 32 identified for which relative abundances correlated across perturbations to the microbial 33 community, including a mutually exclusive Enterobacterales cluster and fastidious-anaerobe 34 group cluster. 24 species were found to correlate with onset of signs and symptoms of infection, 35 11 of which were missed by standard urine culture. 36 

Conclusions. The catheterized urinary tract represents a novel ecosystem that is resilient to 37 disruption by catheter changes but susceptible to antibiotic perturbation. Antibiotic exposure did 38 deplete all species associated with signs and symptoms but also depleted potentially benign 39

INTRODUCTION 49 Indwelling urinary catheters are ubiquitous in modern healthcare and long-term care 50 settings and are a major driver of urinary tract infections (UTI) [1-3]. Up to 10% of residents in 51 long-term care settings such as nursing homes require permanent catheter use, and these 52 patients typically exhibit continuous bacteriuria with a high risk of infection and severe sequelae 53 [1, 4]. The presence of an indwelling catheter dramatically alters the bladder environment to 54 facilitate bacteriuria, making it the leading cause of healthcare-associated UTIs [1, 4]. However, 55 distinguishing asymptomatic bacteriuria from catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) can be clinically 56 challenging due to a high prevalence of subjective, nonspecific signs and symptoms of infection 57 [3, 5]. The burden of CAUTI is estimated to be ~449,334 cases annually in the US alone, 58 leading to prolonged hospitalization, increased morbidity, and healthcare costs exceeding $350 59 million per year [6, 7]. Urinalysis also provides little diagnostic utility for differentiating CAUTI 60 from bacteriuria due to the inflammation and proteinuria induced by the catheter presence [5]. 61 Together, these problems lead to both insufficient and inappropriate antibiotic use, which 62 highlights the need for improved diagnostic criteria in the long-term catheterized population. 63 Clinical management of UTIs has historically followed the view that healthy urine is 64 sterile or at least lacking in traditional uropathogens. However, this paradigm has been 65 increasingly challenged with substantial evidence demonstrating the presence of a urinary 66 microbiome [5, 8-10]. Indwelling urinary catheters further create a unique ecological niche that 67 fundamentally alters the urinary tract environment and favors bacteriuria [11]. The catheter 68 surface provides a substrate for biofilm formation, while catheter-associated trauma to the 69 urothelium may disrupt host defense mechanisms. In catheterized individuals, we and others 70 have demonstrated that culturable microorganisms persist as stable, polymicrobial communities 71 resilient to interventions like antibiotics or catheter changes [5, 10]. However, as standard 72 clinical culture methods under-sample fastidious and anaerobic organisms, these data provide 73

Furthermore, 74 culture based longitudinal assessments of catheterized urine have revealed that microbes 75 identified during symptomatic CAUTI were often present asymptomatically for several weeks 76 prior to diagnosis, demonstrating that symptom onset is not necessarily driven by the acquisition 77 of a new pathogen but may arise from within a resident microbial reservoir [5, 10]. 78 The clinical significance of polymicrobial communities within the catheterized bladder 79 and their relationship to development of symptomatic disease from asymptomatic colonization 80 remains poorly defined. Longitudinal profiling of catheterized individuals may therefore provide 81 key insights into microbial community dynamics that cross-sectional studies cannot fully 82 capture. Potential mechanisms influencing symptom onset may include the acquisition of new 83 virulent species, the loss of protective commensals, or an increase in the pathogenic potential of 84 resident strains through within-host evolution [5, 12-16]. There is growing evidence for the latter, 85 with studies documenting progressive increases in antibiotic resistance in persistent isolates 86 and the accumulation of resistance genes via horizontal gene transfer within clinical settings 87 [13, 15, 17]. However, most large-scale studies remain culture-based and low-resolution, 88 leaving it unclear whether symptomatic transitions are driven by species-level turnover or by 89 strain-level changes in virulence and resistance. 90 No prior study has aligned longitudinal whole-genome sequencing of samples from 91 catheterized individuals with infection signs and symptoms. This approach can potentially 92 determine whether symptomatic infection aligns with new species acquisitions, a loss of 93 beneficial microbes, or genomic changes within persistent strains. We therefore conducted a 94 longitudinal metagenomic study designed to evaluate and overcome technical challenges of 95 sequencing complex catheter urine specimens and to characterize all microbes present in the 96 urine. The sample was spun at 8000g 144 for 10 minutes, the pellet was washed once in 1X PBS and then resuspended in 400 μL TE 145 buffer. The Benzonase reaction was quenched by adding 88 μL of 0.5M EDTA and 880 μL of 146 1.5M NaCl and sample could then proceed with nucleic acid extraction kit. 147

Bioinformatic analysis 222 Reads were demultiplexed with Illumina’s bcl2fastq software v2.17.1. Optimization of DNA extraction methods. 308 To establish a reproducible protocol for metagenomic sequencing of complex catheter 309 urine specimens, we first evaluated DNA extraction and host depletion methods using an 310 engineered mock urine community. This community contained defined quantities of Gram-311 positive and Gram-negative bacteria, fungi ( Candida spp.), a parasite ( Trichomonas vaginalis ), 312 and human cellular material to mimic the composition of clinical catheter urine samples ( Table 313 

1). 314 Five different DNA extraction methods were compared to identify the best extraction kit 315 and the utility of depleting host DNA prior to extraction, as described in detail in the methods. 316 Hypotonic lysis-based methods inconsistently depleted host DNA and introduced taxonomic 317 bias, notably over-representing Staphylococcus (Fig. 1, Table S3 ). The QIAamp kit skewed 318 community representation toward Staphylococcus , while the MagMAX kit over-319 represented Pseudomonas and performed poorly for fungal detection. Neither of these methods 320 performed well to detect the parasite ( Fig. 1, Table S3 ). 321 In contrast, the Zymo HostZERO kit yielded the highest DNA concentrations, achieved 322 optimal depletion of human DNA, and consistently recapitulated the known microbial 323 composition of the mock community across replicates, despite very limited representation of the 324 parasite ( Fig. 1 ). Hence, the Zymo HostZERO method was selected for extracted DNA from the 325 complete longitudinal series of urine specimens from 9 study participants. Water-only and mock 326 community controls were also extracted and processed in parallel, confirming the absence of 327 significant contamination and the continued robustness of the extraction and sequencing 328 pipeline. 329

Catheterized urine samples are largely dominated by uropathogens 330 Across 198 urine samples from 9 participants with long-term catheters, 51.6% of taxa 331 could be categorized as uropathogens, followed by 30.7% as commensal species, and 16.2% 332 as species primarily found in the oral cavity (Fig. S1a ). When examining community 333 composition dynamics across all isolates, 7/9 participants (78%) had uropathogen dominated 334 profiles (Participants 101, 106, 201, 203, 204, 206, and 207) while 2/9 had commensal-335 dominated profiles (Participants 102 and 104) (Fig. S1b ). Oral species were detected in 336 samples from 6/9 participants, while participants 101, 106, and 203 had no detectable oral 337 species with follow up. faecalis , until the next course cleared them and P. aeruginosa reclaimed dominance.
